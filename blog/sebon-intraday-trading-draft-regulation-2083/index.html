0.69% a round trip before you profit: SEBON’s intraday draft brings same-day trading to NEPSE

SEBON published the preliminary draft of the Intraday Securities Trading Regulation, 2083 on November 5 — same-day square-off, auto square-off, margin and exposure limits, feedback open until Mangsir 4, 2083. It is a draft, not a launch. The question is whether it raises liquidity or risk — and the honest answer is both, split by who trades it.

By Nepse Decode Research Desk · Kathmandu · November 6, 2026 · 22 min read
Facebook X LinkedIn WhatsApp Viber

On November 5, 2026, the Securities Board of Nepal published the preliminary draft of the Intraday Securities Trading Regulation, 2083, and asked the market for feedback. Six weeks after the concept paper that first raised the idea, the proposal has a shape: buy and sell the same share on the same day, square the position off before the close, hold margin against it, and do it only in shares SEBON allows, only through brokers SEBON approves. It is a draft for consultation, not a rule in force — nothing about your trading changes today. But the draft is the moment the debate stops being theoretical, and the question it forces is the one this article answers: will intraday trading raise liquidity in NEPSE, or risk? The honest answer is both. The liquidity it adds is real but mostly velocity, not depth. The risk it adds is concentrated, measurable, and falls hardest on the traders least equipped to carry it. And the safeguards in the draft are real — but the numbers that would make them credible are not in it yet.

Signal summary

  • The document: a preliminary draft of the Intraday Securities Trading Regulation, 2083, published November 5, 2026 for consultation. Feedback is open until Mangsir 4, 2083 (November 20, 2026). It is not in force.
  • The mechanism: open positions must be squared off the same day; if not closed in the prescribed time, they are closed automatically. Each client must hold the prescribed margin, with variation margin possible; exposure limits are set per client, per broker, per security and for the whole market. NEPSE and broker-dealers may offer the facility only with SEBON approval, and only listed securities meeting the criteria are eligible.
  • What is missing: no margin percentage, no leverage multiple, no eligible-share list, no launch date. SEBON may implement intraday trading in phases.
  • The cost floor (computed illustration): a Rs 100,000 round trip costs Rs 690 in broker commission (0.33% a side) and SEBON fees (0.015% a side) — 0.69% before the trade makes a rupee. A 1% price move leaves Rs 310 of Rs 1,000; a 0.5% move leaves a Rs 190 loss.
  • The context: NEPSE turned over Rs 4.72 billion on November 5 — a market with real flow, not a market starved of trading. The question is not whether intraday trading adds turnover; it will. It is whether it adds depth.
  • The verdict: liquidity up on turnover, depth unproven, risk up for undisciplined retail traders, system risk contained if the phased rollout holds. The feedback window is the reader’s lever — until November 20.

01. What the draft actually proposes

SEBON’s notice states the purpose plainly: to make the speed of trading, liquidity and the price-discovery process in Nepal’s capital market more effective. The draft is built on the concept paper SEBON published on 2083/06/08 (September 24, 2026), which this site covered in September. What was then a paper about whether intraday trading should happen is now a draft regulation about how it would work. Its main features, as listed in the notice, are:

  • Permission first. The stock exchange and broker-dealers may offer intraday trading only after taking SEBON’s approval. No broker can switch it on unilaterally.
  • Eligible shares only. Only listed securities that meet the prescribed criteria qualify. The criteria themselves are not published in the notice, and neither is the list.
  • Suitability before access. A broker must assess a client’s identity, financial capacity, risk-bearing capacity and suitability before giving the facility — the first gate is the broker’s judgement of the client, not the client’s appetite.
  • Pre-trade checks. Before trading, the broker must test the client’s order, margin, risk limits and other conditions, and maintain the systems, security arrangements and real-time risk-control systems to run them.
  • Same-day square-off. An open position created by buying or selling under intraday trading must be closed the same day. If it is not closed within the prescribed time, it is closed automatically — auto square-off.
  • Margin, with variation. Each client must maintain the prescribed margin, and variation margin may be set according to market fluctuation. The notice states the mechanism, not the percentage.
  • Exposure limits at four levels. Maximum trading limits are to be set per client, per broker-dealer, per security and for the whole market — the draft caps the size of the bet at every layer, not just the trader’s.
  • Complaints and controls. Each broker-dealer must have risk-management, internal-control and grievance-management systems for intraday trading.
  • Investor education. SEBON, the exchange and brokers must run investor education and awareness programmes on intraday trading.
  • Phased arrival. SEBON may implement intraday trading in phases — the whole market does not have to go live at once.

Read together, the design is cautious in form: approval gates, eligibility screens, suitability checks, four layers of exposure limits and a phased rollout. That caution is the draft’s most important feature, and the reason the liquidity-versus-risk question has a split answer rather than a single one.

02. What it does not do — yet

The draft’s silences matter as much as its provisions, because the rumour version of this story will fill them with invented numbers. Here is what the notice does not contain:

  • It is not in force. This is a preliminary draft for consultation. Buying and selling the same share on the same day is not possible today; settlement is still T+2, and the draft changes nothing until a final regulation is adopted.
  • No margin percentage. The notice requires a prescribed margin and allows variation margin, but publishes no figure. No leverage multiple can be stated honestly from this notice — any article claiming a specific multiple is inventing it.
  • No eligible-share list. Which securities qualify is undecided in the published text. The concept paper envisaged starting with a limited set of liquid securities; the draft regulation does not restate that list or its criteria.
  • No launch date. The notice gives a feedback deadline, not an effective date. Phased implementation is possible, but no phase has a date.
  • Short selling is not settled by this text. The concept paper put sell-first-buy-later on hold until securities lending and borrowing infrastructure exists. The draft regulation’s square-off language covers closing a position by buying or selling, but the notice text does not restate the sequencing — whether short selling is in or out of the first phase is not stated in the notice.
  • Costs beyond commission are unaddressed. The 0.69% illustration in section 04 covers broker commission and the SEBON fee only. Whether a demat (DP) charge applies to a position that never settles into a demat account, and how same-day gains are taxed, are not addressed in the notice — so neither is included in the figure.

03. The liquidity case: why same-day trading could help

The case for intraday trading is straightforward, and SEBON’s own stated purpose — faster trading, more liquidity, better price discovery — is its first line. Today, a trader who buys in the morning is committed overnight: whatever happens after the 3 p.m. close, good or bad, belongs to them. That commitment keeps some participants out of the market entirely and makes others trade smaller than they would. Letting a position be opened and closed inside one session removes the overnight risk, and removing it should bring in orders that would otherwise never be placed: more buy and sell orders on both sides, tighter spreads between bid and ask, faster fills, and prices that reflect supply and demand sooner rather than later.

The benefit is not limited to the people who day-trade. Even if you never close a position the same day, a market with more orders is a cheaper and fairer market to trade in: your buy order is more likely to find a seller at a fair price, and your sell order is more likely to find a buyer. The concept paper made the same point in its three goals — deeper liquidity, more effective price discovery and modern market infrastructure — and put investor protection first among its guiding principles, ahead of phased implementation and real-time monitoring. That ordering is deliberate: the regulator is saying liquidity is the aim, but protection is the constraint.

The market intraday trading would land in is not a thin one. NEPSE turned over Rs 4.72 billion on November 5, 2026 — a single session’s turnover, from the exchange’s own close snapshot. The question is therefore not whether NEPSE needs trading volume conjured from nothing. It is whether the volume intraday trading adds is the kind that deepens the market, and that is where the liquidity case meets its honest limit: turnover and depth are not the same thing.

Turnover counts value changing hands. Depth is orders resting on the book, willing to trade at a stated price and to stay there. An intraday order is, by design, an order that cancels itself by the close: it is placed in the morning and gone by the afternoon. A thousand such orders will push reported turnover up sharply while leaving the order book at the close no deeper than it was. The draft will almost certainly raise turnover — the same capital recycling within the day, counted twice, once on the buy and once on the sell. Whether it deepens the book is unproven, and no verified depth metric is published that would let this article measure it. The honest statement of the liquidity case is therefore: more trading, very likely; a deeper market, not demonstrated.

04. The risk case: the 0.69% floor, the forced exit, and the churn problem

The risk case starts with arithmetic, not opinion. Every round trip on NEPSE pays the broker twice and SEBON twice, and the published charges make the floor computable. Take a Rs 100,000 trade as an illustration — a round number, labelled as such, on the published slabs: broker commission of 0.33% on amounts between Rs 50,000 and Rs 5 lakh, and the SEBON fee of 0.015% per side. Buying Rs 100,000 of shares costs Rs 330 in commission and Rs 15 in SEBON fees; selling the same Rs 100,000 costs the same again. The round trip costs Rs 690 — 0.69% of the traded value — before the trade makes a rupee.

The consequence is a break-even line. The price must rise 0.69% in your favour within hours just to stand still. A 1% move on Rs 100,000 is Rs 1,000 gross — minus Rs 690 of charges, Rs 310 kept. A 0.5% move is Rs 500 gross against the same Rs 690, a loss of Rs 190 on a trade that went the right way. A 2% move keeps Rs 1,310 of Rs 2,000. Small gains die in fees before they ever reach the trader’s pocket, and the smaller the move a trader is chasing, the larger the share the floor takes.

Frequency is what turns a floor into a drain. One round trip a month costs Rs 690 on a Rs 100,000 trade; ten round trips a month cost Rs 6,900 — 6.9% of the capital, every month, before a single price move is counted. Intraday trading does not change the fee; it changes how often the fee is paid, and the traders who trade most often are the ones who pay it most. That is a structural transfer from active retail traders to brokers and to the exchange, and it operates whether the trader’s calls are right or wrong.

The second risk is the exit the trader does not choose. Auto square-off closes a position at the prescribed time whether the price is kind or not. A position that is underwater at 2 p.m. and would have recovered by the close under delivery trading is instead closed at the day’s terms, and the loss is locked in by the system, not by the trader’s judgement. The same mechanism cuts the other way for a winning position closed early — the gain is capped by the clock. Add margin to the picture and the risk sharpens: margin lets a trader hold a larger position than the cash would allow, so the same 0.69% floor and the same adverse move are measured against a larger exposure, and variation margin can demand more money in the middle of the day, at the moment the trader can least afford it. The draft sets no margin percentage, so the size of that multiplier is unknown — which is itself a risk, because the market cannot price a leverage it cannot see.

The third risk is behavioural, and it is the one the concept paper’s design already anticipates: speed plus overconfidence is the fastest way beginners lose money. Intraday trading rewards discipline and punishes impulse, and most retail accounts run on impulse. A market that lets a beginner trade ten times a day is a market that lets a beginner pay the 0.69% floor ten times a day, and no safeguard in the draft can stop a determined trader from doing that to themselves. The suitability assessment is a gate, not a guardrail that follows the trader through the session.

05. The safeguards — real, but unquantified

None of this means the draft is reckless. Its safeguards are genuine, and they are the reason the system-level risk looks contained even while the trader-level risk looks high. The suitability assessment keeps the facility from being handed to everyone who asks. The pre-trade tests and real-time risk control give the broker a live view of the client’s exposure instead of discovering it after the close. The exposure limits operate at four levels — client, broker, security and whole market — so a single trader, a single broker or a single stock cannot take the system down with them. The grievance mechanism gives a trader somewhere to go when a square-off goes wrong. And the phased rollout is the strongest safeguard of all: it limits the blast radius to a first phase that can be watched, measured and stopped before the whole market is exposed.

What the safeguards lack is numbers. An exposure limit with no published cap is a promise, not a control. A margin requirement with no published percentage cannot be stress-tested by anyone outside SEBON. Eligibility criteria that are not published cannot be checked against the shares that end up on the list. The credibility of the whole design therefore depends on a step the draft has not taken yet: the final regulation publishing the margin percentage, the eligible-share list and the exposure caps before anyone trades a rupee intraday. If those numbers appear in the final regulation, the safeguards can be judged. If they appear only in circulars after launch, the market will be trading on a design it could not inspect.

The investor-education requirement deserves the same scrutiny. The draft obliges SEBON, the exchange and brokers to run awareness programmes — a sound provision, and one that only works if it reaches traders before they trade, in plain language, with the 0.69% floor in it. An education programme that explains the mechanism without the cost is a brochure, not a safeguard.

06. The road from here: dates that exist, dates that don’t

The timeline is short, and its most important feature is what it does not contain — a launch date. Three dates are fixed; everything after them is process, not schedule.

The gap between the third row and the fourth is the whole story of the next few months. After November 20, SEBON must weigh the feedback, finalise the regulation, publish the numbers the draft omits, and then approve the exchange and the brokers one by one. Any one of those steps can take weeks, and the notice gives no timetable for any of them. The September article’s headline holds for the draft as it held for the concept paper: do not expect it next month. What the feedback window does give the reader is a lever that closes on November 20: the draft is a consultation, and a consultation is only real if the people it affects answer it — above all on the numbers it omits, the margin percentage, the eligible list and the exposure caps.

07. What to take from it

Will intraday trading raise liquidity in NEPSE, or risk? Both — and the split is the finding. Turnover will rise, because the same capital will be counted twice a day, once on the buy and once on the sell. Depth is unproven, because an order that must be gone by the close does not deepen the book at the close. Risk will rise for the traders who use the facility most: a 0.69% floor on every round trip, a forced exit at the prescribed hour, margin that multiplies the move in both directions, and a frequency that turns a small fee into a monthly drain. Risk to the system looks contained, if the phased rollout holds and the exposure limits are real — but the limits have no published numbers yet, so that containment is a design claim, not a verified fact.

The draft is a good step in form and an incomplete one in substance. Consultation, suitability checks, four layers of exposure limits and a phased rollout are the right shape for a market taking on same-day trading. The missing numbers — the margin percentage, the eligible shares, the exposure caps — are the substance, and the test of this reform is not the draft but the final regulation: whether those numbers are published before anyone trades a rupee intraday, or discovered afterwards. Until then, the correct posture is the one the draft itself imposes: read it, send feedback while the window is open, and keep trading exactly as before. Nothing about your trading changes today, and nothing in this article is a recommendation to trade differently when it does.

Frequently asked questions

Can I buy and sell the same share on the same day now?

No. SEBON has published a preliminary draft regulation for consultation — it is not in force, and no launch date has been announced. Settlement is still T+2, and nothing about your trading changes until a final regulation is adopted and NEPSE and brokers receive SEBON approval to offer the facility.

When does the feedback window close?

Mangsir 4, 2083 — November 20, 2026. SEBON’s notice invites all stakeholders to send suggestions on the proposed draft by that date, through the draft link published with the notice on sebon.gov.np.

What does same-day square-off mean?

An open position created by buying or selling under intraday trading must be closed the same day — by selling what was bought, or buying back what was sold. If the position is not closed within the prescribed time, the system closes it automatically (auto square-off), at the prevailing price, whether or not the trader chooses that moment.

What will intraday trading cost?

The draft sets no fee of its own; the existing charges apply per side. On a Rs 100,000 trade, broker commission of 0.33% is Rs 330 and the SEBON fee of 0.015% is Rs 15 — Rs 345 a side, Rs 690 a round trip, or 0.69% of the traded value. That is a computed illustration from the published slabs. Whether a DP charge applies to a position that never settles into a demat account is not addressed in the notice.

Which shares will be eligible for intraday trading?

Only listed securities that meet the prescribed criteria — but the criteria and the list are not published in the notice. The concept paper envisaged starting with a limited set of liquid securities; the draft regulation does not restate the list. Until SEBON publishes it, no share can honestly be named as eligible.

Does the draft allow short selling?

Not settled by the notice text. The concept paper put sell-first-buy-later on hold until securities lending and borrowing infrastructure exists. The draft regulation’s square-off language covers closing a position by buying or selling, but it does not restate that sequencing — whether short selling is in or out of the first phase is not stated in the notice.

When will intraday trading actually start on NEPSE?

No date has been announced at any step. After the feedback window closes on November 20, 2026, SEBON must finalise the regulation, then approve NEPSE and each broker-dealer, and implementation may be phased. The September concept-paper article’s headline still holds: do not expect it next month.
