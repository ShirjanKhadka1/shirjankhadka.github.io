A Q1 report in five numbers — how to read what a listed company just filed

Nabil Bank earned Rs 2.55 billion in three months — the largest profit of any commercial bank in the quarter. The number only means something once you know what to set it against: the same quarter last year, the earnings per share, the book value, and whether the profit was bought with bad loans.

By Nepse Decode Research Desk · Kathmandu · November 10, 2026 · 20 min read
Facebook X LinkedIn WhatsApp Viber

A quarterly report lands on NEPSE and it looks like homework: pages of tables, a balance sheet, a profit-and-loss account, ratios with names that sound like they were designed to keep beginners out. Most investors do one of two things: they read none of it and trade on the headline, or they read all of it and remember none of it. There is a middle way. Five numbers tell you most of the story, and once you know what each one means, the rest of the report is detail you can come back to.

The timing is good for learning the habit. The first-quarter results season for FY2083/84 is under way: Nabil Bank published its Q1 report on November 1–2, 2026, and the twenty commercial banks together earned Rs 19.14 billion in the quarter, up 45.59% on the same three months a year earlier, according to Fiscal Nepal. Nabil’s Rs 2.55 billion was the largest single profit of the twenty. This article uses that one report, worked end to end, as the example — not because Nabil is a recommendation, but because a real report with real numbers is the only honest way to teach the reading.

Signal summary

  • The five numbers: net profit, the change on the same quarter last year, earnings per share, book value per share, and — for a bank — the non-performing loan ratio. Together they answer: how much did it earn, is that more or less than before, what is that per share you own, what is each share worth on paper, and was the profit bought by lending carelessly.
  • The worked example: Nabil Bank’s Q1 FY2083/84 (unaudited): net profit Rs 2.55 billion, up 45.43% from Rs 1.75 billion a year earlier; annualised EPS Rs 33.89; book value Rs 233.90 per share; NPL 4.06%, down from 4.31%.
  • What moved it: net interest income rose to Rs 4.73 billion from Rs 3.90 billion, and impairment charges — the cost of loans going bad — fell by almost three-quarters, to Rs 164 million from Rs 647.2 million. The profit jump is mostly those two lines.
  • The price check: at the November 6 close of Rs 549, Nabil trades at 16.20 times its annualised earnings. The commercial-bank peer median in our November 8 company analysis was 13.87 times. Whether that premium is high or low is a question only the peer comparison answers.
  • The qualifier: a quarterly report is unaudited — the company’s own first draft of the quarter. Read it as a progress update, and let the audited annual report have the last word.
  • The habit: read the five numbers, then compare — the same quarter last year, and the peers beside it. Compare, don’t conclude.

01. The season, and the report

Nepal’s fiscal year runs from mid-July to mid-July, so the first quarter — Q1 FY2083/84 — covers Shrawan to Ashoj, mid-July to mid-October 2026, ending on Ashwin 30. Listed companies file their quarterly reports with NEPSE, and the reports are published on each company’s disclosure page. Banks and other listed companies publish within about a month of the quarter’s end, so most first-quarter statements appear between late October and mid-November. Nabil’s was among the first, reported on November 1–2.

Two things about the document itself matter before any number in it does. First, it is unaudited. The figures are the company’s own, prepared by its management and not yet signed off by an external auditor — that is the normal status of a quarterly report everywhere, and it is printed on the report itself. Second, it is a quarter, not a year. Three months of a bank’s life are seasonal — festival months flatter lending and collections, quiet months do not — so a quarter is only ever read against the same quarter last year, never against the quarter before it. Both rules shape everything that follows.

The market context is worth one line, because it explains why this quarter’s reports are being watched more closely than usual. The twenty commercial banks earned Rs 19.14 billion between them in Q1, up 45.59% on the same quarter last year, and Nabil’s Rs 2.55 billion was the largest of the twenty — about 13% of the sector’s combined profit, computed from the two published totals — with Global IME second at Rs 2.31 billion. The market itself is closed November 9–12 for Tihar, so no fresh closing price is used anywhere in this article; the price figures below are the last verified close, November 6.

02. The five numbers

Here is the whole method in one table. Each number is defined, given its Nabil Q1 value, and given the question it answers. The sections after the table take them one at a time.

1. Net profit — what the company earned in three months. This is the headline number and the one every report leads with. Nabil’s Q1 net profit was Rs 2.55 billion. On its own that number is just big; it only means something against something else, which is why the next number matters more than this one.

2. The change — the same quarter last year. A profit figure without a comparison is a photograph with no scale. Nabil earned 45.43% more than the same quarter last year, and it was the largest profit of the twenty commercial banks. Always compare quarter to the same quarter, not to the previous quarter: businesses are seasonal, and a festival quarter will always flatter itself against a quiet one.

3. EPS — that profit, per share. Net profit belongs to the whole company; EPS divides it by the number of shares, so it speaks in the unit you own. Nabil’s annualised EPS was Rs 33.89, up from Rs 25.49 a year earlier. Quarterly EPS is annualised — multiplied by four — before anyone compares it with the share price, because the price reflects a full year of earnings, not three months of them. One disclosure: ShareSansar’s own release carries a lower EPS of Rs 30.34, which is preference-share adjusted; this article uses Rs 33.89, the figure ICTFrame, ShareHub and ShareGyan all carry, and the same figure used in our November 8 company analysis.

4. Book value per share — net worth per share. If the company stopped today and sold everything, paid every debt, and divided what was left among shareholders, this is what each share would be worth on paper. Nabil’s was Rs 233.90, up 4.28% from Rs 224.30 a year earlier. It is the slowest-moving of the five numbers, and that is its virtue: it does not jump around with one good quarter. (ShareGyan carries Rs 233.39 for the same line; this article uses Rs 233.90, as ShareSansar’s release and our November 8 analysis do.)

5. NPL — bad loans as a share of all loans. For banks, this is the asset-quality number. A loan that has stopped paying is non-performing, and the ratio says what fraction of the loan book has gone that way. Nabil’s was 4.06%, improved from 4.31% a year earlier, with net NPL — after provisions — at 0.63%. It is the number that tells you whether the profit in point one was bought by lending carelessly: a bank can always grow profit by lending to anyone, and NPL is where that bill eventually arrives.

03. What moved the profit

A 45% profit jump invites a second question: what actually changed inside the bank? A quarterly report answers it in the lines above net profit, and in Nabil’s case the answer is two lines, both moving in the bank’s favour at once.

The first is net interest income — the difference between the interest a bank earns on its loans and the interest it pays on its deposits. It is a bank’s core engine, and Nabil’s rose to Rs 4.73 billion from Rs 3.90 billion a year earlier, up 21.3%. Net fee and commission income helped too, rising to Rs 919.4 million from Rs 798.7 million, up 15.1% computed. Operating profit — profit before tax and provisions — reached Rs 3.67 billion, up 42.33%.

The second line is the one that did the heavier lifting. Impairment charges — the money a bank sets aside for loans it expects not to recover — fell to Rs 164 million from Rs 647.2 million a year earlier, down by almost three-quarters (74.7%, computed). When a bank sets aside less for bad loans, more of its operating profit reaches the bottom line. That is exactly what happened here: the profit jump is part genuine earnings growth and part a much smaller bad-loan bill than last year.

This is the part of the reading that the headline never gives you. A profit that grows because interest income grew is a different story from a profit that grows because the bad-loan bill shrank — and Nabil’s quarter is a bit of both, with the smaller impairment bill doing more of the work. The NPL number from the five-number table is the check on that second part: bad loans fell as a share of the book, from 4.31% to 4.06%, so the smaller impairment bill is consistent with a genuinely improving loan book rather than with a bank simply setting less aside. Had NPL risen while impairment fell, that would have been the first thing to question.

04. The one habit that makes it work

Read the five numbers, then do the one thing the report cannot do for you: compare. The same quarter last year, and the peers beside it. Nabil at Rs 549 against an annualised EPS of Rs 33.89 gives a P/E of 16.20 — that is, Rs 16.20 paid for every rupee of annual profit. The arithmetic is Rs 549 ÷ Rs 33.89 = 16.20. On book value the same price gives 2.35 times book (Rs 549 ÷ Rs 233.90 = 2.35, computed).

Whether 16.20 times is high or low is a question only the peer comparison answers. Against the peer median of 13.87 times, Nabil’s price pays more per rupee of earnings than its comparable banks do — and on book value, 2.35 times against a peer median of 1.84 times. That premium is the market’s judgement, not this article’s: the largest profit in the sector, the improving NPL, and the scale of the bank are all visible in the same report, and the reader’s job is to weigh them against the price rather than to take any single number’s word for it. The full peer comparison, with the valuation lenses it supports, lives in the Nabil company analysis of November 8; this article deliberately does not repeat it.

The same habit applies beyond banks. For a hydropower company the fifth number is not NPL but generation and revenue; for a manufacturer it is margins and inventory. The structure does not change: headline profit, the change, earnings per share, book value, and the one number that tells you whether the profit was bought cheaply. Learn the five for one company and the next report reads in minutes.

05. What the report does not tell you

A quarterly report is a progress update, and an honest reading says what it leaves out. It does not tell you the capital adequacy ratio in the five numbers above — this article shows no CAR because the five-number method does not need it, not because the bank hides it. It does not tell you the dividend: Nabil’s distributable profit for the quarter was Rs 2.26 billion, less than the Rs 2.55 billion net profit, because regulatory adjustments and appropriations sit between profit earned and profit that can be paid out. Across the sector the gap is starker — Merolagani reports the twenty banks’ cumulative distributable profit at a negative Rs 9.07 billion, weighed down by provisioning and accumulated past losses, even as their combined net profit rose 45%. Profit and dividend capacity are different things, and a Q1 report shows both if you look for them.

It also does not tell you what the share price should be. Nothing in a quarterly report is a price call: the report records three months that have already happened, while the price reflects what the market expects to happen next. And it does not tell you whether the quarter can repeat. Impairment charges can fall only so far — a bank cannot keep cutting its bad-loan bill below zero — so the part of this quarter’s jump that came from lower provisions is, by construction, harder to repeat than the part that came from higher interest income. That distinction is the single most useful thing a Q1 reading can give you, and it comes from reading the lines above net profit, not the headline.

What to take from it

A quarterly report is not a test. It is five numbers, read in order, then compared twice: against the same quarter last year, and against the peers beside it. Nabil’s Q1 gives the method a clean run: the largest profit in Nepali banking, up 45.43% on the year, built on higher interest income and a much smaller bad-loan bill, with asset quality improving alongside it — and a price that pays more per rupee of earnings than the peer median. None of those sentences is a conclusion on its own. Together, read the way this article reads them, they are the whole skill: know what each number is, know what to set it against, and remember that the report is unaudited, three months long, and already history. The next report that lands will take you five minutes.

Frequently asked questions

Where do I find a quarterly report?

Listed companies file them with NEPSE, and they are published on the company’s disclosure page. The first quarter of FY2083/84 covers Shrawan to Ashoj, mid-July to mid-October 2026, and most first-quarter statements appear between late October and mid-November.

Is a quarterly report audited?

No. Quarterly reports are unaudited; the audited figures come with the annual report. Read Q1 as a progress update, not a verdict — the numbers are the company’s own first draft of the quarter.

Why annualise EPS?

Because the share price reflects a full year of earnings. Comparing a three-month EPS directly with the price would make every company look four times more expensive than it is. Multiply the quarterly EPS by four first, then compare.

Why compare with the same quarter last year, not the last quarter?

Because businesses are seasonal. A festival quarter will always flatter itself against a quiet one, so the honest comparison is the same three months a year earlier — the same festivals, the same harvest, the same lending season.

Does a higher NPL always mean a worse bank?

Not by itself — it is a signal to investigate, not a verdict. Compare it with the bank’s own past quarters and with peers before drawing a conclusion. What matters most is the direction: Nabil’s NPL improved to 4.06% from 4.31% while its impairment bill fell, so the two lines agree with each other.

What is distributable profit, and why is it less than net profit?

Distributable profit is what remains after regulatory adjustments and appropriations — the part of the profit that can actually be paid out as dividend. Nabil earned Rs 2.55 billion and reported distributable profit of Rs 2.26 billion for the quarter. Across the sector the gap is starker: the twenty commercial banks’ cumulative distributable profit stood at a negative Rs 9.07 billion, weighed down by provisioning and accumulated past losses, even as their combined net profit rose 45%.

Can one good quarter justify a share price?

No — and a quarterly report never claims it can. A P/E built on one annualised quarter assumes the next three quarters look like this one, and the price reflects what the market expects next, not what the report records. Use the quarter to test the story — is the profit growing, is the loan book sound, what do peers pay — and let several quarters, and the audited annual report, do the justifying.
