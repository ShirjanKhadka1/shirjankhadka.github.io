NICLBSL at Rs 501: the market is paying for the book, not the earnings — 71.47× earnings against a 28.53× peer median

NIC ASIA Laghubitta earns Rs 7.01 a share, carries Rs 107.77 of book value, and trades at Rs 501 — 71.47 times earnings against a 28.53 times microfinance peer median, but 4.65 times book against a 4.80 times peer median. This analysis shows both lenses, every calculation, and the figures that did not agree.

By Nepse Decode Research Desk · Kathmandu · November 5, 2026 · 21 min read

NIC ASIA Laghubitta Bittiya Sanstha Limited (NICLBSL) is not NIC Asia Bank. The two share a promoter and the first half of a name, and they are easy to confuse — this article is about the smaller one, the Class-D microfinance institution, not the commercial bank analysed here on 2 November. Confusing the two would be a category error: one lends to low-income households and micro-entrepreneurs in small tickets, the other is a full commercial bank. Everything that follows is about the laghubitta, priced on its own numbers.

The headline finding is a split verdict. On earnings, the market is asking a great deal: at Rs 501, NICLBSL trades at 71.47 times its Rs 7.01 of earnings per share, against a 28.53 times median for six comparable microfinance peers. Put through that peer multiple, the earnings lens comes out at Rs 200.00 — less than half the price. On book value, the picture is ordinary: 4.65 times its Rs 107.77 of book value, against a 4.80 times peer median, giving a book lens of Rs 517.30, just above the price. The market, in other words, is paying for the balance sheet and waiting on the earnings. Whether that wait is reasonable depends on one question the numbers below keep returning to: is Rs 7.01 a depressed earnings year on its way back, or is it roughly what this lender now earns?

02. Valuation: paying for the book, waiting on the earnings

The peer set is six Class-D microfinance institutions with Q4 FY 2082/83 fundamentals on the same ShareHub basis and a 4 November 2026 close: National Laghubitta (NMFBS), Laxmi Laghubitta (LLBS), Mahila Laghubitta (MLBSL), NMB Microfinance (NMBMF), Himalayan Laghubitta (HLBSL) and Mithila Laghubitta (MLBBL). All six are national- or regional-level retail lenders, all recently traded, none excluded for illiquidity. The method is the standing one for this series: peer medians re-run fresh at the latest verified closes, the full peer table published so every median recomputes, adjusted and unadjusted prices never mixed, and anything unverifiable omitted as a gap.

How the medians are computed, so they can be checked: with six peers, the median is the average of the third and fourth values when sorted. PE sorted: 20.05, 27.04, 27.99, 29.07, 33.47, 45.89 — (27.99 + 29.07) ÷ 2 = 28.53. PB sorted: 3.94, 3.97, 4.21, 5.38, 5.48, 6.89 — (4.21 + 5.38) ÷ 2 = 4.795, shown as 4.80. EPS sorted: 20.07, 20.43, 24.20, 31.26, 37.91, 58.59 — (24.20 + 31.26) ÷ 2 = 27.73. Book value sorted: 148.70, 150.76, 162.52, 167.93, 204.16, 279.32 — (162.52 + 167.93) ÷ 2 = 165.23. Dropping the highest and lowest peer on each multiple (the trimmed check) leaves PE at 28.53× and PB at 4.80× — the medians are not being carried by one outlier at either end.

The earnings lens. Rs 7.01 × 28.53 = Rs 200.00 (Rs 199.995, rounded). At Rs 501, the market is paying 2.51 times what the peer earnings multiple would imply for this level of earnings. Put differently, for the price to be justified on earnings alone at the peer median multiple, EPS would need to be Rs 17.56 (501 ÷ 28.53) — two and a half times the reported Rs 7.01. That is the arithmetic; it is not a claim that the price will fall to Rs 200, and it is not a target. Prices routinely sit far from any single lens, and a recovering lender’s earnings are the worst possible denominator to annualise mechanically — which is exactly why the book lens is shown beside it rather than instead of it.

The sensitivity, labelled as such. If EPS recovered to the peer median of Rs 27.73, the earnings lens at the peer median multiple would be 27.73 × 28.53 = Rs 791.14. At half the peer median EPS (Rs 13.87), it would be Rs 395.57. These are sensitivities — arithmetic answers to “what if earnings were higher?” — never a forecast and never a target. They show how much of the current price depends on earnings recovery: at the present Rs 7.01, the earnings lens covers only 40% of the price; at half-median earnings it would cover 79%; only at full-median earnings would it exceed the price. The question for a holder is therefore not whether Rs 501 is “cheap” in the abstract, but whether Rs 7.01 is a trough.

The book-value disagreement. Nepse Decode’s own stock page carries a different book value for NICLBSL — Rs 162.72 a share, with PB 3.06× at its Rs 498 snapshot — against ShareHub’s Rs 107.77 and 4.65×. Both sets are internally consistent at their own prices; they cannot both be the same definition of book value, and this article could not verify which definition each source uses. The peer comparison therefore stays on one basis throughout (ShareHub), so the medians recompute from the published table. If the site page’s book value were used instead, the book lens would be materially higher (162.72 × 4.80 = Rs 781.06) — which only strengthens the split verdict’s book half and does not rescue the earnings half, since EPS (Rs 7.01) and PE (71.47×) agree across both sources. The disagreement is printed here rather than resolved by picking the flattering figure.

03. Financials: a profit recovered, a distributable hole not yet closed

The full-year picture for fiscal 2082/83, on the unaudited Q4 figures, is a recovery from near-zero — and the word “recovery” needs its full context. Net profit for the year is Rs 122.0 million (Nepse Decode’s stock page; Rs 121.93 million implied by EPS × shares, which agrees within rounding), giving EPS of Rs 7.01 on 1,73,94,400 shares. The third-quarter report, covered by ICTFrame on 16 April 2026, showed nine-month net profit of Rs 10.69 crore against just Rs 0.13 crore in the same period of the previous year — a near-total collapse followed by a return to profit, not a steady earnings stream. The same third-quarter report carried distributable profit of Rs −32.37 crore: even nine months into a profitable year, accumulated losses meant there was, on that report, nothing distributable to shareholders.

That distinction — profit versus distributable profit — is the single most important financial fact in this article after the EPS gap, because it governs dividends. A company can report a profitable year and still have no distributable surplus if earlier losses have not been absorbed. Whether the fourth quarter closed that hole is unverified at publish time: the audited FY 2082/83 statements, the year-end distributable EPS, the non-performing-loan ratio, the loan book and deposit totals, and any impairment charge for the year were not verified from a published source this article could open, so none of them is stated here. A reader who wants the earnings story complete needs those five figures; this article names the gap instead of filling it with a peer average or an estimate.

The capital structure, by contrast, is fully stated. Paid-up capital is Rs 173.94 crore. Promoters hold 68% and the public 32% — a thinner public float than the microfinance peers in the table in some cases, though float alone does not explain the price: the 4 November tape (4,091 shares) is small in absolute terms, but the float of 55.66 lakh shares is not, by itself, a scarcity story, and the stock traded in each session shown in the site page’s recent record. No lock-in or float-scarcity premium is claimed here; none is verified, and the promoter holding is a plain fact, not a thesis. Book value per share of Rs 107.77 sits just above the Rs 100 face value, which is consistent with a lender that has recently rebuilt from losses rather than accumulated large reserves: net worth implied by that book value is about Rs 187.46 crore (107.77 × 1,73,94,400 shares).

04. Dividends, and the question of what a holder actually receives

The latest dividend record ShareHub carries for NICLBSL is for fiscal 2079/80: a total dividend of 15%, all cash (0% bonus shares, 15% cash), announced on 18 October 2023, with book closure on 11 December 2023. No later dividend proposal for fiscal 2080/81, 2081/82 or 2082/83 was verified at publish time. That is stated as a verification gap, not as a claim that no dividend was proposed: the absence of a verified record in the sources opened for this article is not evidence about board meetings this article did not see. But the combination matters for anyone weighing the price: the last verified cash return to holders dates to a fiscal year three years before the one just reported, and the third-quarter distributable deficit explains, mechanically, why a dividend for the year just ended cannot be assumed.

For context within the peer set, dividend recency varies widely and is not used as a valuation input here: Himalayan Laghubitta, for example, declared a 5% dividend for FY 2082/83 (announced 2 November 2026, per ShareHub), while other peers’ latest records also date back several years. The point of the comparison is not that NICLBSL is uniquely silent; it is that a holder of NICLBSL at Rs 501 is currently holding for price and for a future earnings recovery, not for a demonstrated recent cash return. The dividend yield on the last verified 15% cash dividend, measured against today’s price, would be a historical artefact, not a yield — and it is not presented as one.

05. What to watch, and what this article does not claim

Five publications would change this analysis, in order of importance. First, the audited FY 2082/83 statements: if audited EPS differs from the unaudited Rs 7.01, both lenses move, and the earnings lens moves proportionally. Second, the year-end distributable profit: a positive figure reopens the dividend question; a still-negative figure keeps it closed. Third, the non-performing-loan ratio and impairment charge: microfinance earnings are, more than most sectors, a provisioning story, and a low EPS with high provisioning reads very differently from a low EPS with a clean book. Fourth, the loan book and deposit growth: whether the lender is growing out of its trough or shrinking into it. Fifth, any dividend proposal for FY 2082/83, which would be the first verified proposal in the recent record and would deserve its own treatment, not a footnote here.

What this article does not claim, plainly: it does not claim Rs 501 is cheap or expensive, because its two lenses disagree and the disagreement is the finding. It does not claim the price will move toward either lens. It does not claim alpha, volatility or beta for NICLBSL, because none was verified. It does not adopt Nepse Decode’s engine verdict (Exit / Reduce, score −3, as of 3 November) as a recommendation; engine verdicts are mechanical readings of past prices, and the recent record itself shows three bounce-back cases in which sell-side verdicts were followed by green sessions. And it does not copy any other publication’s valuation, rating or intrinsic value: every multiple above is computed here from the published peer table, and every figure traces to a named source below.

The cleanest one-sentence summary is this: at Rs 501 you are paying a peer-normal price for Rs 107.77 of book value and a peer-exceptional price for Rs 7.01 of earnings. If earnings recover toward the peer median, the earnings lens rises fast — Rs 395.57 at half-median earnings, Rs 791.14 at median earnings, as sensitivities, not forecasts. If earnings stay near Rs 7.01, the book lens is the only leg holding the price up, and book value for a lender with a recently negative distributable position deserves more scepticism than book value for a lender with a long profitable record. That is the bet, stated without dressing it up as a conclusion the numbers do not support.

Frequently asked questions

Is NICLBSL the same company as NIC Asia Bank (NICA)?

No. NICLBSL is NIC ASIA Laghubitta Bittiya Sanstha Limited, a Class-D microfinance institution promoted by NIC Asia Bank and listed separately on NEPSE since 8 July 2020. NICA is the commercial bank, analysed separately on 2 November 2026 at Rs 331. They share a promoter and part of a name; they do not share a balance sheet, an EPS or a price.

Why is NICLBSL’s PE ratio 71.47× — is that a data error?

It reconciles exactly: Rs 501 ÷ Rs 7.01 = 71.47×, and ShareHub publishes the same figure. The multiple is high because the denominator is low, not because the price is extreme: Rs 501 is near the bottom of the Rs 485–679 52-week range. A high PE on depressed earnings says more about the earnings year than about the price level.

What does the earnings lens of Rs 200.00 mean?

It is arithmetic, not a value or a target: NICLBSL’s EPS of Rs 7.01 multiplied by the peer median PE of 28.53× gives Rs 200.00. It answers “what would this earnings stream be worth at a typical peer multiple?” and nothing more. It is not a prediction that the price will fall to Rs 200.

Why does the book lens sit above the price while the earnings lens sits far below it?

Because the two denominators tell different stories. Book value of Rs 107.77 at a peer-normal 4.80× gives Rs 517.30, just above the Rs 501 price — the balance sheet is priced like its peers. Earnings of Rs 7.01 at a peer-normal 28.53× give Rs 200.00 — the earnings stream is priced at 2.51 times its peer-multiple value. The market is paying for the book and waiting on the earnings.

When did NICLBSL last pay a dividend?

The latest verified record is fiscal 2079/80: 15% cash (0% bonus), announced 18 October 2023, book closure 11 December 2023, per ShareHub. No dividend proposal for fiscal 2080/81, 2081/82 or 2082/83 was verified at publish time; that is a verification gap, not a claim that none was proposed.

What would change this analysis most?

The audited FY 2082/83 statements first: audited EPS moves both lenses proportionally. Then the year-end distributable profit (which was Rs −32.37 crore at the third quarter), the non-performing-loan ratio and impairment charge, loan-book growth, and any dividend proposal for FY 2082/83.

So is Rs 501 cheap or expensive?

Neither label fits both lenses, and this article does not pick one. On book value it is roughly peer-priced (4.65× vs 4.80× median); on earnings it is 2.51 times the peer-multiple lens. The honest summary is the split itself: a peer-normal price for the book, a peer-exceptional price for the earnings, with the investment case resting on whether Rs 7.01 is a trough.
