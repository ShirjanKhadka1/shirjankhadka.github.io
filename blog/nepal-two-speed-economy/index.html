Nepal's economy is running at two speeds

A record Rs 3.946 trillion in reserves. Inflation at an 18-month high. Both numbers came from the same central bank report.

By Shirjan Khadka · Founder, Nepse Decode, Kathmandu · September 27, 2026 · Updated September 28, 2026 · 6 min read
Facebook X LinkedIn WhatsApp Viber

Two numbers from Nepal Rastra Bank's latest monthly report do not sit comfortably together. Nepal's foreign exchange reserves reached a record Rs 3.946 trillion, enough to pay for 18.8 months of imports. In the same report, consumer inflation jumped to 5.96%, the highest in a year and a half. The external account has never looked stronger; the household budget has not looked this stretched since Mangsir 2081. The report, covering Shrawan, the first month of fiscal year 2083/84, was released on September 18, 2026. Here is what it actually says, and what the split tells us.

Start with prices, because that is where the pain is. The inflation story is overwhelmingly a food story. The food and beverage group's index rose 6.77% year-on-year, a sharp reversal from -2.28% a year earlier: the same basket that was getting cheaper is now getting expensive. Within food, the worst pressure sits in daily essentials. The ghee and oil sub-group rose 15.62%, fruits 15.39%, fish and meat 8.65%, vegetables 6.90%, and spices 4.80%. Non-food inflation is calmer at 5.52%, which confirms the spike is supply-side rather than demand-driven. The likely culprits are well documented: this season's floods damaged transport links and agricultural output, raising logistics costs and constraining food supply, the same dynamic behind the ADB's revised FY2026/27 inflation forecast of 5.3%. A 5.96% headline reading in Shrawan already sits above the central bank's own comfort zone.

On the other side of the ledger, the external numbers read like a different country. Remittance inflows grew 21.2% to Rs 215.05 billion (US$1.40 billion) in a single month, a powerful reading even allowing that part of the rupee surge reflects the weaker exchange rate rather than purely larger transfers. Combine that with exports growing 61.7%, far outpacing the 31.0% rise in imports, and the arithmetic produces the rare twin: a Rs 94.59 billion current account surplus and a Rs 90.34 billion balance-of-payments surplus, both stronger than last year. Reserves at $25.84 billion give Nepal one of the most comfortable import-cover ratios in South Asia. On paper, the external position is bulletproof.

So why does a record reserve pile coexist with rising inflation and sluggish domestic activity? The report itself contains the answer. Broad money shrank 0.4% during the month and domestic credit fell 1.2%; deposits are growing 14.5% year on year, but private-sector credit is growing only about 7%, and the report notes plainly that "economic activity within the country has not increased as expected." Remittance money arrives, accumulates at the central bank as reserves, and keeps the currency and external accounts strong, but it does not automatically translate into productive domestic lending or investment. The money is in the system; the economy is not quite using it.

Three developments will shape the next data points. First, the Dashain effect: the festival season is now underway, with the central bank distributing new notes from Ghatasthapana, and the pre-festival months typically lift import demand and cash in circulation. In Shrawan itself the central bank was a heavy dollar buyer, purchasing USD 665.8 million from the market against USD 554.2 million a year earlier. The remittance engine is powerful but seasonal. Second, flood reconstruction will likely push imports higher in the coming months, construction materials, equipment, fuel, widening the trade deficit even as it supports growth. Third, the inflation path depends on whether food-supply disruptions prove temporary. If logistics normalize, the 5.96% print could mark the peak; if floods compound with the fuel-price pressure flagged by the ADB, inflation could stay elevated through the winter.

For now, the picture is a two-speed economy: externally robust, domestically strained. Reserves buy Nepal time and policy space. The harder question, how to convert that external strength into domestic activity without reigniting inflation, is the one to watch in the NRB's next monthly release.
