Nepal's Two-Speed Economy: Record Rs 3.946 Trillion in Reserves, Inflation at an 18-Month High

Nepal Rastra Bank's Shrawan report shows the external account at its strongest ever, while food prices squeeze household budgets. What the split means.

By Shirjan Khadka Financial Analyst, Kathmandu
Published September 27, 2026 · 08:30 NPT Updated September 27, 2026 · 08:30 NPT 6 min read
Share Facebook X LinkedIn

At a glance

  • What happened: Nepal Rastra Bank's Shrawan report, released September 19, 2026, shows forex reserves at a record Rs 3.946 trillion (US$25.84 billion), covering 18.8 months of imports.
  • Inflation: Consumer inflation hit 5.96% in Shrawan, the highest in about 18 months and above the central bank's 5.5% target; food and beverage prices rose 6.77%.
  • The engine: Remittance inflows reached Rs 215.05 billion (US$1.40 billion) in a single month, the highest ever recorded (+21.2% year-on-year), while exports grew 61.7%.
  • The disconnect: Bank deposits fell 0.3% and private-sector credit grew just 0.4%: in the report's words, economic activity within the country has not increased as expected.

Two numbers from Nepal Rastra Bank's latest monthly report do not sit comfortably together. Nepal's foreign exchange reserves reached a record Rs 3.946 trillion, enough to pay for 18.8 months of imports. In the same report, consumer inflation jumped to 5.96%, the highest in the past one and a half years. The external account has never looked stronger; the household budget has not looked this stretched since Mangsir 2081. Welcome to Nepal's two-speed economy.

The report, covering Shrawan, the first month of fiscal year 2083/84, was released on September 19, 2026. Here is what it actually says, figure by figure, and what the split between the external and domestic numbers tells us about where Nepal's economy stands.

Why are prices rising?

The inflation story is overwhelmingly a food story. The food and beverage group's consumer price index rose 6.77% year-on-year, a sharp reversal from −2.28% a year earlier, the same basket that was getting cheaper is now getting expensive. Within food, the worst pressure sits in daily essentials: the ghee and oil sub-group rose 15.62%, fruits 15.39%, fish and meat 8.65%, vegetables 6.90%, and spices 4.80%.

Non-food inflation is calmer at 5.52%, which confirms the spike is supply-side rather than demand-driven. The likely culprits are well documented elsewhere: this month's floods damaged transport links and agricultural output, raising logistics costs and constraining food supply, the same dynamic behind the ADB's revised FY2026/27 inflation forecast of 5.3%, which I covered in yesterday's article. A 5.96% headline reading in Shrawan already sits above the central bank's own comfort zone.

Why is the external sector thriving?

On the other side of the ledger, the external numbers read like a different country. Remittance inflows grew 21.2% to Rs 215.05 billion (US$1.40 billion) in a single month, the highest monthly inflow Nepal has ever recorded. In dollar terms the growth was 10.2%, meaning part of the rupee surge reflects the weaker exchange rate rather than purely larger transfers, but the underlying flow remains powerful.

Combine that with exports growing 61.7%, far outpacing the 31.0% rise in imports, and the arithmetic produces the rare twin: a Rs 94.59 billion current account surplus and a Rs 90.34 billion balance-of-payments surplus, both stronger than last year. Reserves at $25.84 billion give Nepal one of the most comfortable import-cover ratios in South Asia. On paper, the external position is bulletproof.

Nepal is living two economies at once: an external economy powered by migrant workers and export momentum, and a domestic economy where food prices are squeezing household budgets at 15%+ in essentials.

Why doesn't the money reach the domestic economy?

So why does a record reserve pile coexist with rising inflation and sluggish domestic activity? The report itself contains the answer. Deposits mobilized by banks and financial institutions declined 0.3% during the month, while credit to the private sector grew just 0.4%. The report notes plainly that "economic activity within the country has not increased as expected."

Remittance money arrives, accumulates at the central bank as reserves, and keeps the currency and external accounts strong, but it does not automatically translate into productive domestic lending or investment. With the interbank rate at 2.75%, the average deposit rate at 3.15% and the average lending rate at 6.48%, banks are flush with liquidity yet cautious about extending credit. The money is in the system; the economy is not quite using it.

What should you watch next?

Three developments will shape the next data points. First, the Dashain effect: CESLAM data shows banks sold foreign currency worth $927.5 million to the central bank in the roughly three months since mid-July, versus $1.02 billion a year earlier, and in the month before Dashain (which ran September 21 to October 5 this year), sales fell 25% to $415.5 million. The remittance engine is powerful but seasonal; the festival window may show a temporary softening.

Second, flood reconstruction will likely push imports higher in the coming months, construction materials, equipment, fuel, widening the trade deficit even as it supports growth. Third, the inflation path depends on whether food-supply disruptions prove temporary. If logistics normalize, the 5.96% print could mark the peak; if floods compound with the Middle East's fuel-price pressure flagged by the ADB, inflation could stay elevated through the winter.

For now, the picture is a two-speed economy: externally robust, domestically strained. Reserves buy Nepal time and policy space. The harder question, how to convert that external strength into domestic activity without reigniting inflation, is the one to watch in the NRB's next monthly release.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Nepal's current inflation rate?
According to Nepal Rastra Bank's Shrawan report released in September 2026, inflation reached 5.96% in the first month of FY 2083/84, the highest level in the past one and a half years. A year earlier, in the same month, inflation stood at just 1.68%.
How large are Nepal's foreign exchange reserves?
Nepal's foreign exchange reserves reached Rs 3.946 trillion (US$25.84 billion), the strongest level ever recorded, sufficient to cover 18.8 months of imports of goods and services.
Why is Nepal's inflation rising while reserves are at a record high?
The two trends have different drivers. Reserves are powered by record remittance inflows (Rs 215.05 billion in a single month) and a 61.7% export surge. Inflation is driven domestically by food-price pressure, food and beverage inflation hit 6.77%, with ghee and oil up 15.62% and fruits up 15.39%, alongside flood-related supply and logistics constraints. A strong external position does not automatically cool domestic food prices.
Is remittance inflow to Nepal still growing?
Yes. Remittance inflows grew 21.2% year-on-year to Rs 215.05 billion (US$1.40 billion) in the first month of FY 2083/84, the highest monthly inflow recorded so far. However, CESLAM data shows the pace softened around Dashain: banks sold 25% less foreign currency to the central bank in the month before the festival than a year earlier.
