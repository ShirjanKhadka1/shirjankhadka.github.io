Rs 5,000 buys the shares — Rs 0 reaches the company: where your money actually goes on NEPSE

NEPSE is the secondary market. When you buy shares there, your money goes to the investor selling them — not to the company. The company is paid only once, when it issues new shares. The belief that buying on NEPSE funds the company is comforting, widely held, and wrong about the plumbing.

By Nepse Decode Research Desk · Kathmandu · November 7, 2026 · 16 min read
Facebook X LinkedIn WhatsApp Viber

Ask a new investor what happens to their money when they buy shares on NEPSE, and the confident answer comes back fast: it goes to the company, which uses it to build things. It sounds right. It feels like how investing should work. And it is wrong about the transaction itself. When you buy shares on NEPSE, your money goes to the investor who sold them to you. The company receives nothing — not a rupee, not a share of the commission, not a paisa of the fees. The company was paid once, at the moment it issued those shares, and that moment is already in the past. Everything after it is investors trading among themselves. This article follows the money through both markets, with the numbers printed, so the distinction is visible rather than asserted.

Signal summary

  • The myth: buying shares on NEPSE funds the company. The fact: NEPSE is the secondary market — previously issued shares traded between investors, and the company does not participate in the transaction.
  • The primary market is the only place the company gets paid: when it issues new shares — an IPO, an FPO or a right issue. Investors buy directly from the company, so the money goes to the company.
  • The worked example (illustrative round numbers): a company issues 10,00,000 shares at Rs 100 in an IPO — Rs 10,00,00,000, or Rs 10 crore, goes to the company. You then buy 10 of those shares on NEPSE at Rs 500 — Rs 5,000 goes to the selling investor, and Rs 0 to the company.
  • The charges go elsewhere too: on that Rs 5,000 buy, broker commission is Rs 18 (0.36%) and the SEBON fee is Rs 0.75 (0.015%) — to the broker and the regulator, never to the company. The DP charge of Rs 25 per scrip per transfer day goes to the depository system, also never to the company.
  • A rising price does not fill the company’s bank account: if the share rises from Rs 500 to Rs 600, your 10 shares are worth Rs 6,000 instead of Rs 5,000 — a Rs 1,000 gain in market value. The company’s cash from that rise is Rs 0.
  • The one real channel: a higher secondary-market price can only help the company if it later issues new shares, at better terms — and only when it actually does. Dividends, likewise, come from the company’s profit, not from your purchase money.

01. Two markets, two different roads for the same rupee

Nepal’s share market is two markets wearing one name, and the money takes a different road in each. The distinction is definitional — it is how the market is structured, not a matter of opinion — and NIC Asia Bank’s investor-education helpdesk states it in the plainest terms available: “In the primary market, investors buy securities directly from the company issuing them. In the secondary market, investors trade securities among themselves, and the company with the security being traded does not participate in the transaction.” NEPSE, the same helpdesk notes, “is the only stock exchange of Nepal” — the country’s sole secondary-market operator, running the fully screen-based NEPSE Automated Trading System.

SEBON draws the same line in its investor guidance. For World Investor Week 2026, the regulator “particularly encouraged new investors to begin their participation through the primary market and mutual funds, while gradually developing their understanding of the secondary market” — a sequence that only makes sense because the two markets are different doors into the same companies, with different risks attached. The primary market is where a company raises capital. The secondary market is where investors trade the claims on that capital among themselves.

02. Follow Rs 5,000 through a NEPSE trade

Take the simplest possible trade, on round numbers labelled as an illustration — no real company, no real share price, no real transaction. You buy 10 shares at Rs 500 on NEPSE. The trade value is 10 × 500 = Rs 5,000. That Rs 5,000, minus nothing, goes to the investor who sold you those shares. The company’s share of it is Rs 0 — not a rounding error, not a small slice, zero. The shares you bought were created and paid for long ago, when the company issued them; what you are buying is another investor’s claim on the company, and the price you pay is the price that investor accepted.

The charges on top of the trade value follow the same rule — they go to the intermediaries, never to the company. On a Rs 5,000 buy, broker commission at the lowest slab (0.36% on amounts up to Rs 50,000) is Rs 18, and the SEBON fee (0.015% per side) is Rs 0.75 — Rs 18.75 in total, paid to the broker and the regulator. The DP charge of Rs 25 per scrip per transfer day is a separate transfer-day charge that goes to the depository system. Every rupee of the cost of trading is accounted for, and the company’s column in that accounting is empty at every line.

This is also why the trade settles the way it does. The money moves from your broker account to the seller’s, and the shares move from the seller’s demat account to yours, on the T+2 settlement cycle — two working days after the trade. The company is not a counterparty to any step of that settlement. Its share register records that the shares changed hands; its bank account does not move.

03. Follow Rs 10 crore through an IPO

Now run the same arithmetic through the other market. A company issues 10,00,000 new shares at Rs 100 — the standard face value for a fixed-price IPO in Nepal — and the issue is fully subscribed. The money raised is 10,00,000 × 100 = Rs 10,00,00,000: Rs 10 crore, paid by investors directly to the company. That is the primary market doing the one thing only it can do — putting new capital into a company. The company can use it to build a project, expand capacity or repay debt. The issue manager’s fees and the regulator’s charges are taken from that sum; the balance is the company’s to deploy.

The contrast with section 02 is the whole article in two numbers: Rs 10 crore to the company when it issues new shares, Rs 0 to the company when those same shares change hands on NEPSE. The share is the same instrument in both cases. What differs is the transaction — a new share being created and sold by the company, versus an existing share being resold by one investor to another. The price in the first case is set by the issue, typically Rs 100 face value for a fixed-price IPO; book-built issues, where the price is discovered from bids, can be priced above par. The price in the second case is set by whatever buyers and sellers agree on the screen that day.

The same logic covers the other two ways a company issues new shares. In an FPO (further public offering) the company sells additional new shares to the public; in a right issue it offers new shares to its existing shareholders first. In both cases the money goes to the company, because in both cases new shares are being created. A bonus share is the one apparent exception that proves the rule: the company issues new shares to existing shareholders, but takes no fresh money for them — it capitalises its own reserves instead. The test is always the same: did the company issue new shares and receive payment for them? If yes, it is the primary market. If the shares already existed, it is the secondary market, and the company is not being paid.

04. A rising price does not fill the company’s bank account

The myth survives because it confuses two different quantities that move together: the market value of your holding, and the cash in the company’s bank account. They are not the same number, and only one of them moves when the price moves. Take the same 10 shares from section 02. You paid Rs 500 a share — Rs 5,000. The price rises to Rs 600. Your holding is now worth 10 × 600 = Rs 6,000, a Rs 1,000 gain in market value. The company’s bank balance has not changed by a rupee. The price is a measure of what investors are willing to pay each other for the share today; it is not a transfer of cash to the company, and no mechanism exists by which a secondary-market price rise deposits money in the issuer’s account.

There is one genuine indirect channel, and it is worth stating precisely rather than letting it rescue the myth. A company whose shares trade at a high price can, when it next issues new shares — an FPO, for example — command better terms, including a premium over face value. But that benefit arrives only if and when the company actually issues new shares, and only from the new money those new shares raise. A high price on its own, with no fresh issue behind it, puts nothing in the company’s hands. The price is a signal about the company; it is not a payment to it.

05. Where dividends come from — and the one way to actually fund a company

Dividends are the second place the myth hides. If the company pays you a dividend, it is tempting to think it is returning some of the money you paid for the shares. It is not. A dividend is paid out of the company’s profit — the earnings it made running its business — and only shareholders on record at book closure receive it. Your purchase money went to the previous owner; the dividend comes from the company’s own earnings, distributed to whoever holds the shares when the books close. The two flows never meet.

That leaves exactly one way for your money to reach a company: subscribe when it issues new shares. Apply for an IPO, an FPO or a right issue through MeroShare, and your payment goes to the company, because you are buying shares the company itself is selling. That is the practical meaning of SEBON’s World Investor Week advice to begin in the primary market — it is the market where your money funds the business, and it is also, for a new investor, the simpler market to start in: a fixed price, a fixed application, and no screen to watch. The secondary market has a different purpose, and it is a real one: it gives you liquidity — the ability to sell when you need to — and it gives every share a visible, tradeable price. What it does not do is channel your money to the company. Knowing which market does what is the difference between investing in a company and trading with another investor.

06. What to take from it

The correction is small and the consequences are not. If you believed buying on NEPSE funded the company, you were judging your trades by the wrong test — whether the company “deserved” your money — instead of the right one: whether the price you are paying another investor is a fair price for a claim on the company’s future earnings. The secondary market is a market in claims, not a funding channel, and its discipline is the price: the seller’s gain on your trade is your cost, the price can fall as well as rise, and nobody in the transaction is underwriting the company’s business. Fund a company by subscribing to its new issues. Trade on NEPSE by asking what the share is worth to you at the price on the screen — and remember that the investor on the other side of that screen is the one your money is going to. Nothing in this article is a recommendation to buy or sell any share.

Frequently asked questions

When I buy shares on NEPSE, where does my money go?

To the investor selling you the shares. NEPSE is the secondary market, where previously issued shares are traded between investors; the company does not participate in the transaction and receives nothing from it.

When does a company actually receive money from investors?

Only when it issues new shares — an IPO, an FPO or a right issue. That is the primary market: investors buy directly from the company, so the money goes to the company. In the illustration in this article, 10,00,000 shares at Rs 100 puts Rs 10 crore into the company.

Does a rising share price help the company?

Not directly — no cash reaches the company from a price rise on NEPSE. The only indirect channel is a later fresh issue: a company whose shares trade high can command better terms, including a premium, when it next issues new shares — and only when it actually issues them.

What about the broker commission and SEBON fee I pay?

They go to the broker and the regulator, never to the company. On a Rs 5,000 buy, broker commission at the 0.36% slab is Rs 18 and the SEBON fee at 0.015% is Rs 0.75. The DP charge of Rs 25 per scrip per transfer day is a separate transfer-day charge to the depository system.

Where do dividends come from — my purchase money?

No. Dividends are paid out of the company’s profit, and only shareholders on record at book closure receive them. Your purchase money went to the previous owner; the dividend comes from the company’s own earnings.

How do I actually give money to a company?

Subscribe when it issues new shares — apply for its IPO, FPO or right issue through MeroShare. That is the one route by which your payment reaches the company, and it is the route SEBON advises new investors to begin with.

If buying on NEPSE does not fund the company, is the secondary market pointless?

No. The secondary market gives you liquidity — the ability to sell when you need to — and a visible, tradeable price for every share. What it does not do is channel your money to the company. Judge a NEPSE trade by whether the price is fair for a claim on the company’s future earnings, not by whether the company “deserves” your money.
