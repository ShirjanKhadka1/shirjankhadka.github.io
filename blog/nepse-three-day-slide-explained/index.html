NEPSE fell for three straight sessions. Nobody panicked.

From Monday to Wednesday the index shed 30.91 points and losers outnumbered gainers roughly four to one. Yet each day's fall was smaller than the last, turnover held near Rs 5 billion, and no single headline explains the slide.

By Shirjan Khadka · Aspiring Financial Analyst, Kathmandu · October 1, 2026 · 6 min read
Facebook X LinkedIn WhatsApp Viber

On Wednesday, the NEPSE index fell 4.82 points to close at 2,598.89. It was the third consecutive losing session, and it took the index below the 2,600 mark. Three sessions, 30.91 points gone, and no single headline to explain any of it.

The sequence is worth laying out plainly. On Monday the index dropped 16.47 points, or 0.62 percent, to 2,613.33. On Tuesday it fell another 9.62 points to 2,603.71. Wednesday's 4.82-point slip brought the three-day total to 30.91 points, about 1.2 percent, from Friday's close of 2,629.80. Session reports from farsightnepal and investopaper put daily turnover at Rs 5.38 billion, Rs 4.65 billion and Rs 4.76 billion across the three days. Wednesday's session saw 12.66 million shares change hands in 47,900 transactions across 352 securities, and the Sensitive index fell 2.02 points to 464.63.

Look closer and the selling has a shape. Each day's loss was smaller than the last: 16.47, then 9.62, then 4.82. Turnover thinned from Monday to Tuesday and then steadied instead of draining away. Market breadth told the same story in reverse. On Monday more than 220 companies fell while only about 50 advanced. Tuesday was deeply red for a second day. By Wednesday the count was 157 losers against 109 gainers. Still negative, but the sellers were losing their majority. A market in panic does not usually see its daily losses halve twice in a row while turnover holds near Rs 5 billion.

Monday's leaderboard carried its own message. The day's biggest gainers were not stocks at all but debentures: KBL Debenture 2090 rose 12.17 percent, Laxmi Sunrise Debenture 10 percent, and Prime Bank Debenture 8.83 percent. Meanwhile Hathway Investment Nepal fell 10.58 percent to Rs 405 and Emerging Nepal dropped 9.92 percent to Rs 496. When fixed-income paper tops the gainers' list on a day equities bleed, it reads as money stepping sideways rather than rushing for the exit. The turnover leaderboard stayed busy too: Himalayan Distillery led with Rs 358.2 million in trades at Rs 1,323 a share, up 0.84 percent, followed by Shivam Cements with Rs 297.8 million.

Tuesday's session had its own texture. Himalayan Distillery was the most traded stock for the second day running, with Rs 229.9 million in turnover even as its price slipped 1.97 percent to Rs 1,297. Shivam Cement, Siddhartha Bank and Garima Bikas Bank filled out the turnover ranks. The losers' list was dominated by hydropower and microfinance names: Dhaulagiri Laghubitta fell 4.23 percent, Apollo Hydropower 4.14 percent, Shikhar Power 4.08 percent. Eleven of the thirteen sector indices closed lower, and the trading sector, Monday's lone gainer, became Tuesday's worst performer with a 1.87 percent drop. Leadership was rotating rather than collapsing.

Wednesday showed the other side of the tape. Ghalemdi Hydro was the most traded stock of the day, with Rs 384.8 million changing hands, and its price rose 7.31 percent to Rs 271.50. Peoples Hydropower followed with Rs 228.3 million in turnover. Sindhu Development Bank jumped 14.37 percent to Rs 444.90, and Sayapatri Hydropower gained 8.41 percent to Rs 580, its second strong session after a 6.79 percent rise on Tuesday. Of the thirteen sector indices, ten fell and three rose: trading added 1.18 percent, hydropower 0.01 percent, and the mutual fund index 0.25 percent. Every declining sector lost less than 0.6 percent. Selective buying did not stop. It just stopped being enough to lift the index.

The week also had a crowded regulatory calendar, which deserves mention as context rather than cause. SEBON's broker-strengthening policy, which sorts brokers into four classes and puts margin trading, short selling, intraday trading and market making behind separate approvals, landed on September 30. The House Finance Committee spent the same stretch of days on capital-market matters, days after its members heard competing diagnoses of the market's problems. None of this is a clean explanation for a 1.2 percent drift, and that is the point. Markets often sag under regulatory uncertainty while participants wait to see which rules actually bite. That is a description, not a diagnosis.

What the three days do not show matters as much as what they do. There was no volume climax, no single-session washout, no sector in free fall. A 1.2 percent slide across three sessions is ordinary market weather, the kind of move that vanishes inside a single strong week. Single-week readings are noisy by nature: they describe what happened, and history is careful about what it promises next. Three sessions do not make a trend, and a quiet drift down is not the same thing as a market losing confidence.

So the slide ends the week as it began: unexplained, orderly, and small. Either this was routine profit-taking after the climb to 2,629.80, or the market was quietly repricing a week in which its regulators redrew parts of the rulebook. The tape, for now, refuses to say which. That unanswered question is the honest place to leave it.
