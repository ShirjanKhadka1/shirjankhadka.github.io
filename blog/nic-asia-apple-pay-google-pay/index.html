NIC Asia can now take Apple Pay. The open question is whose customers come through.

NIC Asia Bank says merchants on its CyberSource gateway can now accept Apple Pay and Google Pay from Visa and Mastercard holders worldwide, a claimed first for Nepal. The announcement targets hotels, travel firms and exporters; whether merchants switch it on will decide if this is a headline or a habit.

By Shirjan Khadka · Founder, Nepse Decode, Kathmandu · October 3, 2026 · 5 min read
Facebook X LinkedIn WhatsApp Viber

NIC Asia Bank (NEPSE: NICA) says Nepali businesses can now accept Apple Pay and Google Pay, and it calls the service a first for Nepal. The announcement, published on the bank’s official news page on October 2, 2026, says merchants on its CyberSource e-commerce payment gateway can receive payments from Apple Pay and Google Pay users worldwide through Visa and Mastercard cards.

The mechanics are the ones every international traveler already knows. Customers tokenize their card details on their Apple or Android devices and pay through the merchant’s e-commerce platform, without the physical card ever coming out. On the merchant side, businesses already onboarded to the bank’s CyberSource gateway, and those that join it, can accept those payments through their own online storefronts.

The bank names its targets plainly: hotels, tours and travels companies, handicraft sellers, airlines, e-commerce businesses, IT firms, exporters, and any business that takes money from international customers. The common thread is the foreign customer who arrives with a phone full of Apple Pay or Google Pay and no patience for a checkout page that only understands domestic payment methods.

The foreign-currency logic behind the announcement

The bank frames the service as more than convenience. The stated aim is a more familiar payment experience for international customers, expected to support the expansion of cross-border trade and the potential for higher foreign-currency earnings. The announcement adds that the service will contribute to tourism promotion and to Nepal’s economy and cross-border payments.

That framing matters. A gateway that lets a tourist pay a Pokhara hotel with Apple Pay is, in the bank’s telling, not just a smoother checkout but a small piece of the country’s foreign-exchange plumbing. For a bank, merchant acquiring on international cards is fee income from transactions that would otherwise route through foreign payment processors or never happen at all.

Why firsts like this are tested by merchants, not press releases

The “first in Nepal” claim is the bank’s, and it describes the service rather than the technology: global wallets paying Nepali merchants through a local bank’s e-commerce gateway. Claims like this have a known lifecycle. The infrastructure arrives first; the value arrives later, when hotels, travel platforms and exporters wire it into their checkouts and travelers actually see the option. Until then, the feature exists mostly on the announcement page.

The test is straightforward to state and hard to fake: how many merchants onboard, and whether the share of international payments settling through the gateway actually grows. Neither is the kind of number that appears in a launch announcement.

What the announcement does not say

The October 2 notice is silent on the commercial terms that would matter to a merchant deciding whether to switch this on: pricing, fees, settlement timelines, and any target for how many businesses the bank expects to onboard. It does not say how long integration takes for a business joining the CyberSource gateway now. The notice is signed by Jayendra Bahadur Rawal, the bank’s assistant chief executive and chief information officer.

Until merchants switch it on and travelers check out with it, the service is a door the bank has opened. Who walks through is the story to watch.
