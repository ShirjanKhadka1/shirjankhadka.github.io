BPCL at Rs 712.30: the hydropower pioneer priced for a 601 MW cascade it only partly owns

Butwal Power Company has generated electricity in Nepal since 1966, pays a steady cash dividend, and holds just 19.40% of the 135 MW project now under construction in Manang. At Rs 712.30, the market is paying 72.4 times trailing earnings for that mix. This analysis separates what is operating, what is on hold, and what is still only a plan.

By Nepse Decode Research Desk · Kathmandu · October 31, 2026 · 25 min read
Facebook X LinkedIn WhatsApp Viber

Butwal Power Company (BPC) is the oldest private hydropower name on the Nepal Stock Exchange, and one of the hardest to value honestly. Its own operating plants are small — 21.9 MW directly owned, plus 34.2 MW through subsidiaries and associates. Its pipeline is enormous — a 601.2 MW cascade in Manang, a 37.6 MW project on hold in the east, and more. And its ownership of that pipeline is uneven: all of the small plants, just over half of the stalled project, and under a fifth of the flagship under construction. Any valuation that prices the whole cascade as if it were BPC's alone is wrong before it starts. This article prices each piece separately, shows every calculation, and says plainly where the record runs out.

02. Valuation view: three lenses, no single number

A company like BPC cannot be reduced to one multiple. Its dividend is real but small, its book value is solid but unaudited at the latest quarter, and its earnings are depressed relative to a price that also reflects a pipeline. Read the three lenses together. A 0.84% yield says the market is not paying for income: a buyer at Rs 712.30 receives Rs 6.00 a share for holding through the FY2081/82 dividend, and the company has paid cash only — 5%, then 6% — since its last bonus issue. A price-to-book of 3.38 times says the market values each rupee of book value at more than three rupees, which is a vote for the pipeline rather than for the existing asset base. And a trailing PE of 72.4 times says current earnings cover very little of the price: at Rs 9.84 of trailing earnings per share, the price implies either much higher future earnings or a long wait. None of these is a recommendation. Together they frame the only honest question about BPCL: how much of the 601.2 MW cascade, and how soon, does the price already assume?

One deliberate omission: no peer-median valuation is presented. A peer comparison done properly needs fresh closes for genuinely comparable, recently traded hydropower peers, a stated trimmed-median rule, and a full peer table so every median recomputes — and it must not mix adjusted and unadjusted prices. That work was not completed to that standard for this article, so no peer figure is printed rather than a thin one. Readers who want a peer view should run it from the same dated closes, not from a single vendor multiple.

A second deliberate omission: no star rating and no intrinsic value. Those are another firm's proprietary research, and this article does not reproduce them. The numbers above are arithmetic on published figures; the judgement about what they mean is left to the reader.

03. Dividends: cash only, and the arithmetic that matters

BPC's recent dividend record is short and consistent. The last bonus issue was 5% in FY2078/79, alongside a 7.5% cash dividend approved at the 30th annual general meeting in January 2023. Since then the company has paid cash only: 5% for FY2080/81 and 6% for FY2081/82. The FY2081/82 dividend was decided by the board on 3 Poush, approved at the 33rd AGM on Monday 28 Poush 2082 (12 January 2026) at Karki Banquet, Babarmahal, Kathmandu, with book closure from 15 to 28 Poush and eligibility for shareholders registered up to 14 Poush (ICTFrame, 19 December 2025). At the 30 October close, that 6% is worth Rs 6.00 per share, or 0.84% of the price — the yield in the valuation table.

The tax note matters for Nepali holders, and it is the same rule this site has explained before: cash dividends are taxed at a flat 5% at source, so the Rs 6.00 per share is Rs 5.70 after the dividend withholding tax, before any other consideration. The yield net of that withholding is therefore about 0.80% at Rs 712.30 (computed: Rs 5.70 ÷ Rs 712.30). Income investors should use the net figure, not the headline 6%.

04. Financials: growth in the half-year, a fuller year behind it

The latest quarterly picture comes from the company's unaudited results for the second quarter of FY2082/83, as of Poush 30, 2082 (14 January 2026), reported by ICTFrame on 11 February 2026. Total revenue reached Rs 464.04 million, up 10.76% from Rs 418.97 million a year earlier, helped by increased electricity sales and tariff revisions in the Andhikhola distribution area. Net profit was Rs 225.38 million, up 16.25% year on year. Book value per share stood at Rs 208.40 and the current ratio at 3.67 times, against 1.02 times in the previous quarter.

One labelling caution on those results. ICTFrame describes the quarter's earnings per share as an "annualised EPS" of Rs 6.61, against Rs 5.69 a year earlier. But Rs 225.38 million divided by 34,090,646 shares is Rs 6.61 for the period itself — the arithmetic gives the period figure, not an annualised one, and annualising a half-year figure would double it. This article therefore quotes Rs 6.61 as the period EPS implied by the reported profit and share count, and does not use ICTFrame's "annualised" label. Readers comparing EPS across companies should check whether each source annualises, and over what period, before comparing.

05. The company: sixty years from Tinau to the Marsyangdi

Butwal Power Company was established in 1966 by Norwegian engineer Odd Hoftun to build hydropower capacity and technical skills in Nepal, and it pioneered private-sector hydropower in the country (bpc.com.np, "Our History"). Its early milestones read like a history of the sector itself: Tinau (1 MW), Andhikhola (originally 5.1 MW), Jhimruk (12 MW), Khudi (4.2 MW), and the upgraded Andhikhola (9.4 MW). Incorporated as a private company in 1965/66, it converted to a public limited company in 1993 (2049 BS). In October 2001 the Government of Nepal invited offers for its 75% stake, and on 3 January 2003 a sale and purchase agreement for those shares was signed with Interkraft Nepal (Norway) in partnership with Shangri-La Energy (bpc.com.np, "BPC Privatization"). Headquarters: Buddha Nagar, Kathmandu.

Today the business is generation and distribution as core areas, plus a holding structure: subsidiaries and associates in generation (Nyadi, Kabeli Energy), engineering and services (Hydro-Consult Engineering, Hydro Lab, BPC Services, which provides operation and maintenance to Khudi). That structure is why the project table below distinguishes what BPC owns outright from what it holds through other companies — the distinction is the whole valuation story.

06. Projects: operating, on hold, and still a plan

Operating, directly owned (21.9 MW). Andhikhola, 9.4 MW in Syangja — upgraded from 5.1 MW, commercial operation of the upgrade in April 2015, run-of-river, selling to the Nepal Electricity Authority, with distribution in the Andhikhola area whose tariff revisions helped drive the Q2 FY2082/83 revenue growth. And Jhimruk, in Pyuthan — 12 MW on the company's history page, 12.5 MW on the NEA licence list carried by Investopaper. This article uses the licence figure in totals and notes both, rather than picking one silently.

Operating through subsidiaries and associates (34.2 MW). Nyadi, 30 MW in Lamjung, operating since 10 May 2022 — built, but with a cautionary tale attached: NEA did not take all wet-season energy because its transmission line was delayed, so the project built an alternative line (myRepublica, January 2023). Khudi, 4.2 MW, with BPC Services providing operation and maintenance. Khimti-I, 60 MW, operated by Himal Power Ltd, in which BPC is a shareholder — its current stake percentage could not be verified from a named source, so Khimti-I is excluded from the operating totals above rather than guessed into them.

On hold (37.6 MW). Kabeli-A, in Panchthar/Taplejung, a peaking run-of-river project with a project development agreement with the Government of Nepal (31 January 2010, amended July 2013), a power purchase agreement with NEA (24 September 2015), and World Bank/IFC debt of USD 78.6 million channelled via HIDCL. Per the company's own project page, the access tunnel is done and about 1,000 metres of the main tunnel were excavated before the contractor's contract was terminated for lack of progress and non-compliance; construction is on hold and a recovery plan is being implemented. BPC's stake in Kabeli Energy Ltd is 55.6% (26% direct, the rest via the Gurans Energy joint venture). At the January 2023 AGM the company said preparations were being made to restart after raising local financial investment (myRepublica). Three years on, a restart — or another delay — is the clearest binary catalyst in the portfolio.

Under construction and in preparation (601.2 MW cascade). The Marsyangdi cascade in Manang is three projects: Manang Marsyangdi, 135 MW, under construction, with financial closure on 22 October 2024 — a China Exim Bank loan of CNY 1,242.5 million (USD 175.25 million) within a total project cost of USD 323 million at an 80:20 debt-to-equity ratio, EPC contractor Sinohydro Bureau 7, a 42-month build, expected generation of 766 GWh a year, and a 430-metre head (bpc.com.np press release; myRepublica; Khabarhub). BPC holds 19.40% of Manang Marsyangdi Hydropower Co. — about 26.2 MW attributable at that stake (computed: 135 × 19.40%), not 135 MW. The other two cascade projects, Lower Manang Marsyangdi (139.2 MW) and Upper Marsyangdi-2 (327 MW), are still in preparation; the company's history page still lists an older "Lower Manang Marsyangdi (100 MW)" reference, and this article uses the newer 139.2 MW cascade-page figure. ICTFrame's February 2026 report additionally lists Chino Khola (7.9 MW) as under construction and Mugu Karnali (174 MW) at feasibility stage; neither is in the verified core table above, and both are watch items only.

08. Risks and what to watch

  1. Kabeli-A execution. On hold after contractor termination; the recovery plan's progress — restart, new contractor, fresh financing — is the clearest binary catalyst in the portfolio.
  2. Cascade concentration and minority position. BPC holds only 19.40% of the 135 MW project company, and the other 466.2 MW of the cascade is not yet in construction. Value depends on its Chinese partners' execution and on Exim Bank debt terms, not only on BPC's own decisions.
  3. Offtake and transmission. Nyadi's wet-season evacuation problem in 2022–23 — NEA not taking all energy because its line was delayed — is the template risk for every new plant BPC brings online.
  4. FPO overhang. A 10-million-unit FPO mentioned in January 2025 has no follow-up on record. If revived, it dilutes holders; verify any new announcement before assuming the share count is fixed.
  5. Earnings quality. Q2 FY2082/83 growth leaned on Andhikhola distribution tariff revisions. Tariff-driven growth repeats only if tariffs hold and volumes follow — watch whether the next quarters repeat it.
  6. Price volatility. A 10.60% session on Rs 264.34 million of turnover, followed by a 2.04% fall two sessions later, is a reminder that this stock moves fast in both directions. Position size should reflect that, not the dividend alone.
  7. Legal overhang. ICTFrame's February 2026 report notes a Supreme Court case against the Department of Electricity Development over royalty issues for the 4.3 MW Andhikhola upgrading project. No outcome was located at publish time; it is flagged, not quantified.
  8. Dividend profile. Cash-only since FY2078/79, with a computed net-of-tax yield of about 0.80% at Rs 712.30. Investors buying for income should compare that with what the same capital earns elsewhere — the case for BPCL is growth optionality, not income.

What to take from it

BPCL is two companies in one listing: a small, profitable, dividend-paying operator of 56.1 MW, and a minority stake in a very large, unfinished idea. At Rs 712.30 the market charges 72.4 times trailing earnings and 3.38 times book value for the combination, and pays a 0.84% gross dividend yield while you wait. That pricing is coherent only if the cascade — or at least the 135 MW now being built, and a restarted Kabeli-A — eventually converts into earnings at a scale the current fleet cannot provide. Until then, the honest way to hold this story is as a dated snapshot: operating plants listed, pipeline staged, stakes stated, gaps admitted. The next facts that matter are a Kabeli-A restart, a Manang Marsyangdi construction milestone, an audited FY2081/82 result, and any revival of the 10-million-unit FPO. Everything else is noise around a price that already assumes a future.
