Capital spending is 20% of Nepal's budget — and last year it spent 47% of that

The budget speech promises a bigger, building Nepal. The allocation says otherwise: recurrent spending still takes nearly sixty paise of every rupee, capital barely a fifth — and the first two months of the new fiscal year already show the same shape.

By Nepse Decode Research Desk · Kathmandu · October 28, 2026 · 12 min read
Facebook X LinkedIn WhatsApp Viber

Finance Minister Dr. Swarnim Wagle presented a Rs 2,124.34 billion budget for fiscal year 2083/84 on May 29, 2026 — the largest in Nepal's history, and the one that best exposes the gap between what the budget promises and what the state can actually spend. Recurrent spending takes Rs 1,270.58 billion, capital spending Rs 431.10 billion, and financial management Rs 422.64 billion. The building part of the budget, the part that creates roads, bridges, schools and power lines, is barely a fifth of the whole. And last year's record shows it is the fifth the state cannot spend.

The financing behind the headline is worth stating plainly, because it explains why the ratio barely moves. Revenue is budgeted at Rs 1,405.31 billion and foreign grants at Rs 61.74 billion, leaving a deficit of Rs 657.29 billion to be covered by foreign loans of Rs 247.28 billion and gross domestic borrowing of Rs 410 billion. But Rs 245.89 billion of that domestic borrowing goes straight back out as principal repayment on old debt, so net domestic borrowing is only Rs 164.11 billion. The budget targets 7% growth and inflation under 6%. The growth target is a target, not a forecast — the distinction matters, and this blog covered it when the World Bank, IMF and ADB each priced Nepal differently. The budget's own summary, published by the Ministry of Finance as a Citizen Budget, is the source for the split; Nepal Register's summary of it and Alpha Capital's budget note report the same figures.

The ratio barely moved

Against last year's original budget of Rs 1,964.11 billion, the new total is up 8.2%. But the growth is uneven in exactly the wrong direction. Recurrent spending rises 7.6% to Rs 1,270.58 billion. Capital rises only 5.7% to Rs 431.10 billion — the slowest of the three headings. Financial management rises fastest, 12.6% to Rs 422.64 billion, because past borrowing keeps coming due. The shares barely shift: recurrent edges down from 60.13% to 59.81%, capital edges down from 20.77% to 20.29%. The building share of the budget did not grow. It shrank.

The "record budget" framing deserves a footnote. The new total is about 25% above the mid-year revised estimate of roughly Rs 1,688 billion, not 25% above what was originally planned. A record measured against a lowered base flatters the headline. What did not change against any base is the shape: roughly six parts recurrent, two parts capital, two parts financing.

The new year is already repeating the pattern

The first two months of FY 2083/84 are already on the books, from the Financial Comptroller General Office via Pardafas, and they show the same shape forming. By September 16, 2026, total spending stood at Rs 199.83 billion, or 9.41% of the annual budget. Recurrent spending accounted for about 9% of its Rs 1,270.58 billion allocation. Financial management had already reached 16.59%, or Rs 70.13 billion of Rs 422.64 billion. Capital spending stood at Rs 7.66 billion — 1.78% of its Rs 431.10 billion allocation. Two months in, the building fifth is again the slowest-moving part of the budget. The full first-trimester split had not been published at writing time; this article will be updated when it lands. But the direction is already visible, and last year's complete record shows where it leads.

Last year, the building fifth delivered less than half

Last year's final outturn, from the Financial Comptroller General Office via Alpha Capital's fourth-quarter newsletter, is the article's central fact. The state spent 80.55% of its total budget. Recurrent spending reached 88.44% of its allocation. Financial management reached 92.56%. Capital spending reached 46.79% — Rs 190.84 billion of Rs 407.888 billion. Less than half the building budget was spent, in a year when the recurrent and financing budgets were spent almost in full. And capital absorption got worse, not better: the previous year's capital spending had reached 63.2%. The gap widened by more than 16 points in a single year.

The year-end figure was not a slow start that caught up. It was a slow year that ended in a rush. Tracked through the year on the same bases, capital spending ran three to six times behind recurrent spending at every checkpoint, and only the final weeks narrowed the gap:

The checkpoints are each documented: the first trimester in Pardafas and Rising Nepal (capital 4.7%, the weakest first trimester in four years except 2021/22's 3.9%); the mid-year review in Ratopati, reporting the Finance Ministry's own mid-term evaluation (capital 12.12%, down from about 16% at the same point the year before); seven months in Nepal News and Rising Nepal (capital 15.62%, down from 19.42% a year earlier, while recurrent accelerated from 45.82% to 47.62%). Even with eight months gone, capital was still under a fifth, at 18.59% according to Pardafas. By Asar 24 it had reached only 40%, per Fiscal Nepal — so the final 46.79% was assembled in the last stretch of the year, the rushed year-end spending that auditors and contractors both warn about.

Why the ratio matters more than the headline

Recurrent spending and debt service are near-automatic. Salaries, allowances and interest must be paid; principal must be repaid. That is why they reach 88% and 93% without drama. Capital spending is the only discretionary part of the budget, and the only part that depends on the state's capacity to prepare projects, acquire land, clear forests, sign contracts and supervise construction. The Finance Ministry's own half-yearly review attributed the weak capital performance to inadequate prior project preparation, complications in land acquisition and forest-area use, and damage from the Gen Z movement. The contractors' federation added terminated contracts, financial constraints and labour shortages. These are capacity constraints, not allocation constraints — which is why raising the capital allocation without fixing delivery has repeatedly failed to raise capital spending.

The stakes are larger than a budget table. The government's 7% growth target sits 2.9 points above even the most optimistic forecaster — the ADB's 4.1%, against the World Bank's 3.7% and the IMF's 3.0% — and every forecaster's case for recovery runs through reconstruction spending. The disaster needs assessment puts total flood losses and damages at Rs 408.28 billion. Reconstruction is capital spending. At a 47% absorption rate, the rebuild itself becomes the bottleneck: the money is budgeted, the damage is documented, and the spending mechanism delivers less than half. Alpha Capital's budget note calls the new budget consumption-oriented and notes it prioritises finishing ongoing projects over starting new ones. Finishing is the right instinct. Last year's 47% says finishing is itself the problem.

What would change the ratio

Not a bigger capital allocation. Capital grew slowest this year and its share still fell, and last year the state left more than half its capital budget unspent. The constraint is not the size of the allocation but the pipeline behind it: projects prepared before the fiscal year starts, land and forest clearances settled before contracts are signed, and funds released early enough that the first trimester delivers more than 4.7%. The mid-year review should be treated as a reallocation moment, not a reporting moment — the point at which unspendable capital is honestly identified rather than carried to a year-end rush. Until the absorption rate moves, every budget speech will promise a building Nepal and every outturn will report a consuming one.

Nepal does not have a budget-size problem. It has a budget-spending problem — and the spending problem lives entirely in the fifth of the budget that builds things.

Frequently asked questions

What is the capital–recurrent split in FY 2083/84?

Recurrent Rs 1,270.58 billion (59.8%), capital Rs 431.10 billion (20.3%) and financial management Rs 422.64 billion (19.9%), of a Rs 2,124.34 billion total. The budget was presented on May 29, 2026.

How much of last year's capital budget was actually spent?

46.79% — Rs 190.84 billion of Rs 407.888 billion in FY 2082/83, down from 63.2% the year before. Recurrent spending reached 88.44% and financial management 92.56% in the same year.

Why is capital spending always slower than recurrent?

Recurrent spending and debt service are near-automatic: salaries, interest and repayments must be paid. Capital spending depends on project preparation, land acquisition, forest clearances, contracts and construction supervision. The Finance Ministry's half-yearly review cited inadequate prior preparation, land and forest complications, and damage from the Gen Z movement.

What sits inside “financial management”?

Debt principal and interest payments plus government investments. In FY 2083/84 it is Rs 422.64 billion, and Rs 245.89 billion of gross domestic borrowing goes straight back out as principal repayment — which is why the fastest-growing heading is largely past debt coming due.

Has the new fiscal year started any better?

Not on the latest data. In the first two months of FY 2083/84 (to September 16, 2026), capital spending was 1.78% of its allocation, against about 9% for recurrent and 16.59% for financial management. The full first-trimester split had not been published at writing time.
