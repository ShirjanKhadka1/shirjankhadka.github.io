The August floods were supposed to empty Nepal's hotels. September brought 12.1% more tourists

The late-August Bhotekoshi floods set off a wave of booking cancellations. September still delivered 88,248 international arrivals, up 12.1% from a year earlier, with Chinese visitors more than doubling.

By Shirjan Khadka · Aspiring Financial Analyst, Kathmandu · October 4, 2026 · 5 min read
Facebook X LinkedIn

In late August, the Bhotekoshi floods tore through river corridors in central Nepal, and the cancellations started almost immediately. Tour operators braced for a lost autumn. Then the September numbers arrived. According to figures released by the Nepal Tourism Board using Department of Immigration data, 88,248 international tourists entered Nepal in September 2026, 12.1% more than the 78,711 who arrived in the same month last year. The disaster that was supposed to empty the hotels did not.

The composition of the rebound is as interesting as the headline. India remained Nepal's largest source market with 17,425 arrivals, up 12% from last September. China came second with 11,212 visitors, a 114.1% surge that more than doubled last year's figure, implying roughly 5,200 Chinese arrivals in September 2025. The United States sent 8,299, Sri Lanka 7,108, and Australia 4,842. The growth was Asia-led: South Asian and wider Asian markets expanded strongly while several long-haul markets in Europe, the Americas, and Oceania softened, a regional split that is becoming a defining feature of Nepal's tourism recovery. For an industry that spent a decade courting high-spending Western trekkers, the customer base is quietly being rewritten by the two giant neighbors.

September also beat August, when 85,444 tourists arrived, and it put the first nine months of 2026 at 865,920 arrivals, up 6% from 815,273 in the same period last year. Timing matters here. The September-to-November window is Nepal's peak trekking and tourism season, accounting for nearly 40% of annual foreign arrivals. A weak September would have poisoned the whole year's numbers. A strong one does the opposite.

How did the arrivals survive the floods? The honest answer is geography plus messaging. The damage was concentrated in specific northern river corridors, while most of the country's trekking regions, cities, and heritage sites stayed open. The government and private operators ran a joint #NepalCalling campaign after the disaster precisely to make that distinction, countering the cancellations with the message that most of Nepal remained safe and accessible. The September figures suggest the message landed, at least with regional travelers who could judge the situation at shorter notice and lower cost.

There is a second, less comfortable number sitting underneath the arrivals data. In FY2025/26, Nepal received 1,158,459 tourists, barely 0.95% more than the year before, and travel income, the foreign exchange tourists actually leave behind, rose 1.5% to Rs 90.05 billion. Put that next to the country's other great inflow: remittances reached Rs 2.363 trillion in the same year, equivalent to 35.8% of GDP. Tourism's entire foreign-exchange contribution is under 4% of what migrant workers send home. In the same year, Nepalis spent Rs 209.10 billion on travel abroad, of which Rs 141.92 billion went to education. Nepal's travel account, in other words, bleeds: the country earns 90 billion from visitors and spends 209 billion sending its own people, mostly students, the other way. The education outflow alone is more than one and a half times the whole inbound tourism income. Record arrivals are good news. They do not fix that imbalance, and no arrival count will until the spending side of the ledger changes.

The caveats deserve plain stating. One month is a noisy reading, and arrivals count heads, not wallets: how much each visitor spends is a separate question the arrival figures cannot answer. Some September trips may simply be August bookings that were postponed rather than cancelled, which would flatter the month. The softness in European, American, and Oceanian markets bears watching, since long-haul visitors tend to stay longer and spend more per day than regional ones. And the flooded corridors themselves still face a long physical recovery, whatever the national arrival count says. The comparison base is at least honest: September 2025's 78,711 was a normal month in a year that grew only 0.95%, so the 12.1% gain is real growth, not a rebound off a depressed base.

The arithmetic for the rest of the year now looks friendly. With 865,920 arrivals in the first nine months, Nepal needs about 293,000 more in the October-to-December quarter to beat FY2025/26's full-year total of 1,158,459. The same quarter last year delivered 343,186. In other words, the bar for a record year is lower than last year's actual performance, and September's momentum is pointed the right way. For the listed hotel operators on NEPSE, whose revenues live and die on occupancy, that is the number that matters more than any single month's arrival count.

Still, the direction of the surprise matters. When the floods hit, the industry's working assumption was a damaged peak season. September answered with the strongest start to autumn in recent memory, led by the two giant neighbors. The real test is October and November, the heart of the season. If the momentum holds through those two months, 2026 will be remembered as the year Nepal's tourism outran its worst disaster in years. If it fades, September will look like a postponement, not a recovery. Either way, the next data point is only weeks away.
