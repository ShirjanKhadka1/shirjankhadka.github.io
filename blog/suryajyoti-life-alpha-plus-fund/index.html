SuryaJyoti Life is now a fund manager too. Where does policyholder money sit now?

The insurer has received SEBON registration for the Rs 50 crore Alpha Plus SuryaJyoti Fund, to be managed by Alpha Plus Ventures. It is a first for Nepal’s insurance industry, and it moves insurer money one step further from the policyholder.

By Shirjan Khadka · Founder, Nepse Decode, Kathmandu · October 3, 2026 · 5 min read
Facebook X LinkedIn WhatsApp Viber

On October 2, 2026, SuryaJyoti Life Insurance Company Limited (NEPSE: SJLIC) announced on the News & Events page of its official website that it has launched the “Alpha Plus SuryaJyoti Fund”, a Rs 50 crore investment vehicle. The company says it has received the fund-registration and issuance-registration certificate from the Securities Board of Nepal, and that Alpha Plus Ventures Limited will handle overall management and investment implementation of the fund.

The announcement is short on mechanics and long on firsts. SuryaJyoti Life describes the launch as the first time in Nepal’s insurance industry that a life insurer is operating its own named special fund. The stated purpose is to widen the company’s investment portfolio and strengthen its long-term financial strategy, while creating long-term, planned investment opportunities for policyholders, agents and stakeholders.

A first, because insurers are usually told where their money may go

The “first” claim matters because of how closely insurer balance sheets are watched. Life insurers collect premiums today against promises that mature years from now, and regulators keep their investment universe deliberately narrow for exactly that reason: policyholder money is meant to be boring. A named special fund, run through a licensed fund manager, is a different animal from the traditional portfolio of government securities, fixed deposits and listed shares. It gives the insurer a dedicated vehicle with its own mandate, and it gives Alpha Plus Ventures discretion within an approved investment policy. That is precisely why the structure raises the question in the headline: the money now travels one step further from the policyholder who paid it in.

Nothing in the October 2 announcement suggests anything improper about that. SEBON has registered the fund, and registration is the regulator’s sign-off on the structure. But the distance between a premium payment and a hydropower equity stake is now longer, with a fund manager and an investment committee in between, and each link in that chain is a place where incentives, fees and reporting standards will matter.

What the Rs 50 crore will be allowed to chase

The company’s announcement lists a wide mandate: production and processing, energy and hydropower, financial and investment services, e-commerce and IT, tourism and entertainment, health and wellness, real estate, infrastructure development, agriculture, hotel and restaurant, mining and processing, along with other private-sector opportunities not prohibited by law, subject to the investment committee and the approved investment policy. That is less a sector bet than a map of the Nepali economy’s growth stories: hydropower, tourism, agriculture and real estate are the sectors Nepali investors already know best.

Breadth is the point and the risk. A fund that can go anywhere can also dilute into everything. The discipline will have to come from the investment policy and the committee that approves each allocation, neither of which the announcement details.

Why an insurer would want its own fund

SuryaJyoti Life is itself a product of consolidation: the merger of Surya Life and Jyoti Life Insurance, Nepal’s first life-insurance merger, with joint operations from December 22, 2022. The company describes itself as one of Nepal’s largest life insurers by capital base, investment, policyholders and agents. A dedicated Rs 50 crore fund is a logical next step for an institution of that scale: it formalizes investment activity that would otherwise sit inside the general portfolio, gives it a name the market can track, and signals ambition to manage investments more actively.

The launch also fits a larger pattern. Nepali financial institutions are hunting for yield and relevance beyond their core business: banks built subsidiaries, and now an insurer has a named fund. If the Alpha Plus SuryaJyoti Fund performs, other insurers will face an obvious question from their own boards about why they do not have one.

What the announcement does not say

The announcement leaves the questions an investor would actually ask unanswered. How will returns from the fund flow back, to the company, to policyholders, to agents? What is the fund’s tenure, its deployment timeline, its reporting standard? How much discretion does Alpha Plus Ventures have, and who watches the watchers? None of that is in the October 2 announcement.

Until the fund’s documents are public, the Alpha Plus SuryaJyoti Fund is a registered vehicle with a wide mandate and a first-of-its-kind label. Whether it becomes a genuine new channel for insurer capital or a press-release fund will be decided by the disclosures that have not been made yet.
