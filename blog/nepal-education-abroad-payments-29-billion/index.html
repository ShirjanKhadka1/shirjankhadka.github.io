Rs 29.55 billion left Nepal for foreign classrooms in two months — nearly twice what tourists brought in

In the first two months of fiscal year 2026/27, Nepali students sent Rs 29.55 billion abroad for education. Foreign visitors spent Rs 14.94 billion inside Nepal in the same period. The country's largest invisible import is not a holiday or a luxury good. It is a seat in a foreign classroom.

By Nepse Decode Research Desk · Kathmandu · October 30, 2026 · 14 min read
Facebook X LinkedIn WhatsApp Viber

Every debate about Nepal's economy eventually comes back to the same two flows: money earned abroad and sent home, and money spent at home on things made abroad. The first flow is famous. The second usually means goods — fuel, fertiliser, vehicles — counted in the trade deficit. But Nepal Rastra Bank's report for the first two months of fiscal year 2026/27, covering mid-July to mid-September 2026, puts a number on a flow that belongs in neither familiar box: Rs 29.55 billion that left Nepal to pay for education abroad. It is the single largest item inside the country's travel payments, it is nearly twice what foreign visitors spent in Nepal over the same two months, and it has been growing for years. If you want to understand where Nepal's foreign exchange actually goes, start here — not at the airport duty-free shop, but at the university admissions office.

Signal summary

  • Rs 29.55 billion went abroad for education in the first two months of FY2026/27 (mid-July to mid-September 2026), out of Rs 41.10 billion in total travel payments — 71.9% of everything Nepali travellers spent abroad on travel.
  • Foreign visitors spent Rs 14.94 billion in Nepal in the same two months. Education payments abroad were nearly twice that (1.98 times, computed from the two reported figures).
  • The services deficit still narrowed — to Rs 22.04 billion from Rs 30.88 billion a year earlier — because travel income rose 28.6% while travel payments fell 13.1%.
  • The two months are not a blip. In FY2025/26, the Ministry of Finance puts education abroad at Rs 141 billion and other foreign travel at Rs 67.18 billion; over FY2020/21–2025/26, 548,717 Nepali students obtained No Objection Certificates to study abroad and Rs 556.68 billion flowed out for education.
  • The central bank has already responded — since April 2026, repeat tuition and living-expense payments must go through the same bank as the first payment, with a No Objection Certificate and enrolment proof. That is a control on the flow, not a reduction of the demand behind it.

01. The number

Nepal Rastra Bank's Current Macroeconomic and Financial Situation of Nepal, based on data to mid-September 2026 and released on October 15, 2026, reports travel payments of Rs 41.10 billion for the first two months of FY2026/27, down 13.1% from the same period a year earlier. Education accounts for Rs 29.55 billion of that total. The remainder — holidays, business travel, medical trips and everything else Nepali travellers pay for abroad — is Rs 11.55 billion, computed by subtracting education from the reported total. In other words, for every rupee Nepali travellers spent abroad on travel in those two months, about 72 paisa paid for study.

Set that against the other side of the travel ledger. Travel income — what foreign visitors spent inside Nepal — rose 28.6% to Rs 14.94 billion. Tourism, the industry Nepal promotes, markets and builds airports for, earned roughly half what its own students paid to universities abroad. The net services position still improved: the services deficit narrowed to Rs 22.04 billion from Rs 30.88 billion a year earlier, because income rose and total payments fell at the same time. But the improvement came despite the education outflow, not because it shrank — and the outflow remains the dominant item in the account.

02. The longer arc: this did not start in July

Two months can mislead — and this pair of months, Shrawan and Bhadra, straddles the main departure season ahead of northern-hemisphere autumn intakes, so the figures must not be annualised. The honest check is the annual record, and it tells the same story at a larger scale. For FY2025/26, the Ministry of Finance reports Rs 141 billion spent on overseas education and Rs 67.18 billion on foreign travel — Rs 208.18 billion combined, reported as roughly Rs 209 billion. In the first eight months of that same fiscal year, to mid-March 2026, education payments alone had already reached nearly Rs 97 billion, according to Nepal Rastra Bank figures reported by myRepublica. The full-year figure is therefore not a rounding-up of one strong quarter; it is the sum of a steady, year-long flow.

The pipeline behind the payments is measured in certificates. Between FY2020/21 and FY2025/26, 548,717 Nepali students obtained No Objection Certificates (NOCs) from the Ministry of Education to study in 83 countries, and Rs 556.68 billion flowed out for education over the same period, according to the Ministry of Finance data reported by myRepublica. An NOC is the government's formal no-objection to a named student studying at a named institution, and it is normally required before tuition money can be sent abroad through a bank — so the certificate count is the closest thing Nepal has to a census of intended departures. The most popular destinations reported for this flow are Australia, Canada, the United States, the United Kingdom and Japan, with South Korea and some European countries emerging. The previous fiscal year anchors the trend: in FY2024/25, Nepal Rastra Bank's annual data, via Nepal News, put education payments at Rs 138.48 billion against travel income of Rs 88.66 billion for the whole year — the same gap, in the same direction, before this year's two months even began.

03. Why it matters for the external account

Nepal's external position looks comfortable in the same report: the current account surplus widened to Rs 169.04 billion in the first two months, from Rs 130.97 billion a year earlier, and foreign exchange reserves reached USD 26.48 billion, covering 17.8 months of goods and services imports. The comfort, however, is imported. Remittance inflows were Rs 436.36 billion in the same two months — education payments abroad were 6.8% of that inflow, computed from the two reported figures. In plain terms, roughly one rupee in every fifteen sent home by workers abroad went straight back out to pay for a relative's foreign education. That is not a criticism of the families spending it: for a household, a degree abroad is an investment, not consumption. But for the economy, it means the remittance engine and the education outflow are two sides of one system — the country exports labour, imports the wages, and then exports a large share of the next generation's schooling.

The policy response so far has been administrative, not structural. Nepal Rastra Bank tightened the foreign-currency facility in April 2026: students sending tuition and living expenses for the second time onward must now use the same bank as their first payment and produce a No Objection Certificate and enrolment proof, to prevent duplicate payments. That plugs a leak in the plumbing. It does not answer the question the numbers keep asking: why do more than a hundred thousand young Nepali students a year seek a certificate to leave, when the country has spent decades building its own universities and colleges? The data in this article cannot answer that — it counts payments, not motives — and any article claiming a single reason would be guessing. What the data does establish is the price of not answering it: a structural, six-figure annual outflow that tourism, Nepal's flagship invisible export, does not come close to matching.

04. What to watch instead of the headline surplus

The current-account surplus will dominate the headlines from this report, and it deserves to — a surplus of Rs 169.04 billion in two months is real strength. But the education line is the better early-warning indicator, for three reasons. First, it is structural where the surplus is partly cyclical: remittances and reserves fluctuate with labour markets abroad, while enrolment pipelines, once built, persist for the length of a degree. Second, it tests the domestic system: if Nepali institutions close the quality and recognition gap that drives students abroad, the line should bend — slowly, over intakes, not quarters. Third, it is where the return question lives: a degree bought abroad only becomes a national asset if the graduate's skills come back, whether physically or through income, enterprise or teaching. The payments data records the departure. Nothing in the same report records the return.

Three releases will tell us whether this story is accelerating or stabilising: Nepal Rastra Bank's three-month report, which will show whether the education share of travel payments stays near 72% once the departure season passes; the Ministry of Education's NOC issuance for FY2026/27, which will show whether the pipeline is still widening; and any further Nepal Rastra Bank rule change on study-abroad payments, which would signal that the central bank sees the flow as a stability question rather than an administrative one. Until then, the honest summary is this: Nepal does not have a tourism-earnings problem. It has an education-payments fact — Rs 29.55 billion in two months, nearly twice what its visitors spent — and the fact is getting bigger faster than the industry meant to balance it.

What to take from it

Do not read the education outflow as money wasted, and do not read the current-account surplus as money secured. Both readings miss the mechanism. Nepal's households are making a rational private investment — a foreign degree — inside a system where the public alternative has not yet earned their confidence at the same scale. The result is an economy that finances its external surplus with workers' wages and spends a growing share of it on other countries' classrooms. That bargain can continue for years; the reserves say so. But every year it continues, the question gets sharper, not softer: what would it take for the next hundred thousand students to choose to stay? The answer will not be found in the services account. It will be found in the institutions those students are leaving behind.

Frequently asked questions

How much did Nepali students pay for education abroad in the first two months of FY2026/27?

Rs 29.55 billion, according to Nepal Rastra Bank's Current Macroeconomic and Financial Situation report to mid-September 2026. That is 71.9% of the Rs 41.10 billion in total travel payments in the same period (computed from the two reported figures).

How does that compare with what tourists spent in Nepal?

Foreign visitors spent Rs 14.94 billion in Nepal in the same two months (travel income, up 28.6% year-on-year). Education payments abroad were nearly twice that amount — 1.98 times, computed from the two reported figures.

Is this a new phenomenon, or a long-running trend?

Long-running. In FY2025/26, the Ministry of Finance reports Rs 141 billion spent on overseas education and Rs 67.18 billion on foreign travel. Over FY2020/21–2025/26, 548,717 Nepali students obtained No Objection Certificates to study in 83 countries and Rs 556.68 billion flowed out for education. In FY2024/25, Nepal Rastra Bank's annual data, via Nepal News, put education payments at Rs 138.48 billion against travel income of Rs 88.66 billion.

Can the two-month figure be multiplied by six to get the annual total?

No. Shrawan and Bhadra straddle the main departure season ahead of northern-hemisphere autumn intakes, so these two months are seasonally heavy. The annual figures quoted in this article come from full-year releases, not from annualising the two-month data.

What is a No Objection Certificate (NOC)?

An NOC is a letter from Nepal's Ministry of Education, Science and Technology stating it has no objection to a named student studying at a named foreign institution. It is normally required before tuition money can be sent abroad through a bank. Since April 2026, Nepal Rastra Bank requires repeat tuition and living-expense payments to go through the same bank as the first payment, with the NOC and enrolment proof, to prevent duplicate payments.

Does the education outflow threaten Nepal's foreign exchange reserves?

Not on the current numbers: reserves reached USD 26.48 billion by mid-September 2026, covering 17.8 months of goods and services imports, and the current account was in surplus at Rs 169.04 billion for the two months. The education outflow matters as a structural trend — a persistent, growing claim on foreign exchange — not as an immediate reserves threat.
