ADB cut Nepal's growth forecast to 4.1%. The inflation number matters more

Floods explain the downgrade. But 5.96% inflation in Shrawan, already above the central bank's 5.5% target, is not a forecast. It is happening now.

By Shirjan Khadka · Founder, Nepse Decode, Kathmandu · September 26, 2026 · Updated September 28, 2026 · 6 min read
Facebook X LinkedIn WhatsApp Viber

This week, the Asian Development Bank handed Nepal's economy a mixed report card. In its September 2026 Asian Development Outlook, the bank cut its growth forecast for FY2026/27 to 4.1%, down from the 4.5% it projected just two months earlier, and raised its inflation forecast to 5.3% from 5.0%. Slower growth and higher prices is the combination no policymaker wants to read about. But the forecast is only half the story. The other half is already in the shops.

Start with the asymmetry, because it is the most interesting part. The same report that downgraded next year upgraded last year: FY2025/26 growth is now estimated at 3.9%, up from the 3.3% projected in July. Private investment surged 39.6% after contracting 8.1% the year before, and private consumption, the largest component of demand, grew 3.2%, supported by remittances and lower interest rates. Weak execution of the capital budget continued to hold back public investment, but the private side surprised to the upside. The downgrade is entirely about what comes next, not what just happened.

What comes next, per the ADB, runs through the monsoon floods. "The tragic loss of life is the most devastating consequence of the floods," the ADB's country director for Nepal, Arnaud Cauchois, said, "but the disaster will also have severe and far-reaching impacts on livelihoods, critical infrastructure, and Nepal's wider economy." The economic channels are specific. Damage to hydropower facilities from the Bhotekoshi flood threatens one of Nepal's most promising growth engines, electricity exports; a dam down for repair sells no power across the border. Damaged road links impede trade flows and tourism. Disruptions to farming and local economic activity are expected to weigh on industry and services in the first quarter of the fiscal year. Reconstruction spending should partly offset the losses by restoring demand, but it also pressures the budget. Destruction now and rebuilding later is a net negative for a single fiscal year's arithmetic, even if rebuilding creates real jobs.

The floods are only half of the inflation story. The ADB also points to higher global fuel prices driven by the ongoing conflict in the Middle East. The domestic data already shows it biting: crude petroleum prices averaged $92.02 per barrel in the first month of the fiscal year, up 36.7% from $67.30 a year earlier, according to Nepal Rastra Bank. That flows straight into transport costs, up 13.17% year-on-year, which then ripple through every supply chain that moves goods by road, which is to say all of them. Reconstruction itself adds demand for construction materials, transport, and labor on top of ample domestic liquidity. Rebuilding is necessary; it is also, mechanically, inflationary.

And here is the part that should catch every reader's attention: inflation isn't a forecast risk anymore. It is the current reality. Consumer price inflation hit 5.96% in Shrawan, the first month of the fiscal year, the highest in roughly 18 months and already above the central bank's 5.5% annual target. The pain is concentrated where households feel it most: food and beverage prices rose 6.77% year-on-year, led by ghee and edible oils at 15.62%, fruits at 15.39%, fish and meat at 8.65%, and vegetables at 6.90%. Non-food items and services rose 5.52%. It is geographically uneven too: Madhesh Province felt 6.94% inflation versus 4.81% in the Kathmandu Valley, and rural areas 6.18% versus 5.88% in urban centers. One caveat that matters: Shrawan is a single month, and early-fiscal-year readings can be noisy. But the ADB's own 5.3% forecast for the full year suggests this isn't just noise, and with flood damage constraining food supply further, the direction of pressure is clear.

Which brings us to the paradox. While growth forecasts fall and prices rise, Nepal's external position has never looked stronger. Foreign exchange reserves hit a record Rs 3.946 trillion (US$25.84 billion) by mid-August, enough to cover 18.8 months of imports. The current account recorded a Rs 94.59 billion surplus; the balance of payments a Rs 90.34 billion surplus. The problem is what the money doesn't do once it arrives. In the first month of the fiscal year, the government collected Rs 92.23 billion in revenue but spent only Rs 41.89 billion, and capital expenditure was a meager Rs 1.33 billion. Meanwhile, Rs 361.81 billion sat idle in government accounts at the central bank. That is not a liquidity problem. That is an execution problem, and it is the crux of Nepal's current economic story: a country that can finance 18 months of imports cannot spend its own development budget.

Two things worth keeping in mind about the ADB's numbers themselves. First, forecasts move in both directions: this same institution revised up its estimate of last year's growth by 0.6 percentage points in the same report that downgraded next year's. A downgrade after an upgrade isn't hypocrisy; it is how new information works. Second, these projections rest on assumptions, about the Middle East conflict, about monsoon damage, about reconstruction pace, that can and will shift. Treat the 4.1% as the ADB's best current reading of the balance of forces, not as a prediction carved in stone.

It is also worth noting the gap between ambition and outlook. The government's own target was 7% growth with inflation capped at 6%, and the central bank built its monetary policy around 5.5% inflation. The ADB's 4.1% and 5.3% now sit below the growth target and near the edge of the inflation comfort zone. Whether policy can close that gap, through faster capital spending, honest flood recovery, and reconstruction that actually reaches the affected districts, is the real story to watch this fiscal year. The forecast tells you where the bank thinks Nepal is headed. The 5.96% in the market tells you where Nepal already is.
