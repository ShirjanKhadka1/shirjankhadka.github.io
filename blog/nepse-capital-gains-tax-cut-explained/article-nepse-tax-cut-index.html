Nepal's Capital Gains Tax U-Turn: The NEPSE Tax Cut, Explained

By Shirjan Khadka September 28, 2026 6 min read

Photo: Clemensmarabu / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)

In the first two months of fiscal year 2083/84, Nepal's government raised capital gains tax on share trading — and then watched the revenue it was supposed to raise collapse by 54%. In mid-September, the Cabinet backed down: the tax on NEPSE share sales was cut to 3.5% for long-term holdings and 5% for short-term holdings, roughly halving the budget's rates. Here is what changed, who pays what, and what the episode reveals about how tax policy interacts with market behavior.

The Numbers: 10% and 7.5%, Then the Fall

The FY 2083/84 budget had raised capital gains tax on the sale of shares of listed companies for natural persons to 7.5% for shares held for more than one year and 10% for holdings of one year or less. Rather than boosting state coffers, the hike landed on an already fragile market — and the data is brutal.

According to CDS and Clearing Limited (CDSC) figures reported by financial media, the government collected just Rs 1.25 billion in share-transaction profit tax through Shrawan and Bhadra of the current fiscal year, down from Rs 2.75 billion in the same period last year — a 54.42% decline. Daily turnover shrank, investor morale sagged, and the NEPSE index slid toward the 2,647 area. The tax hike, in other words, shrank the tax base it was meant to harvest.

A 2.5-percentage-point tax increase that cuts revenue by more than half is a textbook Laffer-curve moment: push the rate past the point investors can bear, and the activity you are taxing simply disappears.

The Response: A 21-Point Rescue Plan

The government answered with the Capital Market Strengthening and Revival Action Plan, 2083 — a 21-point package from the Ministry of Finance aimed at reviving the capital market. The tax cut was its centerpiece, but the plan went wider:

  • Capital gains tax cut — the headline reduction for individual investors (details below).
  • Loss adjustment — investors would be able to offset losses from listed-securities sales against gains within the same income year, with tax applied only to the net gain calculated through the trading and settlement system.
  • Final-levy treatment — capital gains computed through the trading and settlement system to be treated as a final levy on net gains, simplifying settlement.
  • Deeper secondary bond market — measures for a more active market in Treasury bills and development bonds.
  • Institutional money mobilized — pension, social security, insurance, and other institutional funds to get policy and legal facilitation to move out of bank deposits and into securities markets.
  • CDS and Clearing reform — a study on institutional and structural reform to be completed by Falgun-end 2083.
  • Private equity and venture capital framework — clearer, risk-based rules for fundraising, investment, repatriation, and exit.
  • 45-day minimum holding for banks — NRB to require banks and financial institutions to hold securities-market investments for at least 45 days, curbing speculation.
  • IPO rules revised — SEBON to issue general eligibility guidelines immediately, with sector-specific criteria and price-discovery mechanisms for hydropower, manufacturing, hotels and tourism, agriculture, and pharmaceuticals.

The plan also cited the economic slowdown and the August 26 Bhotekoshi River floods — which damaged hydropower projects operated by listed companies — as forces adding pressure to the market.

The New Rates: 3.5% and 5%

Acting on the plan, a mid-September Cabinet meeting approved the cut. As reported by Nepali financial media:

  • Held more than 365 days: tax on gains cut from 7.5% to 3.5% — the long-term investor rate.
  • Held 365 days or less: tax on gains cut from 10% to 5% — the short-term rate.

A note on precision: the Ministry's earlier action-plan draft had proposed 3.75% for holdings above 365 days, and at least one financial outlet reported implementation at that figure when trading resumed on September 22. The Cabinet-approved figure reported by multiple Nepali outlets is 3.5%; the Gazette publication will settle the number in law. Either way, the direction is the same — roughly halving the budget's rates.

One important procedural detail: the new rates apply after publication in the Nepal Gazette, followed by system updates from the Inland Revenue Department and CDSC. Investors should confirm the live rate in their broker's settlement notes before transacting.

What It Means in Rupees

A concrete example makes the cut tangible. Suppose you earn a Rs 1 lakh capital gain selling listed shares:

  • Long-term (held > 365 days): tax falls from Rs 7,500 to Rs 3,500 — Rs 4,000 back in your pocket.
  • Short-term (held ≤ 365 days): tax falls from Rs 10,000 to Rs 5,000 — Rs 5,000 back in your pocket.

Scale that across a portfolio, and the cut is the equivalent of a meaningful yield improvement — without any change in market prices. The deliberate gap between the two rates is the policy message: the government wants you to hold longer. Combined with the proposed loss-netting provision, the structure rewards patient, realized-gains-based investing over high-frequency churn.

How the Market Reacted

Markets voted with their orders. After the reform plan was unveiled, the NEPSE surged 48.57 points (1.87%) to 2,633.62, with all 13 sectoral indices ending higher and turnover nearly doubling to Rs 8.29 billion from Rs 4.68 billion the previous session — the highest in roughly two months. When trading resumed after the September 21 ransomware shutdown, the index added 7.06 points to close at 2,654.28, with turnover of Rs 7.05 billion, as the revised lower tax rates took effect in settlement.

The rally is real, but keep it in proportion: sentiment lifted by a tax cut is not the same as an earnings recovery. The Bhotekoshi flood damage to listed hydropower assets, weak capital spending, and the market's broader liquidity dynamics have not changed. A tax cut removes a headwind; it does not manufacture a tailwind.

The Bigger Lesson: Tax Policy Is Market Policy

The episode carries a lesson larger than two rate cuts. In a frontier market like Nepal's, where participation is thin and turnover is concentrated, transaction taxes are participation taxes. Raise them and you don't just raise revenue per trade — you reduce the number of trades, shrink liquidity, widen spreads, and ultimately collect less than before. The 54% revenue collapse is the receipt.

The government's 21-point package reads as an acknowledgment of that logic: lower friction, mobilize institutional capital, deepen the bond market, modernize brokers, and let the settlement system compute a final, simple tax. For investors, the practical takeaway is modest but useful — understand the 365-day boundary, plan holding periods with it in mind, keep settlement records clean for the proposed loss-netting, and watch the Gazette for the final text. Policy can change fast; the arithmetic of your own portfolio should change faster.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the capital gains tax rate on NEPSE share sales after the 2026 cut?
Under the Cabinet decision reported by Nepali financial media in September 2026, resident individuals pay 3.5% advance tax on gains from shares held for more than 365 days, and 5% on gains from shares held for 365 days or less. The new rates take effect after publication in the Nepal Gazette.
What were the capital gains tax rates on Nepali shares before the cut?
The FY 2083/84 budget had raised the rates to 7.5% for shares held for more than one year and 10% for holdings of one year or less, for natural persons selling shares of listed companies. The September 2026 Cabinet decision halved both rates to 3.5% and 5% respectively.
Why did Nepal reverse the capital gains tax hike on shares?
According to CDSC data, the higher tax burden coincided with a 54.42% collapse in capital gains tax revenue during the first two months of FY 2083/84 — Rs 1.25 billion collected versus Rs 2.75 billion a year earlier — as daily turnover fell and investor participation shrank. The government concluded the hike was counterproductive and reversed it through the Capital Market Strengthening and Revival Action Plan, 2083.
How does the 365-day holding rule work for NEPSE capital gains tax?
The lower 3.5% rate applies only to shares held for more than 365 days; shares sold after 365 days or less attract the 5% rate. The reform plan also proposes allowing investors to offset losses on listed securities against gains within the same income year, with tax applied to the resulting net gain through the trading and settlement system.
Is capital gains tax on Nepali shares a final tax?
The tax collected through the trading and settlement system is an advance tax deducted at source. The Capital Market Strengthening and Revival Action Plan proposes treating capital gains calculated through the trading and settlement system as a final levy on net gains, which would simplify settlement for investors.
