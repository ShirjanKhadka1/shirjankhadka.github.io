Nepal raised the share tax, then watched the revenue collapse 54%

The budget took capital gains tax to 7.5% and 10%. Two months later the Cabinet halved it. What happened in between is a lesson in taxing a frontier market.

By Shirjan Khadka · Founder, Nepse Decode, Kathmandu · September 28, 2026 · Updated September 28, 2026 · 6 min read
Facebook X LinkedIn WhatsApp Viber

In the first two months of fiscal year 2083/84, Nepal's government raised the capital gains tax on share trading, and then watched the revenue it was supposed to raise collapse by 54%. In mid-September, the Cabinet backed down: the tax on NEPSE share sales was cut to 3.5% for long-term holdings and 5% for short-term holdings, roughly halving the budget's rates. It is a small episode as fiscal history goes. It is also a clean natural experiment in what happens when you tax a thin, sentiment-driven market too hard.

The FY 2083/84 budget had raised capital gains tax on the sale of listed-company shares for natural persons to 7.5% for shares held more than one year and 10% for holdings of one year or less. Rather than boosting state coffers, the hike landed on an already fragile market, and the data is brutal. According to CDS and Clearing Limited (CDSC) figures reported by financial media, the government collected just Rs 1.25 billion in share-transaction profit tax through Shrawan and Bhadra, down from Rs 2.75 billion in the same period last year, a 54.42% decline. Daily turnover shrank, investor morale sagged, and the NEPSE index slid toward the mid-2,600s. The tax hike shrank the tax base it was meant to harvest: push the rate past the point investors can bear, and the activity you are taxing simply disappears.

The government answered with the Capital Market Strengthening and Revival Action Plan, a 21-point package unveiled by Finance Minister Dr Swarnim Wagle and aimed at reviving the capital market. The tax cut was its centerpiece, but the plan went wider: investors would be able to offset losses from share investments against gains; the minimum holding period for banks' securities investments would drop from six months to 45 days; and companies would be allowed to buy back their own shares.

Acting on the plan, a mid-September Cabinet meeting approved the cut. As reported by Nepali financial media: shares held more than 365 days are now taxed at 3.5%, down from 7.5%; shares held 365 days or less at 5%, down from 10%. A note on precision: the Ministry's earlier action-plan draft had proposed 3.75% for holdings above 365 days, and at least one financial outlet reported implementation at that figure when trading resumed on September 22. The Cabinet-approved figure reported by multiple Nepali outlets is 3.5%; the Gazette publication will settle the number in law. One important procedural detail: the new rates apply after publication in the Nepal Gazette, followed by system updates from the Inland Revenue Department and CDSC. Investors should confirm the live rate in their broker's settlement notes before transacting.

A concrete example makes the cut tangible. On a Rs 1 lakh capital gain from selling listed shares, the long-term tax falls from Rs 7,500 to Rs 3,500, and the short-term tax from Rs 10,000 to Rs 5,000. Scale that across a portfolio and the cut is the equivalent of a meaningful yield improvement, without any change in market prices. The deliberate gap between the two rates is the policy message: the government wants you to hold longer. Combined with the proposed loss-netting provision, the structure rewards patient, realized-gains-based investing over high-frequency churn.

Markets voted with their orders. After the reform plan was unveiled, the NEPSE surged 48.57 points (1.87%) to 2,633.62, with all 13 sectoral indices ending higher and turnover nearly doubling to Rs 8.29 billion from Rs 4.68 billion the previous session, the highest in roughly two months. When trading resumed after the September 21 ransomware shutdown, the index added 7.06 points to close at 2,654.28, with turnover of Rs 7.05 billion. Media reports said the new lower rates were the rates applied in settlement, though investors were still advised to confirm the live rate in their broker notes pending the Gazette publication. The rally is real, but keep it in proportion: sentiment lifted by a tax cut is not the same as an earnings recovery. The Bhotekoshi flood damage to listed hydropower assets, weak capital spending, and the market's broader liquidity dynamics have not changed. A tax cut removes a headwind; it does not manufacture a tailwind.

The episode carries a lesson larger than two rate cuts. In a frontier market like Nepal's, where participation is thin and turnover is concentrated, transaction taxes are participation taxes. Raise them and you don't just raise revenue per trade; you reduce the number of trades, shrink liquidity, widen spreads, and ultimately collect less than before. The 54% revenue collapse is the receipt. For investors, the practical takeaway is modest but useful: understand the 365-day boundary, plan holding periods with it in mind, keep settlement records clean for the proposed loss-netting, and watch the Gazette for the final text. Policy can change fast; the arithmetic of your own portfolio should change faster.

This article is research and education, not investment advice. Tax rules described here are based on media-reported Cabinet decisions and the Ministry of Finance's action plan; verify the final published rates and consult a tax professional for your specific situation.
