ADB Cuts Nepal's Growth Forecast to 4.1% After Floods: What It Means for the Economy

By Shirjan Khadka September 26, 2026 6 min read

This week, the Asian Development Bank handed Nepal's economy a mixed report card. In its September 2026 Asian Development Outlook, the bank cut its growth forecast for FY2026/27 to 4.1%, down from the 4.5% it projected just two months earlier — and at the same time raised its inflation forecast to 5.3% from 5.0%. Slower growth and higher prices is the combination no policymaker wants to read about. Here's what changed, why, and how the downgrade fits with everything else we know about the economy right now.

The driver, per the ADB, is the devastating floods that hit this monsoon. But the story isn't just about washed-out roads — it runs through hydropower exports, food supply, logistics costs, and global fuel prices. Let's walk through it piece by piece.

The Headline Numbers: What Changed in Two Months

Here's the ADB's trajectory for Nepal across this year:

  • FY2025/26 (ended mid-July): now estimated at 3.9% growth — revised up from the 3.3% projected in July, on stronger private investment and consumption.
  • FY2026/27: now forecast at 4.1% growth — revised down from 4.5% in July, on flood damage.
  • Inflation: the FY2026/27 forecast moved up to 5.3% from 5.0%, while FY2025/26 inflation averaged an estimated 3.1%.

Notice the asymmetry: the past year actually did better than expected. Private investment surged 39.6% in FY2025/26 after contracting 8.1% the year before, and private consumption — the largest component of demand — grew 3.2%, supported by remittances and lower interest rates. Weak execution of the capital budget continued to hold back public investment, but the private side surprised to the upside. The downgrade is entirely about what comes next, not what just happened.

Why the Downgrade: The Flood's Economic Footprint

"The tragic loss of life is the most devastating consequence of the floods," the ADB's country director for Nepal, Arnaud Cauchois, said, "but the disaster will also have severe and far-reaching impacts on livelihoods, critical infrastructure, and Nepal's wider economy." The economic channels are specific:

  • Hydropower. Damage to facilities from the Bhotekoshi flood threatens one of Nepal's most promising growth engines — electricity exports. A dam that's down for repair sells no power across the border.
  • Transport and trade. Damaged road links impede trade flows and tourism, right when both matter for the current fiscal year.
  • Agriculture. Disruptions to farming and local economic activity are expected to weigh on industry and services in the first quarter of the fiscal year.
  • Public finances. Reconstruction and rehabilitation spending should partly offset the losses by restoring demand — but it also pressures the budget, which brings us to a deeper problem below.

The damage isn't permanent to Nepal's growth story in principle — reconstruction will support recovery. But in a single fiscal year's arithmetic, destruction now and rebuilding later is a net negative for growth, even if rebuilding creates real jobs and demand.

The Second Headwind: Fuel and the Middle East

The floods are only half of the inflation story. The ADB also points to higher global fuel prices driven by the ongoing conflict in the Middle East as a source of cost pressure — and the domestic data already shows it biting. Crude petroleum prices averaged $92.02 per barrel in the first month of the fiscal year, up 36.7% from $67.30 a year earlier, according to Nepal Rastra Bank. That flows straight into transport costs — up 13.17% year-on-year — which then ripple through every supply chain that moves goods by road, which is to say all of them.

The ADB raised its inflation forecast partly because reconstruction itself adds demand for construction materials, transport, and labor, on top of ample domestic liquidity. Rebuilding is necessary; it is also, mechanically, inflationary. Policymakers will have to live with both facts at once.

Meanwhile, Inflation Is Already Running Hot

Here's the part that should catch every reader's attention: inflation isn't a forecast risk anymore — it's the current reality. Consumer price inflation hit 5.96% in Shrawan (mid-July to mid-August), the first month of the fiscal year. That's the highest in roughly 18 months, and it's already above the central bank's 5.5% annual target.

The pain is concentrated where households feel it most. Food and beverage prices rose 6.77% year-on-year, led by ghee and edible oils at 15.62%, fruits at 15.39%, fish and meat at 8.65%, and vegetables at 6.90%. Non-food items and services rose 5.52%. It's also geographically uneven — Madhesh Province felt 6.94% inflation versus 4.81% in the Kathmandu Valley, and rural areas 6.18% versus 5.88% in urban centers.

One caveat that matters: Shrawan is a single month, and early-fiscal-year readings can be noisy. But the ADB's own 5.3% forecast for the full year suggests this isn't just noise — and with flood damage constraining food supply further, the direction of pressure is clear.

The Tale of Two Economies: Record Reserves, Weak Demand

And yet — here's the paradox. While growth forecasts fall and prices rise, Nepal's external position has never looked stronger. Foreign exchange reserves hit a record Rs 3.946 trillion (US$25.84 billion) by mid-August, enough to cover 18.8 months of goods-and-services imports. The current account recorded a Rs 94.59 billion surplus, the balance of payments a Rs 90.34 billion surplus. Remittances keep flowing in; exports grew 61.7%.

The problem is what the money doesn't do once it arrives. In the first month of the fiscal year, the government collected Rs 92.23 billion in revenue but spent only Rs 41.89 billion — and capital expenditure, the kind that builds roads and dams and jobs, was a meager Rs 1.33 billion. Meanwhile, Rs 361.81 billion sat idle in government accounts at the central bank. That's not a liquidity problem. That's an execution problem.

This is the crux of Nepal's current economic story: a country that can finance 18 months of imports cannot spend its own development budget. Interest rates have been cut, liquidity is ample — and businesses still won't borrow to invest, because domestic demand is weak. Lower rates, as one market analysis put it, are not a magic bullet when demand itself is the missing ingredient.

How to Read a Forecast (and Why They Keep Moving)

Two things worth keeping in mind about the ADB's numbers. First, forecasts move in both directions — this same institution revised up its estimate of last year's growth by 0.6 percentage points in the same report that downgraded next year's. A downgrade after an upgrade isn't hypocrisy; it's how new information works. Second, these projections rest on assumptions — about the Middle East conflict, about monsoon damage, about reconstruction pace — that can and will shift. Treat the 4.1% as the ADB's best current reading of the balance of forces, not as a prediction carved in stone.

It's also worth noting the gap between ambition and outlook: the government's own target was 7% growth with inflation capped at 6%, and the central bank built its monetary policy around 5.5% inflation. The ADB's 4.1% / 5.3% now sits below the growth target and near the edge of the inflation comfort zone. Whether policy can close that gap — through faster capital spending, smarter reconstruction, and honest flood recovery — is the real story to watch this fiscal year.
