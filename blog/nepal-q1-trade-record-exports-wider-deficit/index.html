Nepal exported a record Rs 114 billion in Q1. The deficit still grew by a third.

Exports rose 57% in the first quarter of FY2083/84 — but almost half of them were soybean oil refined from imported crude, and imports grew faster in rupees, so the trade deficit widened to Rs 532.49 billion. The record is real. It is also mostly re-export.

By Nepse Decode Research Desk · Kathmandu · November 9, 2026 · 25 min read
Facebook X LinkedIn WhatsApp Viber

Nepal exported goods worth Rs 114.28 billion in the first quarter of FY2083/84 (mid-July to mid-October 2026), the most it has ever exported in a first quarter — up 57.03% on the same three months a year earlier, according to the Department of Customs. It is the kind of number that reads like a breakthrough. It is not, on its own, a breakthrough. In the same three months Nepal imported goods worth Rs 646.78 billion, up 38.18%, and the trade deficit — the gap between what the country buys from the world and what it sells to it — widened 34.71% to Rs 532.49 billion. The export growth is real, and it is also narrow: Rs 52.60 billion of it, 46% of everything Nepal sold abroad, was soybean oil refined from crude oil that had been imported first. The record and the wider deficit are the same story, told from opposite ends.

Signal summary

  • The record: exports of Rs 114.28 billion in Q1 FY2083/84, up 57.03% year on year from Rs 72.78 billion — already 36% of last fiscal year’s full-year record of just over Rs 315 billion, reached in a single quarter.
  • The gap: imports of Rs 646.78 billion, up 38.18% from Rs 468.08 billion, pushed the deficit to Rs 532.49 billion, up 34.71% from Rs 395.30 billion — Rs 137.19 billion wider in a year. Imports grew by Rs 178.70 billion in rupees; exports grew by Rs 41.50 billion. The import increase was more than four times the export increase.
  • The loop: soybean oil was Nepal’s top export at Rs 52.60 billion, and crude soybean oil was its top import at Rs 52.60 billion — 298.48 million litres in, 217.09 million kilograms of refined oil out. The same rupees, in and out; the value Nepal keeps is the refining margin, not the headline figure.
  • The month: Ashoj alone brought in Rs 245.26 billion of imports — 38% of the quarter’s total in one festive month — and a computed deficit of about Rs 207.57 billion, 39% of the quarter’s gap.
  • The partner: India took 83.6% of Nepal’s exports (Rs 95.59 billion of Rs 114.28 billion) and supplied 56.6% of its imports (Rs 366 billion of Rs 646.78 billion). The bilateral deficit with India is about Rs 271 billion — roughly half the total gap.
  • The financing: Nepal Rastra Bank’s three-month macroeconomic report, covering the same quarter, had not been published as of November 9, so no Q1 remittance or reserves figure exists yet. The latest verified context is the two-month report: remittances of Rs 436.36 billion and reserves of Rs 4,065.78 billion, enough for 17.8 months of imports.

01. The quarter in five numbers

The Department of Customs released the first-quarter foreign trade figures on October 28, and the reporting since then has been unusually consistent: myRepublica, OnlineKhabar, Fiscal Nepal, ktm2day, redvoicenepal, People’s Review and ShareSansar all carry the same five numbers. That agreement matters, because trade figures are sometimes reported with different bases — one widely shared social media infographic, for example, put total Q1 trade at Rs 535.97 billion and imports at Rs 421.68 billion, figures that contradict the Customs data every outlet above reports. This article uses only the Customs figures, and says so where a number is computed from them rather than published.

Two readings of that table are both true, and the argument of this article is that neither is the whole story. The optimistic reading: exports grew faster than imports in percentage terms (57% against 38%), and the export share of total trade rose from 13.46% to 15.02% — the first time in years the balance has moved in Nepal’s favour on that measure. The pessimistic reading: in rupees, imports grew by Rs 178.70 billion and exports by Rs 41.50 billion, so the deficit widened by Rs 137.19 billion anyway. Percentages flatter the smaller base. For every rupee of goods Nepal imported in the quarter, it exported only about 17 paisa worth — an export-import ratio of 17.7%, computed from the Customs totals. A 57% rise on a small base does not close a gap more than four and a half times the size of the exports themselves.

The scale is worth pausing on. Total foreign trade of Rs 761.07 billion in three months is itself a record pace, and the quarter’s exports alone are 36% of the whole of last fiscal year’s exports — just over Rs 315 billion, itself a record. If the quarterly pace held for the full year, Nepal would export more than Rs 450 billion. It almost certainly will not hold: the first quarter contains the festive season, and the soybean oil trade that drives the growth depends on a duty gap in India that New Delhi can narrow at any time. Section 06 takes up what could break the pace.

02. The record, and what it is made of

Vegetable oil was more than half of everything Nepal exported in the quarter: Rs 59.32 billion of Rs 114.28 billion, or 51.9% computed. Soybean oil alone was Rs 52.60 billion — 46.0% of all exports. Sunflower oil added Rs 2.53 billion and palm oil Rs 2.42 billion. The rest of Nepal’s exports — everything that is not vegetable oil — came to Rs 54.96 billion computed, less than the vegetable-oil total on its own. Nepal’s export “record” is, in the main, a single commodity line.

And that commodity line is, in the main, imported first. Crude soybean oil was the most imported commodity by value in the quarter: 298.48 million litres worth Rs 52.60 billion, led by Argentina with roughly 169.5 million litres worth over Rs 29.20 billion, with China, Brazil, Paraguay and Vietnam among the other suppliers. Nepal imported crude vegetable oils worth Rs 66.66 billion in total. The refineries — mostly in the Birgunj corridor — process that crude and sell the refined oil onward to India, where it enters duty-free under the South Asian Free Trade Area while refined oil from elsewhere faces a steep duty. The mechanism is explained in full in this site’s October 22 article, Nepal’s exports grew 62%. Almost half of them were imported first, which covered the first two months of the fiscal year; this article is the quarter-close update and does not re-litigate it. The one-paragraph version: India charges an effective 35.75% duty on refined edible oil from the rest of the world against 16.5% on crude, and nothing at all on goods from Nepal under SAFTA — so refining in Nepal and selling into India is, for now, a profitable arbitrage, and Nepal’s export statistics record the full refined value as an export.

None of this makes the exports fake. The refining is real work done in Nepal, the plants employ people, and the margin between the crude price and the refined price is real income that stays in the country. What it makes them is fragile and shallow. Fragile, because the trade exists only while India’s duty gap exists: New Delhi cut the basic duty on crude edible oil from 20% to 10% in late May, and any further narrowing of the gap between crude and refined duties would shrink the arbitrage that fills Nepal’s refineries. Shallow, because for every rupee of soybean oil Nepal exports, it first spends roughly a rupee importing the crude — so the Rs 52.60 billion of exports does not mean Rs 52.60 billion of new foreign exchange. The net foreign exchange earned is the refining spread, which the Customs data as reported does not disclose. Any commentary that treats the full Rs 52.60 billion as earnings is overstating the gain by the cost of the crude.

The concentration is the second risk. One commodity is 46% of exports; one destination — India, section 04 — takes 84% of them. A single policy decision in New Delhi, or a single bad quarter for one commodity, moves the national export total. The October 22 article made the same point for the first two months, when soybean oil was 47% of exports; the quarter-close figure, 46.0%, shows the concentration did not dilute as the quarter went on. It hardened.

03. Ashoj did the damage

The quarter was not evenly spread. Nepal Rastra Bank’s two-month report, published October 15, put first-two-months imports at Rs 401.52 billion and exports at Rs 76.59 billion, a deficit of Rs 324.93 billion. Subtract those from the Customs quarter totals and the third month — Ashoj, mid-September to mid-October — stands out: imports of Rs 245.26 billion, exports of Rs 37.69 billion computed, and a deficit of about Rs 207.57 billion computed. In a single month, Nepal imported 38% of the quarter’s total and ran 39% of the quarter’s deficit.

The Ashoj surge is a festive-season story, and the outlets reporting the Customs data say so plainly: Dashain and Tihar fall in this quarter, and households and businesses stock up. Petroleum products were the largest import category at about Rs 119 billion for the quarter; vehicles and spare parts reached Rs 53.19 billion, including electric vehicles worth Rs 15.03 billion (6,636 units); machinery, electrical equipment and iron and steel were also among the biggest import items. Some of that is durable — vehicles and machinery are investment, not consumption — but the timing is unmistakably seasonal: September alone, the month the festive buying peaked, took in Rs 245.26 billion of imports.

The question the next quarter will answer is whether Ashoj was a spike or a new run-rate. If imports settle back toward the first-two-months pace of about Rs 200 billion a month, the deficit narrows on its own. If the quarter’s pace holds for the full year instead, the deficit heads toward Rs 2.1 trillion (Rs 532.49 billion × 4) — a number this article states only as the quarterly pace annualised, not as a forecast, because one quarter is not a trend, but which shows why the Ashoj figure matters more than the quarterly average. The festive season is over; the import bill in the next Customs release will tell whether the appetite was seasonal or structural.

04. India is half the story

India remained Nepal’s largest trading partner by a distance, on both sides of the ledger. Nepal imported goods worth Rs 366 billion from India in the quarter and exported goods worth Rs 95.59 billion there, leaving a bilateral deficit of about Rs 271 billion. Put against the totals, the concentration is stark: 83.6% of Nepal’s exports went to India (computed), and 56.6% of its imports came from India (computed). The bilateral deficit with India alone is roughly half of the country’s entire trade gap.

Where the Rs 532.49 billion deficit sits (Rs billion)

Computed from the Department of Customs figures: imports from India Rs 366 billion minus exports to India Rs 95.59 billion gives a bilateral deficit of Rs 270.41 billion; the remainder of the total deficit is Rs 262.08 billion. The bilateral figure is reported as “approximately Rs 271 billion” by Fiscal Nepal and ShareSansar.

That split carries two different messages. The first is that the deficit is not only an India story: even if trade with India balanced tomorrow, Nepal would still run a deficit of about Rs 262 billion with the rest of the world — driven by fuel from the Gulf, crude edible oil from Argentina, Brazil and Paraguay, and vehicles and machinery from China and elsewhere. Argentina alone became Nepal’s third-largest import source at Rs 35.65 billion, almost entirely on the back of crude edible oil. The second message is that the export story is almost entirely an India story: exports to India rose from Rs 59.12 billion in the same quarter last year to Rs 95.59 billion now, a computed increase of 61.7%, and that increase — Rs 36.47 billion — accounts for nearly nine-tenths of the total export growth of Rs 41.50 billion. Nepal’s export record is, to a first approximation, a record of selling refined soybean oil to India.

The dependence cuts both ways. India is the market that makes the refining trade work, and it is also the source of more than half of what Nepal buys — including the petroleum products that topped the import list. A trade relationship that concentrated is not a weakness in itself; geography makes India Nepal’s natural partner. But it means the country’s external balance is hostage to two decisions it does not control: India’s edible-oil duty structure, and the price of the crude oil and fuel it imports. Diversification is the standard prescription, and the numbers show how far there is to go: exports to the whole rest of the world combined were Rs 18.69 billion computed in the quarter — less than a fifth of what went to India alone.

05. How the gap gets paid for

A trade deficit of Rs 532.49 billion in three months has to be financed, and the honest answer to how it was financed in this quarter is that the full picture is not yet published. Nepal Rastra Bank’s Current Macroeconomic and Financial Situation report for the three months to mid-October — the document that would carry the quarterly remittance, current-account, balance-of-payments and reserves figures — had not been released as of November 9. The central bank’s website still carries the two-month report, published October 15, as its latest. This article does not fill that gap with an estimate. What follows is the two-month picture, labelled as such.

In the first two months, remittances rose 23.9% to Rs 436.36 billion (USD 2.85 billion), the current account recorded a surplus of Rs 169.04 billion, and gross foreign exchange reserves reached Rs 4,065.78 billion by mid-September — enough to cover 17.8 months of goods and services imports, far above the conventional three-month adequacy benchmark. That is the context in which the deficit sits: the country’s workers abroad sent home more in two months (Rs 436.36 billion) than the entire first-quarter trade deficit’s two-month portion (Rs 324.93 billion). Remittances are what make a deficit of this size payable without a reserves crisis, and they are also what make it easy to ignore. The November 3 article in this series, Rs 2,363 billion came home last year. Almost none of it became investment, takes up the longer question of what that money becomes once it arrives; the short version is that it becomes consumption, deposits and land far more often than it becomes the export industries that would shrink the deficit itself.

The reserves position is genuinely strong — 17.8 months of import cover — but it is a stock, not a flow, and it is being drawn on by more than goods trade. In the same two months, Nepali students and families paid Rs 29.55 billion for education abroad, nearly twice what tourists brought in, as the October 30 article in this series reported: Rs 29.55 billion left Nepal for foreign classrooms in two months. Goods, services and transfers all draw on the same foreign exchange. When the three-month NRB report lands — expected in mid-November — the number to watch is not the reserves total but the current account: whether the third month’s import surge pushed it from the two-month surplus of Rs 169.04 billion toward deficit, and whether remittance growth kept pace with the import bill.

06. What to watch

  • The NRB three-month report, expected mid-November. It will give the first official read on Q1 remittances, the current account, the balance of payments and reserves at mid-October — the financing side this article cannot yet show. If the current account has swung to deficit on the Ashoj import surge, the deficit story changes from a trade statistic into a financing question.
  • India’s edible-oil duties. The refining trade lives on the gap between India’s 16.5% effective duty on crude edible oil and 35.75% on refined oil, with Nepali goods entering duty-free under SAFTA. New Delhi has already moved once this year, cutting the crude duty from 20% to 10% in late May. Any further narrowing would cut the margin that fills Nepal’s refineries — and with it, the export line that is 46% of the total.
  • Whether Ashoj was a spike. The next Customs release will show whether monthly imports settle back toward Rs 200 billion or stay near the Ashoj level of Rs 245.26 billion. The answer decides whether the full-year deficit lands near Rs 2 trillion or pushes past Rs 2.1 trillion.
  • Whether anything else starts exporting. Vegetable oil is 51.9% of exports; everything else combined is Rs 54.96 billion. The growth forecasts published in late October — the World Bank at 3.7%, the IMF at 3.0%, the Asian Development Bank at 4.1% for the fiscal year, covered in Three forecasters, three Nepals — all assume an economy that keeps growing on remittance-fuelled consumption. None of them assumes Nepal finds a second export.
  • The full-year export pace. One quarter at Rs 114.28 billion is 36% of last year’s record full-year total of just over Rs 315 billion. Holding the pace would be a second consecutive record year — but the pace contains a festive month and a duty arbitrage, and neither is guaranteed to repeat.

What to take from it

Nepal’s first-quarter trade figures contain a genuine achievement and a warning, and the warning is the more important of the two. The achievement is that exports grew 57% to Rs 114.28 billion, the fastest quarterly growth in years, and the export share of total trade rose for the first time in a long while. The warning is that the growth is one commodity, refined from imported crude, sold to one country, on the back of a duty gap Nepal does not control — and that even at that growth rate, imports grew by Rs 178.70 billion in rupees against exports’ Rs 41.50 billion, so the deficit widened by Rs 137.19 billion anyway. A record that leaves the gap wider is not a turning point; it is a bigger version of the same pattern. The test of whether it becomes a turning point is simple and will be visible in the next two Customs releases: does anything other than soybean oil start to grow, and does the import bill come back down after the festivals? Until both answers are yes, the honest description of Nepal’s trade is the one the numbers give: record exports, wider deficit, and a gap that remittances — not exports — are still paying for. Nothing in this article is a recommendation to buy or sell any share.

Frequently asked questions

What is Nepal’s trade deficit, and how big is it now?

The trade deficit is the gap between what Nepal imports and what it exports. In the first quarter of FY2083/84 it was Rs 532.49 billion: imports of Rs 646.78 billion minus exports of Rs 114.28 billion, according to the Department of Customs. It is 34.71% wider than the Rs 395.30 billion deficit in the same quarter last year.

Why did the deficit grow if exports rose 57%?

Because percentages hide the size of the bases. Exports grew by Rs 41.50 billion in rupees (Rs 72.78 billion to Rs 114.28 billion), while imports grew by Rs 178.70 billion (Rs 468.08 billion to Rs 646.78 billion) — more than four times as much. A 57% rise on a small base cannot close a gap more than four and a half times the size of total exports.

Is the soybean oil export real, or just paper trade?

It is real trade, not paper trade: crude oil is physically imported, refined in Nepalese plants, and physically exported to India. What it is not is Rs 52.60 billion of new foreign exchange. Nepal first spends about Rs 52.60 billion importing the crude, so the foreign exchange it keeps is only the refining margin — the difference between the crude price and the refined price — which the Customs data as reported does not disclose.

Which month drove the deficit?

Ashoj, the third month of the quarter. The first two months ran a deficit of Rs 324.93 billion (Nepal Rastra Bank’s two-month report); subtracting that from the quarter total of Rs 532.49 billion leaves a computed Ashoj deficit of about Rs 207.57 billion, on imports of Rs 245.26 billion — 39% of the quarter’s deficit in a single festive month.

How much of Nepal’s trade is with India?

Most of it. India took 83.6% of Nepal’s exports in the quarter (Rs 95.59 billion of Rs 114.28 billion) and supplied 56.6% of its imports (Rs 366 billion of Rs 646.78 billion). The bilateral deficit with India is about Rs 271 billion — roughly half of the total Rs 532.49 billion gap. The other half, about Rs 262 billion, is with the rest of the world, driven by fuel, crude edible oil, vehicles and machinery.

How does Nepal pay for a deficit this size?

Mostly with remittances. In the first two months of the fiscal year, Nepali workers abroad sent home Rs 436.36 billion, more than the two-month trade deficit of Rs 324.93 billion, and foreign exchange reserves stood at Rs 4,065.78 billion — enough for 17.8 months of imports. The full first-quarter financing picture is not yet published: Nepal Rastra Bank’s three-month report had not been released as of November 9, 2026.

What should I watch next?

Three things. First, Nepal Rastra Bank’s three-month report, expected in mid-November, for the remittance and current-account side of the quarter. Second, the next Customs release: whether monthly imports settle back toward Rs 200 billion after the festive season or stay near Ashoj’s Rs 245.26 billion. Third, India’s edible-oil duties: the refining trade that supplies 46% of Nepal’s exports exists only while the duty gap between crude and refined oil stays wide.
