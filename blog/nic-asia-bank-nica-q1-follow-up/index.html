NICA at Rs 342: the rights issue is cleared and the earnings lens more than doubled — the 9.81% NPL is still the story

NIC Asia Bank’s first-quarter profit was Rs 127.44 million, its bad loans reached 9.81%, and SEBON has now approved a 50% rights issue — 74,587,835 shares at Rs 100. At Rs 342 the share is up 3.3% since October 30, the only gainer of the seven big banks in this article. This follow-up re-runs the valuation lenses on the new numbers and asks what changed since November 2 — and what did not.

By Nepse Decode Research Desk · Kathmandu · November 17, 2026 · 26 min read
Facebook X LinkedIn WhatsApp Viber

Fifteen days ago this desk valued NIC Asia Bank at Rs 331 and found a bank priced like a normal bank on book value and like a crisis on earnings: 1.92 times book against a 1.87-times peer median, and 271 times a full year’s earnings of Rs 1.22 a share. Since then the bank has published its first-quarter results, SEBON has approved its rights issue, and the market has had two sessions to answer. The price is Rs 342. The book-value verdict has barely moved. The earnings lens has more than doubled — and that is exactly why it cannot be read as a value.

This article is a follow-up, not a re-run. It uses the same six peers and the same method as the November 2 analysis, but every figure is fresh: closes at November 13, 2026, the last verified session, and first-quarter numbers for fiscal year 2083/84, published November 2. Where a figure’s basis has changed — and the earnings basis has changed completely — the article says so, because a lens that moves 2.4 times on a change of basis is telling you about the basis, not about the bank.

Signal summary

  • The price rose, then gave some back. NICA closed at Rs 348 on November 6, the first Friday after the Q1 numbers, up 5.1% from Rs 331 on October 30. By November 13 it was Rs 342 — still up 3.3% on October 30, and the only one of the seven banks in this article that is higher than it was then.
  • The quarter’s profit is small and the bad loans are not. Net profit was Rs 127.44 million, up 4.3% on the same quarter last year — 0.67% of the Rs 19.14 billion the 20 commercial banks earned between them. Non-performing loans reached 9.81%, up from 6.99% a year ago and 1.8 times the sector average of 5.44%.
  • The earnings lens more than doubled because its basis changed. Annualising one quarter gives earnings of Rs 3.35 a share, against Rs 1.22 for the whole of last year. At the peer median of 13.91 times, that puts the earnings lens at Rs 46.58, up from Rs 19.67. One quarter, annualised, is not a valuation — it assumes the next three quarters repeat a quarter in which core interest income fell 17.5%.
  • The book lens barely moved, and that is the verdict that matters. Book value is Rs 172.53 a share and the peer median is 1.84 times book, putting the book lens at Rs 317.46, against Rs 322.24 on November 2. At Rs 342 the market still pays 1.98 times book — a normal bank’s price for a bank whose earnings are anything but normal.
  • The rights issue is now real, and it dilutes book value per share. SEBON approved the issue in early November 2026: 74,587,835 shares at Rs 100, one new share for every two held, raising Rs 7.46 billion. Sold at par against a book value of Rs 172.53, those shares cut book value per share to about Rs 148.36 on simple arithmetic. No book-closure or opening date has been announced yet.

From October 30 to November 6 the share rose Rs 17, or 5.1%. From November 6 to November 13 it fell Rs 6, or 1.7% — on November 13 itself it opened at Rs 348, touched Rs 348.10, fell as low as Rs 339.50 and closed at Rs 342, on 107,497 shares worth Rs 3.68 crore across 341 trades. Net over the two weeks: up Rs 11, or 3.3%.

Two cautions before that rise is read as a verdict on the Q1 numbers. First, the index did not rise with it: NEPSE closed at 2,655.22 on November 6 and 2,648.91 on November 13, down 6.31 points or 0.24% across the same week, so NICA rose while the market slipped. Second, a price move in the week results are published is not proof the results caused it — the rights-issue approval landed in the same week, and the market was returning from a four-day holiday. This article describes the move; it does not claim a cause for it.

What the move does establish is the peer comparison in section 05: against the same October 30 starting line, every other bank in the peer set is flat or down. NICA’s 3.3% rise stands alone, and it stands alone in the week its results and its rights approval both landed. That is worth noting, and worth not over-reading.

02. The Q1 numbers: a small profit, a shrinking core, rising bad loans

NIC Asia Bank published its unaudited first-quarter results for fiscal year 2083/84 on November 2, 2026, covering the quarter ended mid-October. The headline is a modest rise in profit. Everything underneath the headline points the other way.

Read the table top to bottom and the profit rise thins out. Net interest income — the money a bank makes from lending minus what it pays depositors — fell by Rs 396.8 million, or 17.5%. Fee income rose, but by Rs 21.6 million, a fraction of what interest income lost. Total operating income fell 12.6% and operating profit fell 16.6%. The net profit still rose, because profit before tax rose only 4.3% on a much smaller operating base — the arithmetic of a small number, not a recovery. Return on equity is 1.94% and return on assets 0.13%: a bank earning almost nothing on the money it holds.

The balance sheet is large and quiet. Deposits stand at Rs 341.85 billion, credit and advances at Rs 191.20 billion, total assets at Rs 389.00 billion. The credit-to-deposit ratio of 60.22% is the lowest of the readings in this article’s peer set by a distance — the bank holds far more in deposits than it lends out, and the gap widened from 66.08% a year ago. That is not a verdict on its own: a low ratio can mean caution, or it can mean loans that are not performing and cannot be replaced. The NPL figure points to the second reading.

The sector context sharpens the picture. The 20 commercial banks earned Rs 19.14 billion between them in the quarter, up 45.59% on last year, with Nabil Bank first at Rs 2.55 billion and Global IME second at Rs 2.31 billion. NIC Asia’s Rs 127.44 million is 0.67% of that total, and the second-smallest profit of the 20 — only Agricultural Development Bank earned less, at Rs 109.2 million. The sector’s distributable profit stands at a negative Rs 9.07 billion, and NIC Asia accounts for the deepest hole in it: negative Rs 15.39 billion, larger than the sector total itself because other banks’ positive distributable profits offset it. A bank with negative distributable profit cannot pay a dividend, and none has been proposed for FY2082/83; the latest dividend remains FY2079/80’s 30.52%, announced in September 2023.

03. The rights issue: approved, priced at par, and dilutive by arithmetic

The second thing that changed since November 2 is the capital question. At the 28th annual general meeting on June 1, 2026, shareholders approved a rights issue. In early November 2026 SEBON gave its approval for the bank to publish the offer document — portal reports differ by a day on the approval date, Kartik 17 or 18, 2083 — and the terms are now public: 74,587,835 shares at Rs 100 each, with NIMB Ace Capital as issue and sales manager. No book-closure date and no opening date has been announced yet.

The ratio needs a careful reading, because it is written two ways. Some reports call it 2:1. The unit count settles what that means: 74,587,835 shares is exactly half the 149,175,670 shares now outstanding, so the issue is one new share for every two held — two existing shares to one new share, not two new shares for one held. The raise is 74,587,835 × Rs 100 = Rs 7,458,783,500, about Rs 7.46 billion, and it is 50% of the bank’s existing paid-up capital of Rs 14.92 billion. After the issue, paid-up capital would be about Rs 22.38 billion and the share count 223,763,505.

The price matters as much as the size. The shares are offered at par, Rs 100, against a book value of Rs 172.53 a share. Selling new shares below book value dilutes the book value of the shares that already exist, and the arithmetic is simple enough to state: the bank’s total book value is about Rs 25.74 billion today (Rs 172.53 × 149,175,670 shares); add the Rs 7.46 billion the rights issue brings in, and it is about Rs 33.20 billion spread over 223,763,505 shares — about Rs 148.36 a share. That is a mechanical illustration, not a forecast: it assumes the issue is fully subscribed and nothing else changes. But it shows the direction clearly. A shareholder who does not subscribe owns the same bank with a smaller claim on each share’s book value; a shareholder who does subscribe pays Rs 100 for a share whose book value, after the issue, is about Rs 148.

The rights issue also answers the capital adequacy question, in part. The capital adequacy ratio fell to 11.12% in the quarter, from 14.03% a year ago, against Nepal Rastra Bank’s minimum of 11% — a margin of 0.12 percentage points. The Rs 7.46 billion the issue raises is new capital, so it lifts that ratio; by how much cannot be computed from the published figures, because the ratio depends on risk-weighted assets, which the quarterly release does not disclose. What can be said is that the timing is not accidental: a bank 0.12 points above its minimum, with bad loans at 9.81%, is a bank that needs the capital, and the market now knows the capital is coming — at par, and at the cost of the dilution above.

The difference is the earnings basis. On November 2 the peers were read on full-year FY2082/83 earnings. The medians are the middle of the six peers, not an average: the P/E median of 13.91 times sits between Sanima’s 13.78 and Kumari’s 14.03, and the P/B median of 1.84 times sits between Global IME’s 1.51 and Sanima’s 2.17. Dropping the highest and lowest peer and re-running the median — the trimmed check — gives 13.88 times for earnings and 1.88 times for book, close enough that the conclusion does not depend on which median is used.

Apply those medians to NIC Asia and the two lenses part company further than they did on November 2. The earnings lens is Rs 3.35 × 13.91 = Rs 46.58. The book lens is Rs 172.53 × 1.84 = Rs 317.46, with a range of Rs 229.46 to Rs 505.51 if the lowest and highest peer multiples are used instead of the median. The price, Rs 342, sits 7.7% above the book lens and more than seven times the earnings lens.

The earnings lens deserves a warning label, and it gets one. On November 2 it was Rs 19.67, built on Rs 1.22 of full-year earnings and a 16.12-times median. It is now Rs 46.58 — 2.37 times higher — because the earnings figure changed, not because the bank earned 2.37 times more. The Rs 3.35 is one quarter’s profit multiplied by four. Annualising assumes the next three quarters each earn Rs 127.44 million, and nothing in the quarter supports that assumption: net interest income fell 17.5%, operating profit fell 16.6%, and bad loans rose to 9.81%. A single quarter’s profit, annualised, is a sensitivity input, not an earnings run-rate.

The sensitivity makes the point in numbers. If NIC Asia earned the peer median of Rs 26.50 a share, the earnings lens would be Rs 368.48; at half the median, Rs 13.25, it would be Rs 184.24. Neither is a forecast or a target — they are the arithmetic of “what if the earnings were normal”, and the gap between Rs 46.58 and Rs 368.48 is the size of the earnings problem, not a price call. The book lens carries no such warning, because book value does not need annualising: Rs 172.53 is a fact at the quarter’s end, and 1.84 times is what the market pays for a rupee of a peer’s book. That is why the November 2 headline still stands, fifteen days later: priced like a normal bank on book value, priced like a crisis on earnings.

A 9.81% bad-loan ratio is 2.42 times Nabil’s 4.06% and 2.05 times Global IME’s 4.79%, and it is rising — 6.99% a year ago, 9.26% at the end of FY2082/83, 9.81% now. Every percentage point of bad loans is a loan that is not paying, and a bank with nearly one rupee in ten not paying is a bank whose interest income is under pressure from both sides: fewer good loans earning, and more provisions eating what is earned. That is the mechanism behind the 17.5% fall in net interest income, and it is why the earnings lens cannot be annualised in good conscience.

The rest of the peer read is consistent with that picture. NICA’s market capitalisation is Rs 51.02 billion, 12th of the 19 commercial banks, just behind NMB’s Rs 52.94 billion. Nabil, at Rs 148.54 billion, is nearly three times bigger. The share sits 36.2% of the way up its 52-week range of Rs 302.90 to Rs 411, above both its 120-day average of Rs 330.61 and its 180-day average of Rs 338.54. The one-year yield ShareHub reports is 11.22% — a number that flatters a share whose earnings yield, on the annualised quarter, is 0.98%.

What to take from it

Three things changed since November 2, and one did not. The price rose to Rs 342, the only gain among the seven banks. The rights issue moved from a shareholder approval to a SEBON approval, with the terms now public and the dilution arithmetic now computable. And the earnings lens more than doubled — on a change of basis, from a depressed full year to one annualised quarter, which is why it is shown here as a sensitivity and never as a value. What did not change is the book-value verdict: at 1.98 times book against a 1.84-times peer median, the market still pays a normal bank’s price for NIC Asia’s book, while the earnings behind that book are the weakest in the peer set on every measure that matters — profit share, bad loans, capital headroom, distributable profit.

What would change the verdict is not a price move. It is a second quarter that shows the bad-loan ratio turning, net interest income stabilising, and the rights issue actually opening and being subscribed — in that order. The rights issue’s book-closure and opening dates, when announced, will set the timetable for the dilution. Until then, the honest summary of NIC Asia at Rs 342 is the same as at Rs 331, with the numbers to prove it: a normal price on the book, a crisis price on the earnings, and a capital raise that fixes the second problem by shrinking the first.

Frequently asked questions

What changed for NICA since the November 2 analysis?

Three things: the bank published its first-quarter results (net profit Rs 127.44 million, up 4.33%; bad loans 9.81%), SEBON approved its 50% rights issue (74,587,835 shares at Rs 100), and the price moved from Rs 331 to Rs 342 at the November 13 close. The book-value verdict did not change: at 1.98 times book against a 1.84-times peer median, the market still pays a normal bank’s price for the book.

Why did the earnings lens more than double, from Rs 19.67 to Rs 46.58?

Because the earnings figure changed, not because the bank earned more. On November 2 the lens used Rs 1.22 of full-year FY2082/83 earnings. This follow-up uses Rs 3.35, which is one quarter’s profit multiplied by four. Annualising a single quarter assumes the next three quarters repeat it — and this quarter’s net interest income fell 17.5% while bad loans rose. The lens is shown as a sensitivity, never as a value.

What is the NICA rights issue, and when does it open?

SEBON approved the issue in early November 2026: 74,587,835 shares at Rs 100, one new share for every two held, raising about Rs 7.46 billion, with NIMB Ace Capital as issue manager. No book-closure date and no opening date has been announced yet. The ratio is sometimes written 2:1, meaning two existing shares to one new share — the unit count is exactly half the shares outstanding, which settles it.

Why does a rights issue at Rs 100 cut book value per share?

Because the new shares are sold below book value. Book value is Rs 172.53 a share, so each Rs 100 share brings in less than the book value it shares in. On simple arithmetic, total book value rises from about Rs 25.74 billion to about Rs 33.20 billion, but the share count rises from 149,175,670 to 223,763,505 — so book value per share falls to about Rs 148.36, assuming full subscription and nothing else changing.

How bad is a 9.81% non-performing loan ratio?

It is 1.8 times the commercial-bank sector average of 5.44%, 2.42 times Nabil’s 4.06% and 2.05 times Global IME’s 4.79% — and it is rising, from 6.99% a year ago and 9.26% at the end of FY2082/83. Nearly one rupee in ten lent is not paying, which is the mechanism behind the 17.5% fall in net interest income.

Can NIC Asia pay a dividend for FY2082/83?

Not from its distributable profit, which stands at a negative Rs 15.39 billion — the deepest negative figure of the 20 commercial banks, larger than the sector’s negative Rs 9.07 billion total. No dividend has been proposed for FY2082/83, and the latest dividend remains FY2079/80’s 30.52% from September 2023.

Is Rs 342 cheap or expensive for NICA?

It depends on the lens, and the lenses disagree — that is the point of this article.
