33 points is not Rs 33 — how to read a NEPSE move

On Wednesday, October 28, 2026, the NEPSE index closed at 2,664.06 — up 33.29 points, or 1.27%. The points are not rupees, the percent is not your return, and a rising index does not mean every share rose. Here is what each number in a market report actually says.

On Wednesday, October 28, 2026, the NEPSE index closed at 2,664.06, up 33.29 points, or 1.27%. Every part of that sentence is easy to misread. The points are not rupees. The percent is not a return on your portfolio. And an index that rises does not mean every share rose — that day, 282 shares advanced and 53 declined. This article is a reading lesson for the most quoted number in Nepali market news: what a point is, where the percent comes from, and what the index cannot tell you.

The example is a dated one, and it is labelled as such throughout: October 28, 2026, sixteen days before this article. Nothing in it is presented as today’s market. NEPSE does not trade on Saturdays, so there is no fresh close to use today; the October 28 close is used because it is the verified example the lesson was built on, not because it is current. Every figure in the article comes from the verified close snapshot of that day, published from NEPSE’s official market data, and from ShareSansar’s methodology explainer on the family of NEPSE indices. Signal summary

  • A point is one unit of the index level, not a rupee. The NEPSE index is a value-weighted average: total market capitalisation divided by a base market capitalisation, multiplied by 100. A point is one unit of that level. Nobody pays or receives rupees per point.

  • Compare days in percent, not in points. Points tell you how far the level moved; percent tells you how big that move was relative to where the market stood. Two days with the same points move can be very different days.
  • The index is an average, weighted by market value. Large companies move it more than small ones. A rising index can hide falling shares: on October 28 the index gained 1.27% while 53 shares declined, 13 were unchanged and 282 advanced, out of 348 scrips traded.
  • The index answers one question only: how did the market as a whole move? For how your share moved, only that share’s own price answers.

01. The confusion: one sentence, three misreadings

“NEPSE up 33 points” is the headline every market day, and it invites three mistakes at once. The first is to hear rupees: 33 points sounds like Rs 33, and Rs 33 per share sounds like money made. It is not. Points are units of the index level, and the index level is not a price. The second mistake is to hear a return: up 1.27% sounds like your portfolio gained 1.27%. It did not, unless your portfolio is the whole market in the same proportions. The third mistake is to hear unanimity: an index that rises sounds like a market where everything rose. On October 28, 53 shares declined while the index gained 1.27%. All three mistakes come from the same source — treating the index as if it were a share price. It is not a share price. It is a measure of the market as a whole, and the rest of this article is simply learning to read it as one.

The confusion is understandable, because the index is quoted like a price. It has a level, 2,664.06, and a daily change, +33.29, and a percent, +1.27%, exactly the way a share has a price, a change and a percent. The resemblance is the trap. A share’s price is what one share costs; the index level is what the whole listed market is worth, measured against a fixed starting point. Keep that distinction in mind and the three misreadings fall away one by one.

02. What an index level is

The NEPSE index is a value-weighted average: total market capitalisation divided by a base market capitalisation, multiplied by 100. In plain words, it tracks the combined market value of listed shares against a fixed starting point. When that combined value rises, the index level rises; when it falls, the level falls. A “point” is simply one unit of that level. It is not a price, and nobody pays or receives rupees per point.

The weighting matters. Because the index is weighted by market value, a large company moves it more than a small one. A 1% move in a company worth tens of billions shifts the total market value — and therefore the index — far more than a 1% move in a company worth a few hundred million. That is why the index and your portfolio so often disagree: your portfolio is weighted by what you hold, the index is weighted by what the whole market is worth. The index is the market’s average, in the market’s own proportions.

The level itself, 2,664.06, has no meaning on its own. It is not high or low in any absolute sense; it is only a reading against the base the index started from, and against its own past. A level of 2,664 means the combined market value stands at 26.64 times the base value the index was set against — the “× 100” in the formula is what turns that ratio into a level. What matters day to day is not the level but the change in it, and that change is where the percent comes in.

03. Where the percent comes from

The percent is the points divided by the previous close. That division is the whole reason points alone mislead. On October 28, 33.29 points worked out to 1.27%. But the same 33.29 points at a lower index level would be a bigger percent: if the index stood at 1,000, 33.29 points would be 3.33%. That 1,000 figure is an illustration only — the index was not at 1,000 on October 28 — and it is included for exactly that reason: it shows why two days with the same points move can be very different days.

Think of it the other way round. A 100-point day when the index stands at 2,600 is a move of about 3.8%. A 30-point day when the index stands at 1,000 is a move of 3%. The 100-point day looks more than three times as big in points, and it is barely bigger in percent. Points measure distance; percent measures size relative to where you started. A market report that gives you only the points has told you the distance and hidden the size.

04. What the index does not tell you

The index is an average, weighted by market value, so large companies move it more than small ones. A rising index can hide falling shares, and a falling index can hide rising ones. October 28 is the example: the index gained 1.27% while 53 shares declined. The index answers “how did the market as a whole move?” — for “how did my share move?”, only that share’s own price answers.

The count that fills the gap is breadth: how many shares advanced, stayed flat and declined. On October 28, 282 advanced, 13 were unchanged and 53 declined, out of 348 scrips traded. Breadth is the number that tells you whether the index’s move was broad or narrow — whether most of the market moved with it, or a few heavy shares carried it. This article does not re-argue breadth; the November 12 article on this site, how to read market breadth on NEPSE, covers it in depth, including the week of November 3–6, when the index rose on days most shares fell. Read that piece for the count; this one is about the level.

There is a second thing the index does not tell you: direction tomorrow. A 1.27% day says the market’s combined value rose that day. It says nothing about the next session, and nothing about any single company. The index is a thermometer, not a forecast — it measures the market’s temperature today, and a thermometer has never predicted the weather.

05. The habit worth building

So the habit worth building is simple: compare days in percent, not in points. When the evening news says the index rose 33 points, ask the next question — 33 points out of what? At 2,664, it is 1.27%, a solid day. At 1,000, it would be 3.33%, a very big day. At 5,000, it would be 0.67%, a quiet one. Same points, three different days. The percent is the number that lets you compare a day in 2026 with a day in 2021, when the index stood somewhere else entirely.

And the second habit: check the breadth beside the index. A 1.27% rise with 282 advancers and 53 decliners is a broad rise. The same 1.27% with 53 advancers and 282 decliners would be a narrow one, carried by a few heavy shares while most of the market fell. The index tells you the market moved; the breadth tells you whether the market moved with it. Both numbers come from the same close, every trading day, on NEPSE’s official market data — and both are worth the ten seconds it takes to read them.

What to take from it

A point is one unit of the index level, not a rupee. The NEPSE index tracks the combined market value of listed shares against a fixed base, multiplied by 100, weighted by market value. The percent is the points divided by the previous close — on October 28, 2026, 33.29 points was 1.27%, and the same points at an index of 1,000 would be 3.33%, which is why days are compared in percent, not in points. The index is an average: it rose 1.27% that day while 53 shares declined, so it answers how the market as a whole moved, and only each share’s own price answers how your share moved. Read the percent, then read the breadth beside it — and you have read the day.

Frequently asked questions

If the index rises 33 points, did I make Rs 33 per share?

No. Points are not rupees and not per-share amounts. Your gain or loss depends on the shares you hold and their own price moves. A 33-point rise in the index means the market’s combined value rose; what it means for your portfolio depends entirely on which shares you own.

Why do two news reports of the same day show different point moves?

Rounding in the reported close and previous close can shift the last decimal; the percent move is the more stable comparison. On October 28, for example, the reported previous close and the reported change differ by 0.01 from the reported close through rounding in the source figures — which is why this article prints only the snapshot’s own close, change and percent, and never a computed previous-close level.

Is a 100-point day always bigger than a 30-point day?

Not necessarily. Compare the percents: 100 points on a high index can be a smaller move than 30 points on a low one. At an index of 2,664, 100 points is about 3.8%; at an index of 1,000, 30 points is 3%. Points measure distance; percent measures size relative to where the market stood.

What is a NEPSE point, exactly?

One unit of the index level. The NEPSE index is total market capitalisation divided by a base market capitalisation, multiplied by 100, and weighted by market value. When the combined market value rises, the level rises; a point is one unit of that level. It is not a price, and nobody pays or receives rupees per point.

Why did the index rise on October 28 when 53 shares fell?

Because the index is a value-weighted average, not a headcount. Large companies move it more than small ones, so a few heavy shares rising can lift the index while many smaller shares fall. That day 282 shares advanced and 53 declined, so the rise was broad — but the mechanism is the same on narrow days. The November 12 article on how to read market breadth covers the count in depth.

Is an index level of 2,664 high or low?

Neither, on its own. The level is only a reading against the base the index started from and against its own past. What matters day to day is the change in the level, and the change is compared in percent, not in points — because the same points mean a different percent at a different level.

Where do I check the index each day?

NEPSE’s official market data is the source — the daily close, the points change and the percent, with the advancers, unchanged and decliners beside them. This site’s daily market summary and market overview carry the same close. Read the percent first, then the breadth beside it.
