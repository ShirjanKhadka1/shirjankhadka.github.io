Three forecasters, three Nepals — and none of them is the economy households actually live in

The World Bank says 3.7%, the IMF says 3.0% and the ADB said 4.1%. The 1.1-point spread is not a rounding error: it is a map of what the August floods broke, what reconstruction will count as growth, and why the GDP headline misses the money Nepali households actually live on.

By Nepse Decode Research Desk · Kathmandu · October 28, 2026 · 12 min read
Facebook X LinkedIn WhatsApp Viber

In the space of three weeks, three institutions priced Nepal's economy three different ways. The World Bank, on October 6, cut its forecast for fiscal year 2026/27 to 3.7%. The International Monetary Fund, on October 13, put Nepal at 3.0% in 2026. The Asian Development Bank had already, in its September outlook, cut its fiscal-year 2026/27 forecast to 4.1% — a number this blog covered at the time, and which is not re-reported as news here. The gap between the highest and the lowest of the three is 1.1 percentage points. For an economy growing at roughly 3–4%, that is not a rounding error. It is nearly a third of the growth rate itself, and it is the story: the spread tells you what the August floods actually broke, how much of next year's "growth" will just be rebuilding, and why the headline number understates what Nepali households earn.

The first thing to notice is that these numbers are not strictly comparable, and anyone who averages them is inventing a fourth forecast nobody published. They were published weeks apart, on different reporting bases, from different models, with different treatments of the flood shock. The IMF's own country assessments, for example, run on a fiscal-year basis that differs from the World Economic Outlook's presentation — which is why this article quotes only the WEO's 2026 figure for the IMF and does not mix the two sets together. The honest comparison is not "the average is 3.6%". It is: three serious institutions, looking at the same flood, could not agree within a full percentage point. That disagreement is information.

What the spread is actually measuring: reconstruction arithmetic

Start with what the floods destroyed, because the forecasters are all, in different ways, pricing the same wreckage. The World Bank's update is blunt about the channels: energy is the most critical, then transportation, trade and tourism, with agriculture and banking/insurance carrying lower aggregate impact. The energy numbers explain why. The floods affected 12 hydropower projects — seven operational, with 256.1 MW of combined capacity, and five under construction, with 395.02 MW — plus a 25 MW solar facility, while damage to transmission infrastructure disrupted a further 149.6 MW. The total affected capacity comes to about 430.7 MW, roughly 10.6% of Nepal's installed hydropower and solar capacity in fiscal year 2025/26. One river system, the Bhotekoshi–Trishuli corridor, took out a tenth of the country's generating base in a single day, August 26. Before any forecaster's model runs, that is the structural fact: Nepal's growth is exposed to a single corridor's hydropower in a way that no diversified economy is.

The human and material scale sits behind the percentages, and it should be stated plainly. Nepal's National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Authority has confirmed 1,455 people dead and 5,285 missing. The Rapid Disaster Needs Assessment prepared by the National Planning Commission and the disaster authority puts physical damage at Rs 274.48 billion and total losses and damages at Rs 408.28 billion. Xinhua's summary of that assessment puts the losses at about USD 2.69 billion and the reconstruction and recovery need at about USD 4.77 billion. Now read the World Bank's recovery path again: growth slows to 3.7% in FY27, then recovers to 5.2% in FY28 as "reconstruction and rehabilitation activities begin supporting economic activity". That 5.2% is not a boom. A large part of it is the arithmetic of rebuilding what was destroyed: GDP measures the flow of new production, and rebuilding a powerhouse counts as production, while the destruction of the old powerhouse — a loss of stock, not flow — never subtracted from GDP in the first place. An economy can therefore post a handsome growth rate while its people are merely getting back to where they were. Any investor who reads the FY28 recovery as prosperity without asking what was rebuilt, and for whom, is reading the wrong number.

Why GDP understates what households earn: the GNP gap

There is a second, quieter distortion in the headline, and it runs in the opposite direction. GDP counts what is produced inside Nepal's borders. It does not count what Nepali workers earn abroad and send home. Remittances are transfers, not domestic production, so they never enter GDP at all — a point Nepali commentators have had to keep correcting, because the "remittances are 25% of GDP" shorthand invites exactly the wrong inference. What remittances do enter is gross national income — the GNP concept: GDP plus net income from abroad. For Nepal, that addition is enormous. Official remittances run to around a third of GDP, according to figures reported by the Nepal Economic Forum. The same source, reporting Nepal Rastra Bank data for 2025/26, puts gross domestic savings — income generated at home minus what the country consumes — at just 9.71% of GDP, against gross national savings of 44.77% once money earned abroad is included. The roughly 35-point gap between those two savings rates is, in substance, remittances: income that nothing Nepal makes or sells has generated.

Put the two distortions together and the headline growth rate is doing double duty badly. On the flood side, it will flatter the recovery, because rebuilding counts and destruction does not. On the household side, it understates the economy people live in, because the single largest source of household income — money earned abroad — is invisible to it by definition. A forecaster arguing about whether growth is 3.0% or 4.1% is arguing about a measure that misses both the damage and the income. That is why the spread matters more than any single number in it: it reveals how much judgement, not measurement, sits inside the headline.

What to watch instead of the headline rate

None of this means the forecasts are useless. It means they are useful as a map of uncertainty, not as a number to trade on. For anyone watching Nepal's economy — investor, policymaker or saver — the honest dashboard for the next year is not a single growth print. It is five things, each directly tied to a number above:

  • Megawatts restored, not rupees announced. The 430.7 MW affected is a physical fact; recovery should be counted in capacity back online along the Bhotekoshi–Trishuli corridor, not in press-release totals.
  • Reconstruction disbursement against the USD 4.77 billion need. The gap between assessed need and money actually spent is where the FY28 recovery either happens or stalls.
  • Corridor and trade flows. The World Bank's transport and trade channels are the second drag after energy; goods moving again is the signal, not the forecast revision.
  • Remittances against domestic savings. If national savings stay near 44.77% while domestic savings stay near 9.71%, the economy's growth is still being imported — and still hostage to labour markets abroad.
  • Inflation. The ADB's September outlook already raised its inflation forecast to 5.3% for the fiscal year; a flood-driven price shock would do more damage to households than a point of growth either way.

The point of the three forecasters is not that one of them is right. The World Bank's post-flood downgrade, the IMF's sober 3.0% and the ADB's earlier 4.1% are all defensible readings of an economy that has just lost a tenth of its power capacity and a corridor to a single day's flood. The point is what their disagreement exposes: an economy whose growth number is moved by one river valley, flattered by its own rebuilding, and blind to its people's largest income. Watch the megawatts, the money actually spent, and the money sent home. The headline rate will follow those — eventually, and with a lag that should make anyone suspicious of trading on it today.

Frequently asked questions

What are the three growth forecasts for Nepal?

The World Bank (Nepal Development Update, October 6, 2026) projects 3.7% in FY27, recovering to 5.2% in FY28. The IMF (World Economic Outlook, October 13, 2026) projects 3.0% in 2026. The ADB (Asian Development Outlook, September 2026) projected 4.1% for FY2026/27 — previously covered by this blog and shown here only for comparison. The government's own target for the current fiscal year is 7%.

Why do the forecasts differ by more than a percentage point?

They were published weeks apart, on different reporting bases, from different models, with different treatments of the August 26 flood shock. The IMF's World Economic Outlook presentation also differs from the fiscal-year basis of its country assessments, so the two IMF sets must not be mixed. The spread reflects genuine uncertainty about the flood's impact, not an error by any one institution.

What did the August floods actually destroy?

According to the World Bank's update: 12 hydropower projects (seven operational at 256.1 MW, five under construction at 395.02 MW), a 25 MW solar facility, and transmission damage disrupting a further 149.6 MW — about 430.7 MW in total, roughly 10.6% of FY2025/26 installed hydropower and solar capacity. The Rapid Disaster Needs Assessment puts physical damage at Rs 274.48 billion and total losses and damages at Rs 408.28 billion; the disaster authority confirms 1,455 dead and 5,285 missing.

Is reconstruction really "growth"?

In GDP accounting, yes — and that is the problem. GDP measures the flow of new production. Rebuilding a powerhouse counts as production, while destroying the old one does not subtract from GDP, because the destroyed asset was stock, not flow. A recovery driven by rebuilding can therefore look strong while households are only returning to where they were before the flood.

Why is Nepal's GNP higher than its GDP?

GNP (gross national income) equals GDP plus net income from abroad. Remittances — money Nepali workers earn abroad and send home — are transfers, not domestic production, so they never enter GDP. But they do enter national income. With official remittances around a third of GDP, Nepal's national income is substantially larger than its domestic product, which is why GDP alone understates what households earn.

Should investors trade on the forecast spread?

No. The spread is a measure of uncertainty, not a signal. This article makes no price prediction and no recommendation about any security. The honest signals to watch are physical and financial facts — megawatts restored, reconstruction money actually spent, corridor trade flows, remittance trends and inflation — not the next revision to a headline rate.
