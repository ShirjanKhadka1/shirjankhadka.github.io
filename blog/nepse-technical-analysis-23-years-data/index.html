NEPSE Technical Analysis: What 23 Years of Market Data Reveal About Trend vs Mean-Reversion Trading

I backtested 19 rule-based trading systems on every NEPSE session from 2003 to 2026. The verdict is unambiguous: this is a trend market — momentum systems beat buy & hold with smaller drawdowns, and mean-reversion lost money.

Most NEPSE trading advice is opinion dressed as analysis. "Buy the dip," "the RSI is oversold," "support will hold" — these get repeated because they sound disciplined, not because anyone checked. So I checked. I ran 19 rule-based trading systems across 5,333 NEPSE index sessions from 2003 to 2026, with identical position sizing and cost assumptions, and scored every system on CAGR, drawdown, and win rate. This article is the plain-English summary of what the data actually says.

The experiment

The dataset is the NEPSE index daily series from 2003 through 2026 — two full market cycles, covering the post-conflict bull run, the 2015–16 earthquake shock, the 2020–21 liquidity-driven mania, and the long drawdown that followed. Each of the 19 systems was defined as a fixed, mechanical rule set: no discretion, no cherry-picked entries, no hindsight. Systems fell into three families:

  • Trend / momentum: RSI trend-ride, EMA crossover, Donchian channel breakout, Bollinger Band breakout.
  • Mean-reversion: RSI(2/14) oversold buying, stochastics, support-level buying, engulfing and hammer candle patterns traded standalone.
  • Benchmark: buy & hold, for the honest baseline every system must beat.

Two facts matter more than the ranking itself. First, every trend/momentum system beat buy & hold with materially smaller maximum drawdowns — they sat out the market's worst phases instead of riding them down. Second, every mean-reversion system lost money over the full window. Not underperformed — lost.

"Cheap" on the NEPSE keeps getting cheaper. Oversold is not a buy signal here — it's a description of a market with no bid.

Why mean-reversion fails on the NEPSE

In deep, two-sided markets, sell-offs get absorbed by institutional bids and prices snap back — that's the physics mean-reversion exploits. The NEPSE doesn't have that physics. Its drawdowns are driven by liquidity withdrawal: margin calls, circuit-breaker cascades, and retail capitulation with no institutional bid underneath. When the bid disappears, "support levels" are lines on a chart, not orders in a book. Buying RSI < 30 into that structure is catching a falling safe, not a falling knife — and the backtest proves it, year after year.

Trend systems win for the mirror-image reason. NEPSE bull phases are liquidity-driven and persistent: once credit expansion or policy easing turns the flow positive, it stays positive for months. A Donchian breakout or RSI trend-ride simply boards that flow early and rides it, then steps aside when the regime breaks. It doesn't predict — it reacts, which is exactly what a thin, flow-driven market rewards.

The regime filter that does the heavy lifting

The single highest-leverage rule in the whole study isn't an entry signal — it's the SMA200 regime filter. The plan only takes breakout and trend entries when the NEPSE index trades above its 200-day simple moving average. Below it, the market is classified as CHOP or BEAR: stand aside, reduce size, protect capital. Most of the trend systems' drawdown advantage comes from this one filter, because the NEPSE's worst losses cluster in long below-SMA200 regimes where mean-reversion traders keep buying dips.

Current read (data through September 18, 2026): NEPSE 2,647.22 vs SMA200 ≈ 2,690 — the market sits in CHOP regime, below the filter line. The study's rule says: wait for a decisive breakout above ~2,690 before committing size. Patience is the position.

From study to trading plan

Findings are useless without execution rules, so the study converts directly into a rules-based plan:

  1. Regime first: SMA200 filter decides whether you're allowed to trade trend systems at all.
  2. Entries: breakout and trend-continuation signals only — no oversold buying, no support catching.
  3. Risk: 1% of capital per trade, maximum three concurrent positions.
  4. Loss brake: two consecutive losses trigger a one-week pause. No exceptions.
  5. Forbidden: averaging down, leverage, and revenge trading — the three behaviors the backtest punishes most.
  6. Review: journal every trade; Sunday review of execution quality, not P&L.

This is the plan I trade against now. It doesn't promise returns — no honest backtest can — but it replaces emotion with procedure, which is the actual edge a retail trader can own.

A note on seasonality

A separate sub-index test across 11 NEPSE sectors found that July showed a positive median return in every sector tested. Interesting — but the study treats it as a descriptive observation, not a standalone signal. Calendar effects without a regime and trend filter behind them are exactly the kind of pattern that backtests well and trades poorly. File it under "context," not "strategy."

Limitations

Honest caveats. The backtest runs on index-level data, so single-scrip behavior (liquidity, circuits, promoter overhang) will differ — a follow-up study on 15 liquid scrips is in progress. Transaction costs are modeled as fixed assumptions, and real slippage in thin NEPSE sessions can exceed models. Finally, 23 years of history describe the market's structure, not its future: regimes change, and any system needs re-validation when they do.
