NRB just made it easier to spend dollars abroad, while guarding every dollar that leaves

One circular raised nearly every forex cap two to five times. The central bank is betting record reserves can handle it.

By Shirjan Khadka · Founder, Nepse Decode, Kathmandu · October 7, 2026 · 5 min read
Facebook X LinkedIn WhatsApp

Nepal Rastra Bank wants you to spend more dollars abroad. On October 5, 2026, the central bank issued the widest single liberalization of foreign-exchange rules in recent memory, raising nearly every cap on taking money out of the country by two to five times. The move arrives at a moment of unusual confidence: foreign-exchange reserves sit at a record Rs 3.946 trillion, enough to cover 18.8 months of imports. The circular does not say what happens if the dollars start flowing out faster than remittances flow in. That is the tension sitting underneath every number in Circular No. 2/2083-84, which amends the Unified Circular 2082.

Start with the numbers that touch ordinary life. The annual exchange facility that banks and financial institutions can provide to Nepali citizens rises from USD 15,000 to USD 30,000. The per-transaction cap moves by the same multiple, from USD 15,000 to USD 30,000, and payments to Indian entities per transaction rise from INR 40 lakh to INR 60 lakh. For anyone paying for education, medical treatment, or travel abroad, the ceiling has effectively doubled overnight.

The pattern is deliberate. Retail-facing caps, the ones used by students, patients, and travelers, roughly double. Commercial caps, the ones used by airlines and telecom operators, jump fivefold: advance payments for aircraft engines and spare parts go from USD 100,000 to USD 500,000, and Nepali telecom companies leasing satellite capacity from foreign providers can now pay up to USD 500,000 a year, also up from USD 100,000. The central bank is clearing bottlenecks for businesses that earn or save foreign exchange, while giving households more room at the same time.

The most structural change is buried in the fine print. A-class commercial banks can now issue foreign-currency bank guarantees and standby letters of credit for foreign loans taken by firms in the IT industry and the energy sector, two sectors that were previously outside the privileged list of commercial agriculture, manufacturing, infrastructure, and tourism. For hydropower developers and IT companies trying to borrow abroad, this single clause matters more than any of the headline numbers: it lowers the cost of the guarantee that makes the loan possible.

But this is not a free-for-all. The same circular tightens the plumbing around the wider taps. Importers must now pay at least 10% in advance before shipment and settle the full amount before goods clear customs. Draft and telegraphic-transfer rules get stricter documentation. The message is consistent with how NRB has always operated: wider doors, but every dollar is still counted at the threshold.

Why now? The answer sits in the reserves data. With USD 25.84 billion in the vault, the central bank can afford generosity it could not have afforded three years ago. Remittance inflows remain strong, up 21.2% in Shrawan, and the current account is in surplus. Liberalization from a position of strength is very different from liberalization under pressure, and NRB clearly wants this read as the former.

The open question is behavioral. Higher caps do not force anyone to spend more dollars; they simply remove the friction for those who already want to. If outbound payments for education, health, travel, and imports accelerate while remittance growth cools, the same reserves that justified this circular become the constraint on the next one. For now, the tap is open. Whether the bucket refills as fast as it drains is the number to watch in the coming quarters.
