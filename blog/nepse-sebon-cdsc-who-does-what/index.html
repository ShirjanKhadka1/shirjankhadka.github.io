NEPSE, SEBON, CDSC — three names, three jobs: who does what when you buy a share

Buy one share on NEPSE and three names show up: NEPSE, SEBON and CDSC. One runs the market, one referees it, and one keeps the record — and mixing them up is the most common confusion a new investor in Nepal has. Here is each institution’s one job, how a single buy travels through all three, and who to call when something goes wrong.

Buy one share on NEPSE and three names show up on the paperwork: NEPSE, SEBON and CDSC. New investors mix them up constantly. Is SEBON the market? Does CDSC set prices? Do you complain to NEPSE when your Demat account is wrong? The answer is simpler than the alphabet soup suggests. Each institution has exactly one job, and your share passes through all three: NEPSE is the market where the trade happens, SEBON is the regulator that watches it, and CDSC is the record keeper that settles it and holds the ownership record. One sentence each, and the whole system falls into place.

This article is a reading lesson, not a forecast and not investment advice. Every fact in it comes from the three institutions’ own official pages, read on November 13, 2026: NEPSE’s Introduction page, SEBON’s About page, and CDSC’s Introduction page. No portal, no secondary source, no number that moves. The market reopens today, Friday, November 13, after the Tihar break of November 9–12 — a good day to learn the plumbing, because the first session back is the first chance to watch it work.

01. The confusion: three names on one trade

Start with the confusion itself, because it is nearly universal. A new investor places a buy order, and the confirmations, the contract note and the Demat statement between them mention three different institutions. The natural questions follow: which one is the market, which one is in charge, and which one actually holds my shares? The confusion is understandable — the three names are always together, and they were all born within a few years of each other. SEBON was established on 7 June 1993. NEPSE was converted from the Securities Exchange Center in 1993. The trading floor opened on 13 January 1994. CDSC followed in 2010. Four dates, three institutions, one trade — and the rest of this article is simply sorting out which name does which job.

The sorting rule is one sentence per institution, and it never changes: NEPSE runs the market, SEBON referees it, CDSC keeps the record. If a question is about where a trade happens, the answer is NEPSE. If it is about who makes and enforces the rules, the answer is SEBON. If it is about where the shares sit after the trade, the answer is CDSC. Hold those three sentences and the rest is detail.

02. NEPSE: the market

The Nepal Stock Exchange is where buying and selling happens. Its own stated objective, on its Introduction page, is to impart free marketability and liquidity to government and corporate securities by facilitating transactions in its trading floor through its members and market intermediaries, such as brokers and market makers. In plain words: to make sure a share can be bought and sold. When your buy order meets a sell order at the same price, that match happens on NEPSE.

NEPSE’s history is the history of the market itself. The Securities Exchange Center was established in 1976. Then, in the government’s own words on the same page, the Nepal Government, under a program initiated to reform capital markets, converted the Securities Exchange Center into the Nepal Stock Exchange in 1993 — and NEPSE opened its trading floor on 13 January 1994. Before the conversion, the Center had been the only capital markets institution undertaking brokering, underwriting, managing public issues and market making for government bonds; after it, the jobs split apart, which is exactly why there are three institutions to explain today.

The one thing NEPSE does not do is decide what your share is worth. Prices are set by the buyers and sellers matching on the exchange, not by the exchange itself. NEPSE provides the floor, the matching and the rules of the trading session; the market provides the price. That distinction is the whole point of the next two sections: the price comes from the market, the rules come from SEBON, and the record comes from CDSC.

03. SEBON: the regulator

The Securities Board of Nepal is the referee. It was established by the Government of Nepal on 7 June 1993 as the apex regulator of the securities market, and it regulates the market under the Securities Act, 2006. Apex means at the top: SEBON licenses the stock exchange itself. Every other institution in this article operates under a licence SEBON issued.

SEBON’s functions, from its own About page, are the list worth memorising. It issues licences to operate the stock exchange, and to stock brokers, dealers, merchant bankers and fund managers, and to depository companies, depository participants and credit rating agencies. It registers the securities of public companies. It regulates and systematises the issue, transfer, sale and exchange of registered securities. And it takes necessary action to prevent insider trading and other offences relating to transactions in securities, in order to protect the interest of investors. That last phrase is the job description in miniature: SEBON exists to protect investors, by licensing who may operate and acting when the rules are broken.

One line to remember: SEBON watches the whole trade, but SEBON itself never buys or sells. It is a regulator, not a market participant — there is no SEBON trading desk, no SEBON portfolio, no SEBON order in the market. When SEBON acts, it acts by rule: licensing, registering, regulating, and taking action against offences. That is why the “who to call” section below sends broker problems to SEBON: the broker operates on SEBON’s licence, so the regulator is the desk that can hold it to account.

SEBON has been the most visible of the three institutions lately, and readers of this site will recognise the name from its recent actions rather than its founding: the anti-money-laundering directive in October, the Information Technology Policy passed by its board in early November, and the intraday-trading draft regulation that would bring same-day trading to NEPSE. Those are all SEBON doing its job — writing and changing the rules of the market. None of them changes the three-way split this article describes; they are the regulator regulating.

04. CDSC: the record keeper

CDS and Clearing Limited is where the record lives. NEPSE established it in 2010 as the central depository for equities, bonds and warrants held in dematerialised form — that is, as electronic entries rather than paper certificates. It handles the clearing and settlement of securities traded on NEPSE under a T+2 settlement cycle, and it is responsible for the safekeeping, deposit, withdrawal and transfer of ownership of securities. CDSC is jointly owned by the Government of Nepal and NEPSE, and it operates under the Securities Act 2063 and the Company Act 2063.

Your shares sit in a Demat account — a beneficiary account — opened with a depository participant, a DP, usually your bank or broker. CDSC is the ledger behind it. The official page puts it plainly: investors are linked to the depository through the Beneficiary Accounts or Demat Account opened with depository participants. You deal with your DP; your DP deals with CDSC; CDSC holds the record. When you sell, the shares leave your Demat through CDSC; when you buy, they arrive the same way, two trading days later.

CDSC is the youngest of the three by seventeen years, and the least visible, because its work is invisible when it works: shares arriving on time, ownership records matching, settlement completing without a phone call. Its age shows in the chart above — 16 years against 33 for the other two — but its job is as old as the market’s need for a trustworthy record. Before dematerialisation, that record was paper; now it is CDSC’s ledger, and the T+2 cycle is its timetable.

05. How one buy travels

Put together, a single purchase runs like this. You place the order with your broker. NEPSE matches your buy with someone’s sell — that is the trade, and it happens on the exchange. CDSC settles the trade on T+2: the money moves one way, the shares move into your Demat the other way, two trading days after the trade. And SEBON stands over the whole chain, licensing the broker, setting the rules, and taking action when the rules are broken.

The T+2 part is the one that surprises new investors most, so it is worth stating plainly: the shares do not reach your Demat on the day you buy. Trade day is T. Settlement is T+2 — two trading days later. If you buy on Friday, the shares arrive on Tuesday, because Saturday and Sunday are not trading days. The money leaves your account on the same timetable, in the other direction. Nothing has gone wrong when the shares are not there the next morning; that is the cycle working as designed. The broker takes your order, the exchange matches it, the depository settles it — and each step belongs to a different institution, which is why knowing the three names matters.

Notice what each institution does not touch. NEPSE never holds your shares — CDSC does. CDSC never sets your price — the market does, on NEPSE. SEBON never holds your shares and never sets your price — it licenses the people who do the holding and the matching, and writes the rules they follow. The split is the protection: no single institution both runs the trade and keeps the record of it, and no single institution both makes the rules and profits from them.

06. Who to call when something goes wrong

This is the part worth memorising, because it saves days of being passed between offices. A problem with your broker — an order that did not go through, a charge you do not recognise, a broker that has stopped responding — is SEBON’s territory: the broker operates on SEBON’s licence, and SEBON’s function is to protect investors against offences in securities transactions. Start with SEBON.

A problem with your Demat account — shares missing, a transfer that did not arrive — starts with your depository participant, your DP, the bank or broker where you opened the account. Your DP is your link to the depository; CDSC is the ledger behind it. Go to the DP first, because the DP holds your account relationship; CDSC is the record the DP checks against.

A halted stock, a trading question, or a question about a session itself goes to the exchange, NEPSE — it runs the trading floor. And if the problem is that the shares you bought yesterday are not in your Demat yet, check the calendar before calling anyone: settlement runs on T+2, so shares reach your Demat two trading days after the trade, not on the trade day itself. The most common “something is wrong” in a new investor’s first week is the cycle working exactly as designed.

07. What each one does not do

The fastest way to test whether the split has sunk in is the list of things each institution does not do. SEBON does not buy or sell shares, and it does not set prices. NEPSE does not decide what a share is worth, does not hold your shares, and does not regulate the brokers — it is itself licensed by SEBON. CDSC does not decide prices, does not take your orders, and does not license anyone. Your broker takes your orders, but your broker is not any of the three — it is a licensed intermediary, operating under SEBON’s licence, trading on NEPSE’s floor, settling through CDSC.

Two more things the split does not include. The Nepal Rastra Bank regulates banks, not the securities market — it touches the market indirectly, through margin-lending rules and the banks that are also DPs, but it is not one of the three institutions in this article. And the “only stock exchange” claim is not made here: NEPSE’s own Introduction page does not state that it is Nepal’s only stock exchange, so this article does not say it either. What the page does say is that the Securities Exchange Center, before its conversion, was the only capital markets institution doing the whole job — which is the history, not a claim about today.

What to take from it

Three names, three jobs. NEPSE is the market: it matches your buy with someone’s sell, on a trading floor that opened on 13 January 1994. SEBON is the regulator: established 7 June 1993, licensing the exchange, the brokers, the depository and everyone else in the chain, and acting against insider trading to protect investors — while never buying or selling a share itself. CDSC is the record keeper: established by NEPSE in 2010, holding the ownership record in dematerialised form and settling every trade on T+2. Your order travels broker, to NEPSE, to CDSC, under SEBON’s rules. When something goes wrong, the name on the problem tells you the desk: broker to SEBON, Demat to your DP and CDSC, trading to NEPSE. That is the whole system, and it fits in one paragraph — which is why the confusion was never about complexity. It was about three names arriving together, before anyone said which one does what.

Frequently asked questions

Is SEBON the stock market?

No. SEBON regulates the market; NEPSE is the market. SEBON was established by the Government of Nepal on 7 June 1993 as the apex regulator of the securities market, and it licenses NEPSE itself. SEBON never buys or sells shares.

Does CDSC decide share prices?

No. CDSC keeps the ownership record and settles trades under the T+2 cycle. Prices are set by buyers and sellers matching on NEPSE. CDSC holds the record of who owns what; it takes no view on what anything is worth.

I bought shares yesterday. Why are they not in my Demat yet?

Settlement runs on T+2: shares reach your Demat two trading days after the trade, not on the trade day itself. If you bought on Friday, they arrive on Tuesday, because Saturday and Sunday are not trading days. Check the calendar before calling anyone.

My broker is not responding. Who do I complain to?

SEBON. It licenses brokers, dealers and merchant bankers, and its functions include acting against offences in securities transactions to protect investors. The broker operates on SEBON’s licence, so the regulator is the desk that can hold it to account.

My Demat account shows shares missing. Who fixes that?

Start with your depository participant — the bank or broker where you opened the Demat account. Investors are linked to the depository through their DP, and CDSC is the ledger behind it. Your DP checks your account against CDSC’s record.

When did NEPSE start trading?

The government converted the Securities Exchange Center into the Nepal Stock Exchange in 1993, and NEPSE opened its trading floor on 13 January 1994. The Securities Exchange Center itself was established in 1976.

Is NEPSE Nepal’s only stock exchange?

This article does not claim that, because NEPSE’s own Introduction page does not state it. What the page does say is that the Securities Exchange Center, before its 1993 conversion, was the only capital markets institution undertaking brokering, underwriting, public issues and market making — that is the history of the predecessor, not a claim about today.
