FPO in Nepal explained — when a listed company sells new shares to the public

An FPO is a company already listed on NEPSE selling fresh shares to the public — not an IPO, and not a right share. Nepal Investment Bank charged Rs 601 a share for its FPO; Unique Nepal Laghubitta charged Rs 100. Here is what an FPO is, why companies issue one, and how allotment works.

By Nepse Decode Research Desk · Kathmandu · November 15, 2026 · 21 min read
Facebook X LinkedIn WhatsApp Viber

Most investors know the IPO: a company comes to the market for the first time and sells shares to the public at Rs 100. Fewer know what happens afterwards. A company that is already listed can come back to the public and sell more shares. That second sale is the FPO — Further Public Offering — and it is where a surprising number of beginners get confused, because it looks like an IPO, behaves a bit like a right share, and is neither.

Every figure in this article is historical, and each one is labelled with its date. The examples are FPOs from 2072 to 2080, reported in full at the time by ShareSansar and NepseKhabar; the rules are SEBON’s Securities Issue and Allotment Guidelines 2074, the same instrument verified live at sebon.gov.np on 25 October 2026 for the IPO-allotment article. No FPO is open for application as this article is published — SEBON’s approved-issue list and ShareHub showed no open FPO at the last check, on 14 November 2026 — so nothing here is an offer, and no price in it is current. The lesson is the mechanism, taught on cases that already happened.

01. The confusion: three ways to buy new shares, one word each

The confusion is understandable, because the three ways a company sells you new shares sound alike and two of them even look alike on MeroShare. An IPO is a company’s first sale, before listing. A right share is a sale to people who already hold the company’s shares, in proportion to what they hold. An FPO sits between them: a listed company, selling to the general public, exactly like an IPO — except the company is already on the market, its shares already have a trading price, and the FPO price is usually set with that market price in mind. Hold the three apart and the rest of the article is bookkeeping: who the money goes to, what the price is made of, and who gets how many shares.

The first thing to hold on to is the money’s direction. In an FPO, as in an IPO, your money funds the company. When you buy the same company’s shares on NEPSE the next day, your money goes to the investor who sold them to you. That is the difference between the primary market and the secondary market, and it is the whole point of an FPO: the company is raising capital, not the shareholders cashing out. The primary-versus-secondary explainer covers that split in full; this article stays with the FPO itself.

02. What an FPO is

An FPO is a fresh issue of shares by a company whose shares already trade on NEPSE. The company creates new shares and offers them to the general public, usually through MeroShare, exactly the way an IPO is applied for. The money goes to the company. That is the definition, and the two contrasts that define it: unlike an IPO, the issuer is already listed; unlike a right share, the buyer does not need to already hold the company’s shares.

Two details from Nepal’s biggest FPO still matter. First, SEBON barred Nepal Investment Bank’s own promoter shareholders, board members and their immediate families from applying to its FPO — an FPO is for the general public, and the regulator said so in that case. Second, once the shares were listed, they traded freely. The three-year lock-in that applies to promoter and project-affected-local quotas in an IPO does not apply to public FPO shares: once listed, they are ordinary shares in the market.

03. Why a company issues one

The most common reason in Nepal is a rule, not an ambition. Nepal Rastra Bank requires banks and financial institutions to keep at least 30% of their shares with the general public, a promoter-to-public ratio of 70:30. Companies that fall short of that line issue FPOs to get there. Nepal Investment Bank is the textbook case: before its FPO, promoters held 80% of the bank and the public 20%. The FPO moved the structure to 70:30 — that post-FPO ratio is computed from the bank’s own figures, and the arithmetic is shown in the sources section. Nepal SBI Bank did the same, from 70.12:29.88 to 70:30, and so did Unique Nepal Laghubitta in 2080, from 94:6 to 70:30.

Other FPOs raise money for projects. Butwal Power Company’s FPO, approved by SEBON at Rs 501 a share, was meant to fund the Kabeli-A (37.6 MW) and Nyadi (30 MW) hydropower projects — 40,81,000 units, worth Rs 2.04 arba, managed by NMB Capital. The company’s own story is told in full in the Butwal Power company analysis, which also records that the FPO price was Rs 100 of face value plus Rs 401 of premium. The reason an FPO exists is the first question to ask before applying: an FPO that lifts public holding to 30% is a compliance exercise, while one that funds a named project can be judged on that project.

04. The worked example: NIBL, 2072

Nepal Investment Bank issued 90,69,388 units at Rs 601 each from Falgun 11 to 14, 2072 — an issue worth Rs 5.45 arba. The price is the part that surprises people who expect every public issue to cost Rs 100. The face value was Rs 100; the other Rs 501 was premium, and that premium went straight into the bank’s reserves, which rose by Rs 4.54 arba. Paid-up capital rose from Rs 6.34 arba to Rs 7.25 arba. Demand was roughly nine times the issue: about Rs 48 arba was applied by about 2.30 lakh applicants — the figure was approximate at reporting time, and the final collection could vary, so this article keeps the “about” the original report carried. Citizens Investment Trust managed the issue, and ICRA Nepal graded it 3+.

The arithmetic is worth checking, because the numbers in this section are the article’s exhibit. 90,69,388 units times Rs 601 is Rs 5,450,702,188 — Rs 5.45 arba, as reported. The premium, Rs 501 a share times the same 90,69,388 units, is Rs 4,543,763,388 — Rs 4.54 arba, the reported rise in reserves. Paid-up capital: Rs 6.34 arba plus the face value of the new shares, Rs 0.9069 arba, is Rs 7.25 arba, as reported. And the demand: about Rs 48 arba applied for Rs 5.45 arba of shares is 8.8 times the issue — stated here as about nine times, which is arithmetic on the reported figures, labelled as such. None of these is a new number; each is the reported figure, recomputed and matched.

05. Par or premium

An FPO is not always priced at Rs 100, and it is not always priced above it. Unique Nepal Laghubitta issued its 3,79,425 units at exactly Rs 100 in Jestha 2080, collecting Rs 3.79 crore and lifting its paid-up capital from Rs 11.06 crore to Rs 14.85 crore. Nepal Bank priced its 2075 FPO at Rs 280 — 1,76,84,858 units, Ashad 15–19, 2075 — and NMB Bank at Rs 333 — 1,14,15,163 units, from Jestha 30, 2075. Nepal SBI Bank’s 2073 FPO, a small one of 67,767.87 units, was priced at Rs 971 and was oversubscribed more than 73 times. The chart below puts the six FPO prices in this article side by side; the face value is Rs 100 in every case, so the height of each bar above Rs 100 is the premium the market was asked to pay.

To charge a premium, a company must meet SEBON’s conditions — profit in two of the last five years and a net worth per share not below par value — and the FPO must be endorsed by its annual general meeting. A company that cannot meet those conditions, like Nepal Grameen Bikas Bank, issues at par: it had not been in operation for five years, so there was no premium to charge. The premium, in other words, is not a price the company invents; it is a price the regulator lets it charge, on the strength of earnings and net worth behind it.

And a premium price is no guarantee the issue fills. Nepal Bank’s Rs 280 FPO collected only Rs 2.61 arba of a Rs 4.95 arba issue, from 23,681 applicants, and the issue had to be extended by a month, to Shrawan 13, 2075, with underwriters taking up part of what the public left. NMB Bank’s Rs 333 FPO went the other way: it collected Rs 3.88 arba against a Rs 3.80 arba issue, from more than 70,650 applicants, oversubscribed by about 2.10%. Same year, same rulebook, two different outcomes — which is why the price is a question to investigate, not a signal to follow.

06. How to apply, and how allotment works

Applying is the same as for an IPO: through MeroShare, with the money blocked in your bank account until allotment. Under SEBON’s Securities Issue and Allotment Guidelines 2074, every applicant gets a minimum of 10 units first; then 10 more units go to those who applied for 20 or more; if 10 units each is not possible, allotment is by lottery. An individual issue can set its own minimum — Nepal Bank’s 2075 FPO set 50 units. Allotment must be completed within 30 days when applicants are below two lakh, 40 days for two to three lakh, and 50 days above three lakh, and the shares must list within two months of the issue closing. The allotment rule is the same one the IPO allotment article explains in depth; the FPO follows it because the Guidelines cover both.

What you pay to apply is the issue price times the units you ask for, blocked — not debited — until allotment. Once the shares list and you sell them, the ordinary trading charges apply: broker commission, the SEBON fee and the DP charge, covered in what a trade costs.

07. IPO, FPO, right share — the three-way split

Put the three side by side and the confusion dissolves. All three put your money into the company; that is what makes them primary-market issues rather than secondary-market trades. What differs is who issues, who can apply, what it usually costs, and why.

The three ways a company sells you new shares

Who issues itUnlisted companyListed companyListed company

The table’s last row is the one beginners most often miss. An IPO and an FPO allot by the same 10-unit rule, so a small applicant has the same footing in both; a right share allots in proportion to what you already hold, so a small holder gets a small entitlement. That is why the FPO feels like an IPO to apply for and like a right share only in the sense that the issuer is already listed — and why it is neither.

08. What to check before you apply

An FPO is not a bargain by definition and not a trap by definition. The price is the first question: a premium is only as good as the earnings and net worth behind it, which is why SEBON sets conditions for charging one. Compare the issue price with the share’s market price on NEPSE — NMB’s FPO was priced at Rs 333 while the share last traded around Rs 349 to Rs 367 in the reports of the time, which is part of why demand built slowly and only oversubscribed on the last day. A premium far above the market price asks you to pay more than the market charges for the same share the same week.

The reason is the second question: an FPO that lifts public holding to 30% is a compliance exercise, while one that funds a named project can be judged on that project — its size, its status and whether the money is enough to build it. And the third is the simplest: the money you pay goes to the company, so the question is always what the company will do with it. Read the offer letter before you apply; it states the units, the price, the dates, the issue manager and the use of the proceeds, and it is the document the regulator approved.

What to take from it

An FPO is a listed company selling new shares to the public. The money goes to the company, not to existing shareholders. The most common reason in Nepal is the 30% public-holding rule; the other is funding a named project. The price is Rs 100 of face value plus whatever premium the company is allowed to charge — Rs 100 at Unique Nepal Laghubitta, Rs 971 at Nepal SBI Bank — and a premium is earned by profit in two of the last five years and net worth per share at or above par. Allotment follows the 10-unit rule, the same as an IPO. And the record cuts both ways: NIBL’s FPO drew about nine times its size, NMB’s oversubscribed on the last day, and Nepal Bank’s had to be extended because the public left half of it unsold. The FPO is a mechanism, not a verdict — read the offer letter, compare the price with the market, and ask what the company will do with your money.

Frequently asked questions

Is an FPO the same as an IPO?

No. An IPO is a company’s first sale of shares to the public, before listing. An FPO is a further sale by a company that is already listed. You apply the same way, through MeroShare, and allotment follows the same 10-unit rule — but the issuer already trades on NEPSE, and the FPO price is usually set with that market price in mind.

Can promoters apply for an FPO?

An FPO is for the general public. In the Nepal Investment Bank FPO, SEBON barred promoter shareholders, board members and their immediate families from applying. That bar is recorded for the NIBL case; the general principle — the FPO is the public’s issue — is the point.

Why is an FPO sometimes priced above Rs 100?

The face value is Rs 100; anything above it is premium. A company may charge a premium only if it meets SEBON’s conditions, including profit in two of the last five years and net worth per share at or above par, and the FPO must be endorsed by its annual general meeting. Nepal Investment Bank charged Rs 601 — Rs 100 face plus Rs 501 premium — while Unique Nepal Laghubitta, which issued at par, charged Rs 100.

How many units do I get if the FPO is oversubscribed?

Under SEBON’s 2074 Guidelines, every applicant gets a minimum of 10 units first, then more in rounds of 10; if 10 units each is not possible, allotment is by lottery. In the Nepal SBI Bank FPO, which was oversubscribed more than 73 times, all allottees received 10 units through the lottery. An individual issue can set its own minimum — Nepal Bank’s 2075 FPO set 50 units.

Can I sell FPO shares immediately?

Once the shares are listed on NEPSE, public FPO shares trade freely. There is no three-year lock-in on the public quota — that lock-in applies to promoter and project-affected-local quotas in an IPO, not to public FPO shares. The shares must list within two months of the issue closing.

Does the FPO money go to the existing shareholders?

No. Like an IPO, the money goes to the company. Buying the same shares on NEPSE is the transaction where your money goes to the selling investor. That is the difference between the primary market, where companies raise capital, and the secondary market, where investors trade with each other.

What happens if an FPO is undersubscribed?

The issue can be extended, and the unsold part can be taken up by underwriters. Nepal Bank’s 2075 FPO collected only Rs 2.61 arba of a Rs 4.95 arba issue, from 23,681 applicants; it was extended by a month, to Shrawan 13, 2075, and underwriters took up part of what the public left. A premium price is no guarantee an issue fills.
