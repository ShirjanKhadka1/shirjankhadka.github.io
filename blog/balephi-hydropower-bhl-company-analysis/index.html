BHL at Rs 233.90: below its hydro peers on both lenses — but the profit behind the price is only one year old

Balephi Hydropower earns Rs 7.72 a share in its first profitable year, carries a book value still below face value, and trades at 30.30 times earnings against a 45.21 times peer median. This analysis separates the cheapness the market is offering from the single year of profit it rests on, and shows every calculation.

By Nepse Decode Research Desk · Kathmandu · November 4, 2026 · 30 min read
Facebook X LinkedIn WhatsApp Viber

Balephi Hydropower Limited (BHL) is the simplest kind of company to describe and one of the hardest to price honestly. It owns one thing: the 36 MW Upper Balephi ‘A’ hydroelectric project, a run-of-river plant in Jugal Rural Municipality, Sindhupalchok district, that has sold power to the national grid since late 2022. There is no second plant, no distribution business and no diversified portfolio to smooth a bad season. What the river gives, the company earns; what the dry months withhold, it does not. That single-asset reality is the whole story of this analysis, because the market is currently pricing the company as though its first profitable year were already a pattern — and the filings show it is, so far, exactly one year.

On 3 November 2026, the latest trading session before this article, BHL closed at Rs 233.90, up Rs 6.00 or 2.63% on the day, on volume of 453,436 shares and turnover of Rs 10.51 crore across 1,043 trades. The price sits 67% of the way up its 52-week range of Rs 172.10 to Rs 264.40 (computed), 11.54% below the 52-week high and 35.91% above the 52-week low (computed). Against the latest quarterly filing — the fourth quarter of fiscal 2082/83 — the company earned Rs 7.72 a share on a book value of Rs 81.22 a share. Those two per-share figures, divided into the close, give a price-to-earnings ratio of 30.30 times and a price-to-book ratio of 2.88 times (both computed). The question this article answers is not whether those ratios are high or low in isolation. It is whether they are high or low against genuinely comparable hydropower companies — and what breaks first if the single profitable year does not repeat.

02. Valuation: cheaper than its peers — on both lenses

Peer selection follows the standing valuation standard: genuinely comparable NEPSE companies only — same sector, recently traded, with a filed fourth-quarter fiscal 2082/83 result and positive earnings, so that every ratio in the table is defined. Six hydropower companies meet that test at the 3 November closes carried in NEPSE Trading’s hydropower quarterly table: Chilime, Himalaya Urja, Molung, Synergy Power, Singati and Panchakanya Mai. Loss-making hydropower companies are excluded, not because their prices do not matter, but because a negative earnings figure makes a price-to-earnings ratio meaningless — and including them would have flattered or distorted the median without adding information. Large diversified names and very small single-plant names outside this earnings-positive, recently filed set are likewise left out, with the reason stated here rather than buried. 03. Financials: one profitable year, built on a wet season

Nepali listed-company quarterly reports are cumulative, year to date — a point that changes how every number below must be read. The fourth-quarter figure is the full-year figure; the first-quarter figure is three months. The table therefore shows both: the cumulative profit as filed, and the standalone quarter computed by subtracting the previous cumulative figure. The standalone column is where the story lives. Fiscal 2082/83 opened strongly, with Rs 12.45 crore in the first quarter alone, added Rs 10.36 crore in the second, then nearly stopped: Rs 0.76 crore in the third quarter, the dry-season quarter, before recovering to Rs 4.66 crore in the fourth. A run-of-river plant with no storage cannot generate what the river does not carry, and the third quarter — Magh to Chaitra, the driest months — is when that constraint binds hardest. The full-year profit of Rs 28.23 crore is real, and it is also, in its composition, a wet-season profit with a dry-season hole in the middle.

Set that year against the two before it and the recovery sharpens — and so does the caution. At the third quarter of fiscal 2081/82, the last point of that year in the tearsheet series used here, the company carried a cumulative loss of Rs 32.67 crore, reserves of minus Rs 86.09 crore and a book value of Rs 76.45. The full year of fiscal 2080/81, on the pre-rights share base, was a loss of Rs 41.97 crore with a book value of Rs 53.20. The full-year figure for fiscal 2081/82 itself is not in the tearsheet series used here — a gap, stated rather than filled — but the direction across the three years is not in doubt: from deep losses, through a loss-making third quarter, to a profitable year. The book value tells the same story in slower motion. Reserves improved from minus Rs 86.09 crore to minus Rs 68.64 crore across fiscal 2082/83, and book value rose from Rs 78.02 to Rs 81.22, but the balance sheet still carries the accumulated losses of the construction and early-operation years. That is why book value sits below the Rs 100 face value even in a profitable year: one year of Rs 28.23 crore does not erase several years of losses, and any reader who treats Rs 81.22 as a floor rather than a still-recovering figure is misreading the balance sheet.

A second structural break runs through the whole series and must be stated before any multi-year comparison is made. Paid-up capital doubled during fiscal 2081/82, from Rs 1.83 arab in the first quarter to Rs 3.48 arab in the second and Rs 3.66 arab by the third (NEPSE Trading tearsheet), consistent with the 1:1 rights issue of that year. Per-share figures from before that expansion sit on roughly half as many shares as today’s figures, so earnings and book values across the break are not directly comparable without adjusting for the new share count — and this article does not present an adjusted multi-year per-share trend for that reason. All of the valuation work in section 02 uses only the post-rights, fourth-quarter fiscal 2082/83 per-share figures, on the current 36,559,400 shares. Revenue for fiscal 2082/83 is a further gap: the tearsheet extract used here carries profit, reserves and per-share figures but not the power-sales revenue line, so no revenue figure, margin or growth rate is stated. The profit is verified; the revenue behind it is not in the sources used, and it is not estimated here.

04. The project: one river, one contract, one set of risks

Upper Balephi ‘A’ is a 36 MW run-of-river project on the Balephi river in Jugal Rural Municipality, Sindhupalchok, developed under a build-own-operate-transfer model. Commercial operation began on 2079/08/06 in the Bikram Sambat calendar (November/December 2022), and the company signed its long-term power purchase agreement with the Nepal Electricity Authority on 15 December 2015, for contracted energy of 38.62 gigawatt-hours in the dry season and 174.21 gigawatt-hours in the wet season — 212.83 gigawatt-hours in total (computed sum). Power is evacuated over a 22-kilometre, 132-kilovolt line to the Authority’s substation at Lamosanghu. The project’s gross head is 208 metres with a design discharge of 20.80 cubic metres per second. Those are the fixed facts of the asset; everything else about the company is a consequence of them. A run-of-river plant has no reservoir to carry wet-season water into the dry season, so its quarterly earnings will always look like section 03: strong when the river runs, thin when it does not. The contracted energy split says the same thing in advance — 82% of contracted energy falls in the wet season (computed: 174.21 ÷ 212.83) — so a reader who annualises a wet-season quarter, or who treats a dry-season quarter as a collapse, is making the same error in opposite directions.

The project’s history also explains the balance sheet. The company was incorporated on 15 February 2012 as a private limited company and converted to a public limited company on 6 January 2017 to facilitate public participation (ShareSansar). The plant was still 95–98% complete at the end of November 2021, years behind its original schedule, and the capitalised cost reached Rs 8,640 million according to ICRA Nepal’s July 2023 rating note — against a revised budgeted cost of Rs 7,856 million cited at the locals’ IPO in early 2022. Cost overrun and delayed completion were the reasons ICRA Nepal gave in July 2023 when it downgraded the issuer rating to [ICRANP-IR] B and moved the company to its ‘issuer not cooperating’ category, citing a lack of adequate information on the project’s operational performance. That rating is three years old, and no current rating was located in the sources used — so it is reported here as history, not as a current assessment. But it explains the shape of the early losses: a project that cost more than budgeted, finished late, and then had to service its debt from a single river’s seasonal flow. The 2019 IPO grading had already flagged the underlying risk in plain terms — high hydrology risk, given the absence of a deemed-generation clause in the power purchase agreement — meaning that when the river runs low, the contract does not make the company whole (ICRA Nepal, 2019 IPO grading). That clause, or its absence, is still the single most important sentence in the company’s economics, and it has not changed.

05. The subsidiary question, kept in its place

Nepse Decode covered Balephi’s newest move on 4 October 2026, and this analysis does not re-argue it (Balephi is building an investment company with shareholder money. The notice does not say what it will buy). The facts, from the company’s own disclosure to NEPSE dated 2083/06/15, are these: BHL Investment Limited, a public company registered with the Office of the Company Registrar for investment objectives, carries authorised capital of Rs 50 crore and issued capital of Rs 2.5 crore; Balephi Hydropower committed Rs 2 crore as equity investment in it, and its board approved paying the first Rs 1 crore when BHL Investment called it on 2083/06/02. The remaining Rs 1 crore of the commitment sits unpaid, on a timeline the notice does not give, and the notice names no target sectors, expected returns, deployment timeline or co-investors for the rest of the issued capital. For this valuation, the subsidiary matters in proportion: Rs 2 crore is 0.55% of Balephi’s Rs 365.59 crore paid-up capital (computed), and Rs 1 crore paid is smaller still. The immediate financial exposure is limited, and no part of the peer medians or lenses in section 02 depends on it. What matters is directional, and it is the same question the October article asked: a listed hydro developer has formally entered the investment business, and the market will now watch what its new company does with the money. Until a further disclosure arrives, the subsidiary is a footnote to the valuation and a headline for governance — and this article keeps it in that order.

06. What to watch, and what this article does not claim

The honest summary of BHL is a tension, not a verdict. On one side: a profitable year, a book value recovering from deep negative reserves, a price below both peer lenses, and a one-year yield of +25.89% that says the market has already begun to notice. On the other: a single-asset, single-river company whose dry-season quarter earned Rs 0.76 crore, whose book value remains below face value, whose earnings history before this year is losses, and whose power purchase agreement contains no deemed-generation protection against a weak river year. The lenses say the market is paying less than peers for these earnings and this book. They do not say the earnings will repeat, and the earnings lens in particular should be discounted in the reader’s mind by exactly the fact that it annualises one year. If fiscal 2083/84 repeats a profit near Rs 28 crore, the earnings lens hardens into a pattern; if the dry season bites harder or the river disappoints, the book lens — Rs 245.77 against a price of Rs 233.90 — is the gap that survives contact with a bad year, because book value does not vanish in a single dry quarter the way a quarterly profit can.

Three things to watch, in order. First, the next quarterly filing: whether the first quarter of fiscal 2083/84 holds near the Rs 12.45 crore of a year earlier, or whether the wet-season base is slipping. Second, any disclosure from BHL Investment — the call of the remaining Rs 1 crore, or any named deployment — because the subsidiary is where governance, not hydrology, will next move the story. Third, the promoter–public shareholding split, which neither ShareHub nor NEPSE Trading states reliably for this company (both show promoter holding at zero, which is not credible for a company with this history, and Nepse Decode’s own stock page withholds the split as unverified): until a verified split is published, any claim about who owns BHL is a guess, and this article makes none. The audited full-year result for fiscal 2082/83, the power-sales revenue line, a current credit rating and the fiscal 2081/82 full-year profit are likewise gaps, listed here so that a reader can see exactly where verification stopped. Nothing in this article is a recommendation to buy, sell or hold any security; it is research and education, built so that every reader can recompute every ratio from the figures printed beside it.

What did BHL earn, and why is one year not a pattern?

How do the peer medians work, and who was left out?

Why is book value below the Rs 100 face value in a profitable year?

Because accumulated losses still outweigh the profitable year. Reserves and surplus stand at minus Rs 68.64 crore against Rs 365.59 crore of paid-up capital, giving net worth of Rs 296.95 crore (computed) and book value of Rs 81.22 a share. Reserves improved from minus Rs 86.09 crore at the third quarter of fiscal 2081/82, but one year of Rs 28.23 crore profit does not erase several years of losses. Book value recovering is not the same as book value recovered.

Why was the dry-season quarter so weak?

Because Upper Balephi ‘A’ is a run-of-river plant with no storage: it generates what the river carries. Standalone quarterly profit (computed from cumulative filings) was Rs 12.45 crore in the first quarter of fiscal 2082/83, Rs 10.36 crore in the second, just Rs 0.76 crore in the dry-season third quarter, and Rs 4.66 crore in the fourth. The contracted energy split points the same way — 82% of contracted energy falls in the wet season (computed). The power purchase agreement has no deemed-generation clause, so a low river is not compensated under the contract, as flagged in the 2019 IPO grading.

Does BHL pay a dividend?

No dividend is shown for the most recent fiscal year on the tearsheet used here, and no dividend proposal for fiscal 2082/83 was located in the sources checked at publish time. That is a gap in the record, not a claim that the company will never pay one: a company still carrying minus Rs 68.64 crore of reserves has a balance-sheet reason to retain profit, but any future proposal would need its own board disclosure, which this article does not anticipate.
