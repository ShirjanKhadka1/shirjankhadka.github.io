The index rose and most shares fell — how to read market breadth on NEPSE

On Wednesday, November 4, the NEPSE index closed up 0.05% while 171 shares fell and only 98 rose. Nothing was wrong with the numbers. The index and the market were measuring different things — and breadth, the daily count of shares that rose, stayed flat and fell, is the number that tells you which one your portfolio actually lived through.

On Wednesday, November 4, 2026, the NEPSE index closed up 0.05% at 2,665.53. A green day, by the only number most investors ever check. But only 98 shares rose that day while 171 fell and 9 stayed flat. If you held an average share, your day was red while the headline was green. Nothing was wrong with the numbers — the index and the market were simply measuring different things, and most investors only ever look at one of them. The other number is called breadth: the daily count of listed shares that advanced, stayed unchanged and declined. The index is one number; breadth is the vote behind it.

This article is a reading lesson, not a forecast. It uses one week — November 3 to 6, 2026, the last trading week before the Tihar break — because that week contains all four kinds of day: a narrow win, a split day, a broad fall and a broad rise. Every figure comes from NEPSE’s own official close data, as published in the verified daily market summaries for those four sessions. The market is closed for Tihar until Friday, November 13, so there is no fresh session to chase; the week behind us is the cleanest classroom the market has offered in a while.

01. The confusion: a green index on a red day

Start with the day that breaks the habit. Wednesday, November 4: the index rose 1.40 points to 2,665.53, a gain of 0.05% as NEPSE displays it. The day’s top gainer, NRIC, rose 15.00% to Rs 921.70. Anyone who checked only the index went home thinking the market had a good day. But the count underneath said the opposite: 98 shares advanced, 9 were unchanged, 171 declined. Of the 278 shares counted that day, 171 — 61.5% — fell. The most common experience of the day was a loss, on a day the headline called a gain.

This is not a data error, and it is not rare. It is the normal consequence of how the two numbers are built, and once you see the mechanism you will never again read the index as a summary of how most shares did. The rest of this article is that mechanism, worked through on four real days.

02. What breadth is

Every trading day, NEPSE counts how many listed shares closed higher than the day before, how many closed flat, and how many closed lower. That three-way count — advanced, unchanged, declined — is called breadth. It is published with each session’s market summary on nepalstock.com.np, and Nepse Decode’s daily summary carries the same count.

The index is one number; breadth is the vote behind it. It answers the question the index never asks: did the whole market move, or just a few heavy names? A day on which 158 shares rise and 107 fall is a different market from a day on which 98 rise and 171 fall, even if the index prints the same small gain on both. Breadth is the difference between those two days, written down.

Two things breadth is not. It is not a measure of money: it counts shares, not rupees, so a day of heavy turnover in a few large names can look narrow even while a lot of money moved. And it is not a prediction: a broad day tells you what today was like, not what tomorrow will be. Both limits matter, and the article keeps them in view.

03. Why the index can disagree with the market

The NEPSE index is capitalisation-weighted — value-weighted, in the exchange’s own description: the index equals total market capitalisation divided by base market capitalisation, multiplied by 100, with the base set at 100 in January 1994. A company’s size decides how much it moves the index. A few large companies rising can lift the whole index even while most shares fall, because small companies barely move it however many of them fall. One giant rising can outvote a hundred small falls.

That is not a flaw in the index — it is what the index is designed to do. The index measures where the market’s value went. Breadth measures how many of its companies went with it. The mistake is reading the first number as if it were the second. On Wednesday, November 4, the value went up and the companies mostly went down, and both numbers were right.

The same logic runs in the other direction. A day on which the index falls a little while breadth collapses — Thursday, November 5, below — is a worse day than the index alone suggests. And a day on which the index rises a little while breadth surges — Friday, November 6 — is a better day than the index alone suggests. The index gives you the direction and the size; breadth gives you the width.

05. The Monday footnote: why November 2 sits outside the chart

Monday, November 2 is deliberately left out of the charts above. Its breadth — 45 advanced, 12 unchanged, 286 declined — was counted across all 343 scrips from NEPSE’s own dated Today’s Price table for 11/02/2026, while Tuesday to Friday use the index-mapped count of about 279 scrips. Different definitions cannot be compared in one chart, so Monday sits outside it — as the week’s heaviest down day by count, stated separately.

Stated separately, it is the starkest day of the week: 286 of 343 scrips fell, 83.4% of everything listed. The index fell 32.65 points, −1.21% to 2,657.50. On Monday the two numbers agreed completely — the value fell and the companies fell with it — and the agreement is the point. A broad fall and a narrow fall can print similar index moves; only the count tells you which one you lived through.

06. The three-step check

Reading breadth takes ten seconds once the habit is set. Three steps, in order, using the same week as the worked example.

1. Index direction. Up or down — and by how much? Wednesday: up 1.40 points, +0.05%. Thursday: down 12.95 points, −0.48%. Friday: up 2.64 points, +0.09%. This is the number you already check; keep checking it.

2. Breadth direction. Did most shares follow, or go the other way? Wednesday: 98 rose, 171 fell — most went the other way. Thursday: 56 rose, 211 fell — most followed, down. Friday: 158 rose, 107 fell — most followed, up. Tuesday: 143 rose, 122 fell — most followed, but only just.

3. Agree or disagree. If they agree, the move is broad. If they disagree, a few heavyweights did the lifting — and your portfolio may have had a very different day from the headline. Tuesday agreed, narrowly. Wednesday disagreed: the index rose while the market fell. Thursday agreed, downwards. Friday agreed, upwards, and more broadly than Tuesday despite the smaller point gain.

Run the check on the day that matters to you, not on the week in this article. The market reopens on Friday, November 13 after the Tihar break; the first session back is the first chance to try it live. The count is published with every session’s close, so the check costs nothing beyond the habit.

07. The one habit that makes it work

Check breadth first, then decide what the day meant. The index tells you where the market’s value went. Breadth tells you how many of its companies went with it. Neither number alone is the whole story — and on a day like Wednesday, November 4, the difference between them was the whole story.

The habit also protects you from the two common misreadings. The first is treating a green index as a green day for your holdings: on Wednesday, most holders lost money on a day the index gained. The second is treating a small index move as a quiet day: Thursday’s −0.48% looks modest until you see that 211 shares fell, and Friday’s +0.09% looks trivial until you see that 158 rose. The index measures the move; breadth measures the participation. A move with participation behind it and a move without it are different markets, and only the count tells you which one you are in.

The same three-way count works on any list of shares, not just the whole market. Count your own holdings the same way at the close — how many rose, how many were flat, how many fell — and compare your count with the market’s. On Wednesday, a portfolio that fell was not underperforming a rising market; it was the market. That is the sentence breadth exists to let you say, and the reason it is worth the ten seconds.

What to take from it

The NEPSE index and market breadth answer different questions, and the week of November 3–6 asked both of them. The index said the week ended almost flat, down 2.28 points. Breadth said the week was a narrow win, a split day, a broad fall and a broad rise — and that the one day the index rose while most shares fell was Wednesday, when 171 shares fell and only 98 rose. Neither number is wrong; each is incomplete without the other. Read them together, in that order — index first for direction, breadth second for width — and a green headline will never again pass for a green day without the count to back it.

Frequently asked questions

What is market breadth?

The daily count of listed shares that advanced, stayed unchanged, and declined. On NEPSE it is published with every session’s close: for example, on Friday, November 6, 2026, 158 shares advanced, 14 were unchanged and 107 declined. Breadth is the vote behind the index — it says how many companies moved with the market, while the index says where the market’s value went.

Can the NEPSE index rise while most shares fall?

Yes. The index is capitalisation-weighted, so large companies move it far more than small ones. On Wednesday, November 4, 2026 the index rose 0.05% to 2,665.53 while 171 shares fell and only 98 rose — 61.5% of the counted shares fell on a day the headline called green.

Where do I find breadth?

NEPSE publishes the advancers / unchanged / decliners count with each session’s market summary on nepalstock.com.np, and Nepse Decode’s daily market summary carries the same count. Check it at the close, alongside the index — the two numbers together are the day’s verdict.

Does breadth predict where the market goes next?

No. Breadth describes what happened today; it is a reading tool, not a forecast. A broad rise does not promise another one tomorrow, and a narrow rise does not promise a fall. This article makes no prediction about any future session, including the first session after the Tihar break on Friday, November 13.

Why is Monday, November 2 not in the week’s chart?

Its breadth was counted across all 343 scrips, while Tuesday to Friday use the index-mapped count of about 279. Different definitions cannot be compared in one chart, so Monday is stated separately: 45 advanced, 12 unchanged, 286 declined — 83.4% of listed scrips fell — with the index down 32.65 points to 2,657.50.

What does “unchanged” mean?

A share that closed at exactly the same price as the previous session. In the week of November 3–6, 2026, between 9 and 14 shares a day were unchanged. They are carried in the table and counted in the percentages, but not drawn as bars — a bar of 9 next to a bar of 211 would say nothing the table does not say better.

Can I use breadth on my own portfolio?

Yes, and it is the most useful version of the habit. Count your own holdings the same way at the close — how many rose, how many were flat, how many fell — and compare your count with the market’s. On Wednesday, November 4, a portfolio that fell was not underperforming a rising market; it was the market. Breadth works on any list of shares; the market-wide count is simply the biggest list.
