GBIME at Rs 266: Nepal’s biggest bank, priced below both lenses — on the quarter’s earnings alone

Global IME earned Rs 2.31 billion in the first quarter of FY2083/84 — the second-largest profit of any commercial bank — and its shares closed at Rs 266 on November 6. On that quarter’s annualised earnings and book value, the price sits below what its peers’ multiples would pay. On the audited full year it does not, and the whole of the quarter’s growth came from provisions falling. The gap between the two readings is the story of this article.

By Nepse Decode Research Desk · Kathmandu · November 11, 2026 · 35 min read
Facebook X LinkedIn WhatsApp Viber

Global IME Bank is the biggest bank in Nepal by balance sheet and the second-biggest on NEPSE by market value, and it is also, on the latest quarterly figures, the cheapest of the large commercial banks. At the November 6 close of Rs 266, the market is paying 11.39 times the bank’s annualised first-quarter earnings and 1.51 times its book value. Its six closest listed peers, on the same quarterly basis, trade at a median of 15.12 times earnings and 2.26 times book. That gap is the whole story of this article: a discount of about 25% on earnings and about 33% on book, for a bank whose latest quarter was the second-strongest in the sector — and whose profit jump was driven entirely by provisions falling, not by lending growing. Nothing here is a recommendation to buy or sell. It is a measurement, with the workings shown.

The discount needs its qualifier up front, because it does not survive every reading of the numbers. On the audited full year that ended in mid-July 2026, Global IME earned Rs 6.04 billion, and the earnings per share that implies — Rs 15.85 on the pre-bonus share count, computed — puts the same Rs 266 price at 16.78 times earnings, above the peer median rather than below it. The market, in other words, is pricing the run-rate of the quarter that ended in mid-October, not the audited year behind it. Whether that quarter’s run-rate can repeat is the question the rest of this article works through.

Signal summary

  • The price: Rs 266 at the November 6 close (+0.30% on the day), 84% of the way up its 52-week range of Rs 218.90–275. NEPSE itself ended the week almost flat at 2,655.22.
  • The quarter: first-quarter FY2083/84 net profit of Rs 2.31 billion, up 24.49% year on year — the second-largest of any commercial bank, 12.1% of the sector’s Rs 19.14 billion. Annualised earnings per share Rs 23.35; book value per share Rs 175.77.
  • The catch: impairment charges fell 51.7% (Rs 1,011.0 million to Rs 488.3 million) — a saving of about Rs 523 million, larger than the entire Rs 455 million increase in net profit. Net interest income grew only 1.46%. The profit jump is provisions, and then some.
  • The valuation: the earnings lens is Rs 353.05 (peer median P/E 15.12×) and the book lens is Rs 397.24 (peer median P/B 2.26×). The Rs 266 price sits 24.7% below the first and 33.0% below the second — on the quarter’s figures. On the audited full-year earnings it would be 16.78×, above the median.
  • The dividend: 10% for FY2082/83 (4% bonus shares plus 6% cash), approved at the 20th AGM on October 2. At Rs 266 that is a 3.76% yield, 2.26% of it in cash — and the books closed on September 21, so a buyer today does not receive it.

02. Valuation view: two lenses, both above the price

The valuation follows the same method as the earlier company analyses in this series: take six genuinely comparable listed commercial banks, all recently traded, re-run their multiples at the latest verified closes, and publish the full table so every median recomputes. The peers are Nabil, Everest, NMB, Sanima, Kumari and Standard Chartered — the sector’s other large, profitable, normally-traded banks, and the same six used in the Nabil analysis of November 8, at the same November 6 closes: no trading has taken place since, the market being closed November 9–12 for Tihar, so the closes are still the latest verified ones.

Two exclusions are stated, not hidden: Rastriya Banijya Bank is unlisted, so it has no market price; NIC Asia is excluded as a distressed-earnings outlier (its annualised first-quarter EPS is Rs 3.42 and its full-year EPS Rs 1.22 — see the NIC Asia analysis of November 2), and the same reasoning excludes Agricultural Development Bank (negative EPS) and Citizens Bank (Rs 2.68). 03. The quarter: Rs 2.31 billion, led by a collapse in impairment

Global IME’s first-quarter statement for FY2083/84, published on November 1–2, is the freshest verified picture of the bank, and it is a strong one on the surface. Net profit was Rs 2.31 billion, up 24.49% from Rs 1.86 billion in the same quarter last year — the second-largest quarterly profit of any of Nepal’s 20 commercial banks, behind Nabil’s Rs 2.55 billion, and 12.1% of the sector’s combined Rs 19.14 billion. The sector as a whole grew 45.59% in the quarter, so GBIME grew at about half the industry’s pace; what sets it apart is the scale, not the direction.

The composition of that growth is where the judgement lies. Net interest income rose to Rs 4.19 billion from Rs 4.13 billion, up just 1.46% — barely any growth in the bank’s core recurring income, and far behind Nabil’s 21.3% in the same quarter. Annualised, it runs at about Rs 16.8 billion against Rs 16.20 billion for all of last year: the lending engine is idling. Net fee and commission income rose 34.4% to Rs 1.22 billion, and total operating income rose 8.9% to Rs 6.01 billion. But the largest single contributor to the profit jump was not income at all: impairment charges fell 51.7%, from Rs 1,011.0 million to Rs 488.3 million, a saving of about Rs 523 million — larger than the entire Rs 455 million increase in net profit. Without that saving, profit would have fallen. Operating profit rose 35.9% to Rs 3.22 billion, and profit before tax matched it at Rs 3.22 billion, up from Rs 2.49 billion. A quarter built on lower provisions is a genuine improvement in the cost of past lending, but it is not the same as a quarter built on more lending: provisions can only fall so far, and next quarter they have almost nothing left to fall from.

The balance sheet tells the same two-sided story. Deposits reached Rs 690.00 billion, up 3.11% on the same quarter last year, and total assets Rs 835.36 billion, up 1.8% on the year-end. Loans and advances stood at Rs 471.96 billion, up 7.49% on the year-ago quarter — faster than deposits in the quarter, a reversal of last year’s pattern, when deposits grew 21.5% and loans just 7.1%. But the reversal starts from a low base: the credit-to-deposit ratio of 70.11% still leaves the bank with far more deposits than it is lending out, and the gap is why net interest income barely moved despite the bigger balance sheet. Asset quality did improve on the measure that matters most: the non-performing loan ratio fell to 4.79% from 4.98% a year earlier, against a sector average of 5.64% — mid-pack for a large bank, well behind Everest’s 0.50% and Standard Chartered’s 1.83%, but far from the distressed end where NIC Asia sits at 9.81% and Prabhu Bank at 14.80%. The cost of funds fell to 3.26% from 3.92%, and the base rate to 4.61% from 5.40% — cheaper funding is the quiet tailwind behind the whole sector’s recovery, and it has further to run here than at banks whose funding is already cheaper. Distributable profit stood at Rs 2.07 billion, down from Rs 4.85 billion a year earlier — the fall is the 8% cash dividend for FY2081/82, paid out of retained earnings during the year — while the sector as a whole still carries a negative cumulative distributable profit of Rs 9.07 billion.

One ratio in the quarter deserves a warning of its own. The capital adequacy ratio fell to 11.32% from 12.98% a year earlier, against Nepal Rastra Bank’s minimum of 11% — a buffer of just 32 basis points. That is one of the tightest capital positions in the sector — only NIC Asia’s 11.10% at the last year-end was lower — and it is the binding constraint on the bank: a bank that close to its regulatory floor cannot grow its loan book aggressively, cannot absorb a bad quarter of provisions, and cannot pay a large dividend without first rebuilding capital. The 4% bonus shares just issued did not help: a bonus issue moves reserves into paid-up capital without adding a rupee of total capital, so the share count rose while the capital base stayed put — which is also why book value per share fell 3.76% to Rs 175.77 even as total equity grew. The next section shows this is not a new problem.

04. The full year: Rs 6.23 billion unaudited, Rs 6.04 billion audited — and scale that outran earnings

The full year behind the quarter is FY2082/83, reported unaudited in August 2026 and audited at the 20th annual general meeting on October 2. On the unaudited print, net profit was Rs 6.23 billion, up 22.7% from Rs 5.08 billion, on operating profit of Rs 8.68 billion (up 20.0%) and net interest income of Rs 16.20 billion — which actually fell 2.9% from Rs 16.68 billion the year before. The same impairment story runs through the full year: charges fell 19.9% to Rs 4.79 billion from Rs 5.98 billion, while fee and commission income rose 13.0% to Rs 3.64 billion and personnel expenses rose with the branch network. The audited figures presented at the AGM put net profit lower, at Rs 6.04 billion (up 15.90%), and operating profit higher, at Rs 9.60 billion: the profit figure is about 3% below the unaudited print, and the two versions do not reconcile cleanly. This article uses the unaudited print for the year’s detail and the audited figure as the headline profit, and says which is which wherever it matters.

The five-year pattern is the article’s second big finding: total assets grew 2.4 times, from Rs 345 billion to Rs 821 billion, while earnings per share fell from Rs 19.25 to Rs 16.34 and reported return on equity fell from 13.4% to 9.1%. Scale increased; per-share earning power did not keep pace. Two forces explain it. The first is the denominator: the mergers that built the balance sheet also built the share count, and every new share dilutes the profit that belongs to the old ones. The second is the margin: net interest income has fallen for three straight years, from Rs 17.11 billion in FY2079/80 to Rs 16.20 billion, even as deposits grew by more than half — the bank is taking in far more money than it can lend at a margin, and the surplus sits in cash and investment securities, which were Rs 290.1 billion at the year-end, 35.3% of total assets. The FY2082/83 profit recovery did not come from lending either: it came from a lower impairment charge, higher fee income, and a larger deferred-tax credit. Pre-provision operating profit rose only about 2% year on year.

One discrepancy in the five-year record is stated here rather than resolved by picking the friendlier number. The unaudited fourth-quarter release for FY2081/82, reported in August 2025, put that year’s net profit at Rs 6.20 billion, with EPS of Rs 16.28. The audited figure that followed was materially lower: the annual report’s Rs 5.08 billion, and the 20th AGM’s claim of 15.90% growth on the audited Rs 6.04 billion implies about Rs 5.21 billion for FY2081/82. The two audited readings agree with each other within 2.5%; the unaudited print sits about a fifth above them. This article uses the audited figure and does not use the Rs 6.20 billion print anywhere — but the pattern is worth naming, because the same gap, smaller, recurs this year: the audited Rs 6.04 billion against the unaudited Rs 6.23 billion. A bank whose audited profits run below its unaudited prints two years running is a bank whose quarterly figures should be read with the audit in mind, and whose Q1 annualised EPS of Rs 23.35 — the figure both lenses are built on — is a first draft, not a final number.

The credit-quality picture underneath the profit is genuinely better than the headline NPL ratio suggests. Gross NPL ended the year at 4.96%, up slightly from 4.86%, and has risen every year since FY2078/79 — but net NPL, gross bad loans less provisions, fell from 0.95% to 0.59% across the four quarters of FY2082/83, and loan-loss provision coverage rose to 120.47%, meaning reserves now exceed the gross bad-loan balance by roughly a fifth. The improvement is in reserve coverage, not in the headline ratio declining. The capital position tightened through the year: the capital adequacy ratio ended at 12.10%, with a Tier 1 ratio of 10.04%, against an NRB minimum of 11% — a buffer of about 110 basis points, the narrowest in the five-year record, and the first-quarter figure of 11.32% has narrowed it further still.

05. Dividends: 10%, and what it yields at Rs 266

Global IME has paid a dividend in fifteen fiscal years on record, averaging 15.27% a year, and the latest declaration continues that record. For FY2082/83 the board proposed, and the 20th AGM approved on October 2, 2026, a total dividend of 10% — 4% in bonus shares and 6% in cash — announced on September 2, 2026 with book closure on September 21. The previous year’s dividend was 8% in cash, approved at the 19th AGM in November 2025; in FY2080/81 the bank paid 5.50% in bonus shares, and in FY2079/80 it paid 9% (1% bonus plus 8% cash).

At the Rs 266 close, the 10% dividend is Rs 10.00 per share on the Rs 100 face value, a yield of 3.76%; the cash leg alone is Rs 6.00 per share, a yield of 2.26%. The 17.75% dividend capacity the bank reported for the quarter is a statement about the first three months, not a promise about the year.

06. The company: built by twenty-one mergers

Global IME Bank Limited began as Global Bank Limited, established in 2007, and became Global IME Bank in 2012 when it merged with IME Financial Institution and Lord Buddha Finance. It has traded on NEPSE since July 26, 2017. What followed is the most aggressive merger programme in Nepali banking: twenty-one banking and financial institutions absorbed over roughly a decade — five commercial banks, ten development banks and six finance companies — the largest steps being Janata Bank Nepal in December 2019, which roughly doubled deposits, and Bank of Kathmandu in January 2023, which added about Rs 166 billion of assets and Rs 149 billion of deposits in a single year. Its head office is in Kamaladi, Kathmandu, and it now operates 341 branches, 366 ATMs, 147 branchless banking centres and 69 extension and revenue collection counters, with three foreign representative offices — the first private-sector commercial bank to reach all 77 districts of the country. It is the largest commercial bank in Nepal by total assets (Rs 820.77 billion) and by deposits (Rs 669.17 billion), and the second-largest by market capitalisation (Rs 105.44 billion, behind Nabil’s Rs 148.54 billion). Its promoter–public split is 51% promoter and 49% public, with 19.42 crore shares in public hands — a free float of Rs 51.67 billion, the second-largest in the sector. The bank has also begun construction of a new corporate building in Uttar Dhoka, Kathmandu, with work advanced in early November 2026.

The group around the bank is part of the story. Its subsidiaries are Global IME Capital (merchant banking), Global IME Laghubitta (microfinance, in which the bank holds 70% of the promoter shares) and Global IME Securities (stock brokerage, NEPSE broker number 89), with associates including Mero Microfinance, First Microfinance Development Bank, RSDC Laghubitta and Chhimek Laghubitta. The chairman is Chandra Prasad Dhakal, representing IME Investment, and the chief executive is Surendra Raj Regmi, in the role since Bhadra 2081 (August 2024). The bank has been named Best Bank Nepal in 2024, 2025 and 2026, and holds Euromoney Awards for Excellence from 2022, 2024 and 2025. None of that changes the arithmetic in section 02; it explains why the market might one day close the discount, and why it has not yet.

07. The price path: 84% up a narrow 52-week range

GBIME’s 52-week range is narrow for a bank of its size: Rs 218.90 to Rs 275, a spread of Rs 56.10, or about 26% from low to high. The November 6 close of Rs 266 sits 84% of the way up that range — closer to the top than the bottom, and nearer the high than any of the discount in section 02 might suggest. The all-time high, Rs 765 in July 2014, belongs to a different era of the market and a much smaller share count, and it is not a meaningful reference for today’s price; the adjusted all-time high of Rs 415.75 is the comparable figure, and the current price is about two-thirds of it.

08. Risks and what to watch

  • Provisions cannot fall twice. The first-quarter profit jump rested on impairment charges falling from Rs 1,011.0 million to Rs 488.3 million — and the saving exceeded the entire profit increase. If the second quarter’s charges return to even half their year-ago level, the annualised EPS of Rs 23.35 — the figure both lenses are built on — comes under pressure, and with it the earnings lens itself.
  • Net interest income is barely growing. It rose 1.46% in the quarter and has fallen for three straight full years, even as deposits grew by more than half. A bank that cannot turn deposits into loans at a margin is not growing the base that produces next year’s interest income; the cost of funds at 3.26% has limited room to fall further, and a third of the balance sheet already sits in cash and securities.
  • Capital is the binding constraint. The capital adequacy ratio of 11.32% leaves 32 basis points over the 11% minimum, the second-tightest in the sector. Until the bank rebuilds capital — through retained profit, a debenture, or a fresh issue — loan growth, dividend capacity and the ability to absorb a bad quarter are all capped.
  • The audit keeps cutting the profit. The audited FY2081/82 profit was about a fifth below the unaudited print, and the audited FY2082/83 profit about 3% below it. The Q1 figures in this article are unaudited first drafts; the audit has the last word, and it has not been kind to this bank’s prints.
  • Asset quality is mid-pack, not best-in-class. An NPL ratio of 4.79% is better than the sector’s 5.64% but far above Everest’s 0.50% and Standard Chartered’s 1.83%, and it has risen every year since FY2078/79. Any deterioration would hit provisions first, which is exactly the line that delivered this quarter’s growth.
  • Watch the second quarter. The next verified data point is the Q2 FY2083/84 statement, due in late January 2027: whether impairment stays low, whether the loan book keeps growing faster than deposits, whether net interest income starts moving again, and whether the capital adequacy ratio stops falling from 11.32%. Also watch the listing of the 4% bonus shares, which has already lifted the share count to 39.64 crore and moved the per-share figures with it.

What to take from it

Global IME Bank at Rs 266 is the clearest example in this series of a big bank at a low price. It is the largest bank in Nepal by balance sheet, it earned the second-largest profit in the sector last quarter, it pays a dividend every year, and its bad loans are falling. The market knows all of that, and has charged less for it than for any of its large peers: 11.39 times annualised first-quarter earnings and 1.51 times book, against peer medians of 15.12 and 2.26. The lenses say Rs 353.05 on earnings and Rs 397.24 on book — computed comparisons, not values — and the price is below both. But the discount is a statement about one quarter, and that quarter’s profit was earned in large part by provisions falling, and provisions cannot fall twice. On the audited full year, the same price is 16.78 times earnings — above the median, not below it — and the capital ratio of 11.32% leaves almost no room for the bank to grow its way out of the gap. If the second quarter shows the loan book growing, net interest income moving again and impairment staying low, the discount has a foundation. If it shows the reverse, the lenses will have been the expensive read. Nothing in this article is a recommendation to buy or sell any share.

Frequently asked questions

What does Global IME Bank do, and how big is it?

Global IME Bank is a Kathmandu-based ‘A’ class commercial bank, established in 2007 as Global Bank and renamed in 2012 after merging with IME Financial Institution and Lord Buddha Finance. It is the largest commercial bank in Nepal by total assets (Rs 820.77 billion) and by deposits (Rs 669.17 billion), with 341 branches, 366 ATMs and a presence in all 77 districts. Its head office is in Kamaladi, Kathmandu.

Why is the price below both lenses if the bank is doing so well?

Because the market is charging less for the bank’s earnings and book than for its peers. At Rs 266 the bank trades at 11.39 times annualised first-quarter earnings and 1.51 times book, against peer medians of 15.12 and 2.26. The lenses — Rs 353.05 on earnings and Rs 397.24 on book — are what the price would be if GBIME traded at the peer medians. A discount is not an error; it is the market’s judgement that GBIME’s earnings deserve a lower multiple — and the article’s question is whether the quarter behind that judgement can repeat.

Is the 24% profit jump sustainable?

Only partly. Fee income grew 34.4% and loans grew 7.49%, which is durable if lending and margins hold. But the whole of the profit increase — and more — came from impairment charges falling 51.7%, from Rs 1,011.0 million to Rs 488.3 million, while net interest income grew just 1.46%. Charges that low have almost nothing left to fall from. The second quarter will show whether the growth has a second engine.

What dividend would I get if I buy at Rs 266?

Not the 10% just declared: the books closed on September 21, 2026, so that dividend — Rs 10.00 per share, a 3.76% yield at Rs 266, with Rs 6.00 of it in cash — goes to whoever held the shares then. A buyer today is paying for the next dividend, which will be judged against the distributable profit of Rs 2.07 billion the bank carried into the second quarter, about Rs 5.22 per share.

Why does the article use Rs 266 when the site’s own stock page shows Rs 266.20?

Two independent vendors, ShareHub and Merolagani, agree on a close of Rs 266.00 with a last trade at 14:59:57 on November 6, and on the session volume of 210,512 shares. Nepse Decode’s own stock page shows Rs 266.20 with 205,047 shares. The article uses Rs 266.00 because two sources agree on it, and records the site’s figure in the method section rather than hiding it.

How does GBIME compare with Nabil, the other bank analysed recently?

They are mirror images at the moment. Nabil’s annualised EPS is Rs 33.89, its P/E 16.20×, its NPL 4.06% and its distributable profit Rs 2.26 billion, and its price sits above both lenses. GBIME’s annualised EPS is Rs 23.35, its P/E 11.39×, its NPL 4.79% and its distributable profit Rs 2.07 billion, and its price sits below both lenses. Nabil earns more per share and the market charges more for it; GBIME earns less and the market charges less. The Nabil analysis (November 8, 2026) is the other half of the comparison.

What should I watch next?

The second-quarter FY2083/84 statement, due in late January 2027: whether impairment charges stay low, whether the loan book keeps growing faster than deposits, whether net interest income starts moving again, and whether the capital adequacy ratio stops falling from 11.32%.
