01. The headline number is a summary, not the story

Every trading day ends with one number on the news: the NEPSE Index. On Tuesday it was 2,636.10, down 0.48% from Friday’s close.

One number is useful for the headline. It is useless for the question every investor actually asks: did my part of the market fall, or did something else drag the average down? The NEPSE Index averages the whole market. When it falls 0.48%, some sectors fell much further and some barely moved. You cannot tell which from the headline alone.

That is what the sector indices are for. They split the market into thirteen corners and report each corner’s move separately, so a fall that looks uniform in the headline turns out to be uneven underneath — and a sector that held up, or rose, stops being invisible.

02. What a sector index is

NEPSE groups every listed company into a sector — Banking, Hydropower, Life Insurance, Hotels And Tourism, and nine more — and publishes one index per sector. Each sub-index tracks the stocks mapped under that sector: NEPSE’s Datewise Indices page carries the note that turnover values, volumes and transactions “are based on the stocks mapped under the selected index” — the mapping of stocks under each index is NEPSE’s own wording.

So a sector index is, in plain language, the average price movement of one corner of the market. There are 13 of them, and they move independently: on a day the whole market falls, a single sector can still rise — as Mutual Fund did on Tuesday.

One honest gap: NEPSE publishes the sub-indices and their values, but no page on the official site defines them or explains the method. The definition in this article is a plain-language restatement of what an index is, not a quote from NEPSE. Nothing in this article claims a base date or a base value for any sub-index; neither was verified.

04. How to read a sector index (and how not to)

Compare percentages, never levels. Life Insurance sits at 11,718.08 and Mutual Fund at 19.94. That does not mean life insurers are “bigger” — the levels sit on different scales. Only the percentage move compares across sectors.

A point fall is not a percentage fall. Life Insurance’s −99.47 points looks dramatic next to Trading’s −0.93, but in percentage terms Life Insurance fell 0.84% and Trading 0.02%. Points measure the index’s own scale; percentages measure the move. Life Insurance lost 107 times as many points as Trading, but only 42 times as much in percentage terms — the point gap is mostly the level gap, not the move gap.

One session is not a trend. Tuesday says Hotels had the worst day of the thirteen. It says nothing about where Hotels goes next. A sector index tells you where a move happened, not where it is heading. The very next session proved the point: on Wednesday 18 November the market fell again, 0.41% to 2,625.05, but the sector story flipped — Trading, the flattest sector on Tuesday at −0.02%, fell the furthest at −2.49%, while Banking and Mutual Fund rose.

05. What to take from it

The next time the headline says the market fell, check the sector table before judging your own shares. If your sector fell 0.11% on a day the market fell 0.48%, your corner of the market held up better than the average — and if it fell 1.21%, it drove the average down. The NEPSE Index tells you the market moved. The thirteen sub-indices tell you where.

The habit is cheap and the mistake it prevents is common: reading the headline number as if every share in it moved the same way. On Tuesday, no two sectors moved the same way — not even the two tied at −0.84%, which arrived there from very different levels. The table is on NEPSE’s own homepage, in the Sub Indices tab of the NEPSE Index card, and it updates with each session’s close.

Frequently asked questions

How many sector indices does NEPSE publish?

Thirteen, one per sector: Banking, Development Bank, Finance, Hotels And Tourism, HydroPower, Investment, Life Insurance, Manufacturing And Processing, Microfinance, Mutual Fund, Non Life Insurance, Others, and Trading.

Where can I see the sector indices?

On NEPSE’s official homepage (nepalstock.com.np), in the NEPSE Index card’s Sub Indices tab. The values update with each session’s close.

Why did 12 sectors fall and one rise on 17 November?

That is what the data shows: Mutual Fund rose 0.12% while the other twelve fell between 0.02% and 1.21%. The sub-indices report the move; they do not explain why it happened.

Can I compare two sectors by their index levels?

No. Levels sit on different scales (Life Insurance 11,718.08 vs Mutual Fund 19.94 on the same day). Compare the percentage change, not the level.

Does a sector index tell me which shares to buy?

No. It describes where a move happened, on one day. It is not a recommendation, and this article is educational information only, not investment advice.

What is the difference between the NEPSE Index and a sector index?

The NEPSE Index covers the whole market; a sector index covers only the stocks mapped to that sector. NEPSE also publishes three other headline indices — Sensitive, Float, and Sensitive Float — alongside the NEPSE Index.
