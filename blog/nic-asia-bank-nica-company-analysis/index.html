NICA at Rs 331: priced like a normal bank on book value, priced like a crisis on earnings

NIC Asia Bank earned Rs 1.22 a share in FY2082/83 after losing money the year before, carries a 9.26% bad-loan ratio and negative distributable profit — yet at Rs 331 the market still pays 1.92 times book value, almost exactly the peer median. This analysis separates the price the market is paying from the earnings it is ignoring, and shows every calculation.

By Nepse Decode Research Desk · Kathmandu · November 2, 2026 · 24 min read
Facebook X LinkedIn WhatsApp Viber

NIC Asia Bank (NICA) is the hardest commercial bank on NEPSE to value honestly, because its two valuation lenses point in opposite directions. On earnings, it is the most expensive bank in the country: Rs 331 divided by Rs 1.22 of annual earnings is 271 times. On book value, it is utterly ordinary: Rs 331 divided by Rs 172.32 of book value per share is 1.92 times, almost exactly the 1.87 times median of six comparable commercial banks. Both numbers are arithmetic on the same published figures. The market is not confused; it is telling you which lens it trusts. It has stopped pricing NIC Asia on what it earned last year, and started pricing it on what its balance sheet is still worth. This article tests whether that judgement survives contact with the bank's bad loans, its thin capital buffer and its negative distributable profit — and shows every calculation so you can test it yourself.

02. Valuation view: three lenses, no single number

A company analysis that prints one "value" for NIC Asia would be dishonest, because the bank's earnings are too depressed for the earnings lens to work. The standing rule for thin or depressed earnings applies here in full: the earnings lens is shown but never alone, the book lens carries weight, the output is a range with the drivers that would move it, and no false-precision point target is printed. Peer medians are re-run fresh at the 30 October 2026 closes below — nothing is carried from an earlier note.

Peer selection. Six commercial banks, all in the same sector, all traded on 30 October 2026, all with positive Q4 FY2082/83 earnings per share and a published book value per share: Nabil, Global IME, Kumari, Everest, NMB and Sanima. Prabhu Bank is excluded because its EPS is negative (Rs −1.02), which makes a price-to-earnings ratio meaningless, not low. Other banks are omitted because their 30 October closes were not re-verified in this run — the peer set is these six, stated openly, so every median below recomputes from the table. Median rule. With six peers, the median is the mean of the middle two values. Dropping the lowest and highest price-to-earnings ratios (Kumari 8.48× and Everest 19.68×) leaves the median unchanged at 16.12× — the median is not being driven by an outlier at either end.

Read the three lenses together and the market's judgement is legible. It refuses to capitalise Rs 1.22 of earnings at any normal multiple — correctly, because Rs 1.22 is not a normal year's earnings (see §03). It instead pays roughly what peers fetch per rupee of book value, which is a bet that the book value itself — Rs 172.32 a share — survives the bad-loan cleanup. That bet is exactly what §04 tests: if provisioning eats further into net worth, the book lens falls with it, and the price has no earnings leg to stand on.

03. Earnings: a loss, then a near-zero profit

NIC Asia lost Rs 3.87 billion in FY2081/82 and made about Rs 0.18 billion in FY2082/83. The sector summary that reports these two figures describes the move as a "decline by 104.65%". That word is wrong and this article does not repeat it: moving from a Rs 3.87 billion loss to a Rs 0.18 billion profit is a swing of Rs 4.05 billion in the right direction, and 104.65% is the size of that swing measured against the prior year's loss (4.05 ÷ 3.87). What the swing does not do is restore health. Rs 0.18 billion on Rs 14.92 billion of paid-up capital is Rs 1.22 a share — the second-lowest EPS among all 20 commercial banks, ahead of only loss-making Prabhu Bank at Rs −1.02, and far below the industry average of Rs 18.64. The newest data point complicates, rather than settles, the picture. For the first quarter of FY2083/84 (the quarter ended mid-October 2026), NIC Asia's own Q1 statement, published 1 November 2026, puts its quarterly net profit at Rs 127.44 million, up 4.32% year on year — which implies about Rs 122.2 million in the same quarter last year (127.44 ÷ 1.0432, computed). The sector as a whole earned Rs 19.14 billion in the quarter, up 45.59% from Rs 13.15 billion (FiscalNepal's 1 November 2026 summary of the banks' unaudited statements); NIC Asia's share of that is 0.67% (127.44 ÷ 19,145.4, computed), against its 3.63% share of the sector's paid-up capital (14.92 ÷ 411.17, computed). On the statement-based quarterly figures, NIC Asia remains a marginal earner in its own sector.

But there is a conflict in the record, and it is printed here rather than smoothed over. Nepal Rastra Bank-based releases for the first two months of FY2083/84 (Shrawan–Bhadra) put NIC Asia's profit at about Rs 499 million for two months — a figure that cannot sit alongside a Rs 127.44 million three-month total unless the third month was deeply negative, and the sector totals conflict the same way (Rs 19.46 billion for two months on one release, Rs 17.81 billion on another, against Rs 19.14 billion for the full quarter on the statement summary). The two series evidently measure different things or different dates, and no published summary reconciles them. This article therefore does not annualise the quarterly figure, does not use the two-month figure as a run-rate, and treats NIC Asia's own published Q1 statement (1 November 2026, Rs 127.44 million) as the document that describes the quarter — while the two-month figure remains unreconciled and unexplained. Any analysis that annualises Rs 127.44 million into a "recovered" EPS without mentioning this conflict is selling precision the record does not contain.

04. Asset quality and capital: the repair bill

If earnings are the symptom, asset quality is the diagnosis. NIC Asia's non-performing loan ratio is 9.26%: nearly one rupee in eleven of its loans is not performing, against a 5.44% industry average. Only a handful of banks are worse — Prabhu Bank, at 15.55%, is the extreme — while the cleanest, Everest at 0.49%, shows what the other end of the industry looks like. A 9.26% NPL ratio does two things at once: it forces provisioning, which is what crushed FY2081/82 into a loss and held FY2082/83 to Rs 1.22 a share, and it ties up capital that could otherwise support new lending. The sector's total impairment charge for FY2082/83 was Rs 37.30 billion; NIC Asia's own charge could not be read from a named source at publish time (see the gap note in the signal summary) and is not estimated here.

Capital tells the same story from the other side. NIC Asia's capital adequacy ratio is 11.10%, the lowest of the 20 commercial banks, against a 12.85% industry average; the strongest, Standard Chartered Bank Nepal at 18.13%, holds roughly two-thirds more buffer per rupee of risk-weighted assets. A low CAR leaves little room for further provisioning without either shrinking the loan book or raising capital — and with a 61.07% credit-to-deposit ratio, the bank is already lending cautiously relative to its deposits (Prime Commercial Bank, at the other end, lends at 83.36%). Its base rate is the industry's highest at 5.83%, against Rastriya Banijya Bank's lowest at 4.20%, and its interest spread is 3.50%: the bank charges more for money than its peers, which is consistent with a lender pricing in its own risk — and with a lender struggling to win good borrowers at those prices. None of this is a prediction of failure. It is the arithmetic of a thin buffer: the book value the market is paying for (§02) has less protection than the peer average.

05. Dividends: the last one was FY2079/80

The latest dividend in NIC Asia's history as checked for this article is for FY2079/80: 30.52% in total, made up of 29% bonus shares and 1.52% cash for tax purposes, announced on 15 September 2023 with book closure on 3 October 2023. That was three fiscal years ago. The reason no dividend follows from FY2082/83 is visible in one number: distributable profit per share of negative Rs 99.65, the lowest of the 20 banks (Everest, at the top, reports Rs 38.32; Himalayan, next-worst after NIC Asia, reports Rs −43.58). Distributable profit is the accumulated pool a bank may legally pay out; a negative pool means there is nothing to distribute until future profits fill a computed deficit of about Rs 14.87 billion (Rs 99.65 × 149,175,670 shares, computed). No FY2082/83 dividend proposal for NIC Asia was found in the sources checked at publish time. Shareholders waiting for a dividend are therefore waiting on two separate events, in order: first, audited FY2082/83 accounts approved at an annual general meeting, and second, enough subsequent profit to turn distributable profit positive. The first is a calendar event; the second is an earnings event, and on FY2082/83's Rs 1.22 a share it is not close.

06. The company: from NIC Bank to NIC Asia

NIC Asia Bank Limited is a Kathmandu-based 'A' class commercial bank headquartered at Trade Tower, Thapathali, Kathmandu. It was founded as Nepal Industrial and Commercial (NIC) Bank on 21 July 1998 and renamed NIC Asia Bank on 30 June 2013, after merging with Bank of Asia Nepal — the first merger between two commercial banks in Nepalese banking history, with final Nepal Rastra Bank approval on 26 April 2013. It listed on the Nepal Stock Exchange on 25 July 2007 in the Commercial Banks sector and trades under the symbol NICA. By market capitalisation it ranks 14th of the 19 actively traded commercial banks — a mid-to-small bank by market value despite its Rs 14.92 billion paid-up capital, because the price has fallen toward the bottom of its range.

Ownership is split 51% promoter and 49% public: 76,079,591 promoter shares and 73,096,078 public shares out of 149,175,670 listed shares. The 49% public float is worth Rs 24.19 billion at Rs 331, which makes NICA liquid enough to trade — 308,851 shares changed hands on 30 October alone — but the promoter majority means control is not contestable through the market. Nothing in this article depends on who the promoters are; what matters for valuation is that the float is large enough for the Rs 331 price to be a real market price, formed in 837 trades on the day, not a quotation on thin volume.

07. The price path: a quiet climb back from Rs 309

The price path gives the book-value story some support and some warning in equal measure. NIC Asia closed at Rs 309 on 7 October 2026 and at Rs 331 on 30 October — a computed rise of 7.1% ((331 − 309) ÷ 309) across three and a half weeks that included the post-Dashain sessions. That climb, from near the Rs 302.90 52-week low toward the middle of nothing in particular, looks like a market re-rating a bank off its floor rather than chasing a recovery: the 1-year yield is just 2.19%, against 28.91% for Sanima, 26.46% for Kumari and 16.32% for NMB over the same published window. NIC Asia's shareholders have, over the year, barely beaten holding cash — while holding a bank with a 9.26% bad-loan ratio. The 30 October session itself was soft (down 1.19% on the day, on heavy volume of 308,851 shares), which fits a stock whose buyers and sellers disagree loudly rather than one quietly trending.

08. Risks and what to watch

  • The Q1 FY2083/84 statement (published 1 November 2026). It confirms the quarterly profit at Rs 127.44 million but does not settle the two-month-versus-quarter conflict in §03 — the two-month figure remains unreconciled. Watch the NPL ratio and the impairment charge first, the profit figure second: a profit built on lower provisioning is not the same as a profit built on recoveries.
  • The audited FY2082/83 accounts and the AGM. Unaudited Q4 figures get restated — in other sectors, restatements have cut published profits sharply. If the audit moves net worth, the Rs 172.32 book value and the whole book lens move with it. The AGM's dividend decision will confirm what the negative distributable profit already implies.
  • Capital. At 11.10% CAR, the lowest in the industry, NIC Asia has the least room to absorb further provisioning. Any capital raising — rights, debentures or a merger conversation — would reprice the share count and every per-share figure in this article. No such plan was found in the sources checked; that absence is noted, not assumed permanent.
  • Concentration of the thesis. The entire investment case at Rs 331 rests on book value surviving. If NPL recoveries stall and provisioning rises, book value falls, the peer-multiple book lens falls with it, and there is no earnings leg to catch the price — Rs 1.22 of EPS cannot support Rs 331 at any normal multiple.
  • What would change this article's mind. Two consecutive quarters of falling NPL with provisioning covered by operating profit, a CAR moving back toward the 12.85% industry average, and a distributable-profit deficit shrinking rather than growing. Until then, "priced on book value" is a description of the market's bet, not an endorsement of it.

What to take from it

NIC Asia at Rs 331 is a bank priced as if its balance sheet were normal and its earnings were an accident. The balance sheet is not quite normal — 9.26% bad loans, the industry's lowest capital ratio, negative distributable profit — but the earnings really are the accident: a Rs 3.87 billion loss followed by a Rs 0.18 billion profit is not a run-rate, it is a repair in progress. The honest valuation is therefore the range, not a number: about Rs 322 on peer-median book value, Rs 241 to Rs 488 across the full peer book-multiple range, and an earnings lens (Rs 19.67) that exists only to prove it should not be used. Anyone who tells you NIC Asia is "cheap" because some multiple looks low, or "expensive" because 271 times earnings looks high, is using one lens and hiding the other. Use both, watch the November statement, and let the bad-loan ratio — not the share price — tell you when the repair is real.

Frequently asked questions

What is NICA?

NICA is the NEPSE symbol of NIC Asia Bank Limited, a Kathmandu-based 'A' class commercial bank headquartered at Trade Tower, Thapathali. It began as Nepal Industrial and Commercial (NIC) Bank on 21 July 1998, became NIC Asia Bank on 30 June 2013 after merging with Bank of Asia Nepal, and listed on NEPSE on 25 July 2007.

Why is NIC Asia's price-to-earnings ratio 271 times?

Because the denominator collapsed, not because the price soared. Rs 331 divided by Rs 1.22 of FY2082/83 earnings per share is 271.31 times. The peer median is 16.12 times. The ratio measures how depressed earnings are; it is not evidence that the share is 17 times "more expensive" than peers in any useful sense.

Is Rs 331 expensive for NIC Asia?

On book value, no: Rs 331 is 1.92 times book value of Rs 172.32, against a 1.87 times peer median, giving a book-implied figure of Rs 322.24 — the price sits 2.7% above it. On earnings, the question has no meaningful answer while EPS is Rs 1.22. The price is fair on the lens the market is using and indefensible on the lens it is ignoring.

Will NIC Asia pay a dividend for FY2082/83?

Nothing found at publish time proposes one, and the arithmetic argues against it: distributable profit per share is negative Rs 99.65, a computed deficit of about Rs 14.87 billion that future profits must fill before any distribution. The last declared dividend was for FY2079/80 (30.52%: 29% bonus plus 1.52% cash). The audited accounts and the annual general meeting will confirm the position.

How bad is a 9.26% non-performing loan ratio?

Bad, but not the worst in the industry. The commercial-bank average is 5.44%, the cleanest bank (Everest) is at 0.49%, and the worst (Prabhu) is at 15.55%. At 9.26%, roughly one rupee in eleven of NIC Asia's loans is not performing, which is why provisioning has consumed its earnings for two years.

What does the first quarter of FY2083/84 show?

NIC Asia's own Q1 FY2083/84 statement, published 1 November 2026, puts its Q1 profit at Rs 127.44 million, up 4.32% year on year — 0.67% of the sector's Rs 19.14 billion quarterly profit (FiscalNepal's 1 November 2026 summary). But Nepal Rastra Bank-based two-month releases imply about Rs 499 million for two months, which cannot be reconciled with the quarterly figure. The published Q1 statement settles the quarterly number; the two-month figure remains unexplained.

What should I watch next for NIC Asia?

In order: the Q1 FY2083/84 statement (NPL ratio and impairment charge before the profit headline), the audited FY2082/83 accounts and AGM dividend decision, and the capital adequacy ratio against the 12.85% industry average. Two quarters of falling NPL with provisioning covered by operating profit would be the first real evidence the repair is working.
