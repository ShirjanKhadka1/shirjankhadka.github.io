NABIL at Rs 549: the biggest profit in Nepali banking, priced above both lenses

Nabil Bank earned Rs 2.55 billion in a single quarter — more than any other commercial bank — and its shares closed at Rs 549 on November 6. On the latest quarterly figures, that price sits above what its peers’ multiples would pay for its earnings and for its book. The premium is real. The question is whether the quarter that justifies it can repeat.

By Nepse Decode Research Desk · Kathmandu · November 8, 2026 · 28 min read
Facebook X LinkedIn WhatsApp Viber

Nabil Bank is the biggest bank on NEPSE by market value, the most profitable commercial bank in the country last quarter, and the subject of the most ordinary question in investing: is the price fair? At Rs 549, the market is paying 16.20 times the bank’s annualised first-quarter earnings and 2.35 times its book value. Its six closest listed peers, on the same quarterly basis, trade at a median of 13.87 times earnings and 1.84 times book. That gap is the whole story of this article: a premium of about 17% on earnings and about 28% on book, for a bank whose latest quarter was the strongest in the sector — and whose profit jump was driven mostly by provisions falling, not by lending growing. Nothing here is a recommendation to buy or sell. It is a measurement, with the workings shown.

Signal summary

  • The price: Rs 549 at the November 6 close (−0.18% on the day), about two-thirds of the way up its 52-week range of Rs 485–581. NEPSE itself ended the week almost flat at 2,655.22.
  • The quarter: first-quarter FY2083/84 net profit of Rs 2.55 billion, up 45.43% year on year — the largest of any commercial bank, 13.3% of the sector’s Rs 19.14 billion. Annualised earnings per share Rs 33.89; book value per share Rs 233.90.
  • The catch: impairment charges fell 74.7% (Rs 647.2 million to Rs 164 million). Net interest income grew 21.3% — the durable part of the improvement. Against the audited year-end loan book, lending was essentially flat in the quarter.
  • The valuation: the earnings lens is Rs 470.05 (peer median P/E 13.87×) and the book lens is Rs 429.21 (peer median P/B 1.84×). The Rs 549 price sits 16.8% above the first and 27.9% above the second.
  • The dividend: 15.80% for FY2082/83 (5% bonus shares plus 10.80% cash), approved at the 42nd AGM on October 8. At Rs 549 that is a 2.88% yield, 1.97% of it in cash — and the books closed on September 30, so a buyer today does not receive it.
  • The lenses are computed comparisons, not values, targets or forecasts. There is no DCF lens: no verified discount rate, forecast or payout schedule exists to build one from.

02. Valuation view: two lenses, both below the price

The valuation follows the same method as the earlier company analyses in this series: take six genuinely comparable listed commercial banks, all recently traded, re-run their multiples at the latest verified closes, and publish the full table so every median recomputes. The peers are Global IME, Everest, NMB, Sanima, Kumari and Standard Chartered — the sector’s other large, profitable, normally-traded banks. Two exclusions are stated, not hidden: Rastriya Banijya Bank is unlisted, so it has no market price; NIC Asia is excluded as a distressed-earnings outlier (its annualised first-quarter EPS is Rs 3.42 and its full-year EPS Rs 1.22 — see the NIC Asia analysis of November 2), and the same reasoning excludes Agricultural Development Bank (negative EPS) and Citizens Bank (Rs 2.68). 03. The quarter: Rs 2.55 billion, led by a collapse in impairment

Nabil Bank’s first-quarter statement for FY2083/84, published on November 1–2, is the freshest verified picture of the bank, and it is a strong one on the surface. Net profit was Rs 2.55 billion, up 45.43% from Rs 1.76 billion in the same quarter last year — the largest quarterly profit of any of Nepal’s 20 commercial banks, ahead of Global IME’s Rs 2.31 billion, and 13.3% of the sector’s combined Rs 19.14 billion. The sector as a whole grew 45.59% in the quarter, so Nabil grew with its industry, not against it; what sets it apart is the scale, not the direction.

The composition of that growth is where the judgement lies. Net interest income rose to Rs 4.73 billion from Rs 3.90 billion, up 21.3% — real, recurring income growth, and annualised it runs at about Rs 18.9 billion against Rs 17.01 billion for all of last year. Net fee and commission income rose 15.1% to Rs 919.4 million, and total operating income rose 16.3% to Rs 6.13 billion. But the largest single contributor to the profit jump was not income at all: impairment charges fell 74.7%, from Rs 647.2 million to Rs 164 million, a saving of about Rs 483 million — roughly three-fifths of the Rs 798 million increase in net profit. Operating profit rose 42.8% to Rs 3.67 billion, and profit before tax matched it at Rs 3.67 billion, up from Rs 2.48 billion. A quarter built on lower provisions is a genuine improvement in the cost of past lending, but it is not the same as a quarter built on more lending: provisions can only fall so far, and next quarter they have almost nothing left to fall from.

The balance sheet tells the same two-sided story. Deposits reached Rs 619.23 billion and total assets Rs 775.66 billion, both up on the year-end. Loans and advances stood at Rs 476.01 billion — but the growth the quarterly statement shows is measured against its own unaudited year-end base of Rs 460.31 billion. Against the audited year-end loan book of Rs 476.87 billion, presented at the AGM, the first-quarter book is essentially flat (−0.2%), and deposits are up only 2.7% on the audited base. The bank got bigger in the quarter mainly because its year-end starting point was restated upward, not because it lent much more. Asset quality did improve on the measure that matters most: the non-performing loan ratio fell to 4.06% from 4.31% a year earlier, against a sector average of 5.64% — mid-pack for a large bank, well behind Everest’s 0.50% and Standard Chartered’s 1.83%, but far from the distressed end where NIC Asia sits at 9.81% and Prabhu Bank at 14.80%. Distributable profit stood at Rs 2.26 billion, the fourth-highest in the sector, behind Everest (Rs 3.87 billion), Siddhartha (Rs 3.57 billion) and Nepal Bank (Rs 2.75 billion) — while the sector as a whole still carries a negative cumulative distributable profit of Rs 9.07 billion.

04. The full year: Rs 7.91 billion, and the audited restatement

The full year behind the quarter is FY2082/83, reported unaudited in August 2026 and audited at the 42nd annual general meeting on October 8. Net profit was Rs 7.91 billion, up 33.50% from Rs 5.92 billion, on operating profit of Rs 11.56 billion (up 25.22%) and net interest income of Rs 17.01 billion (up 4.18%). The same impairment story runs through the full year: charges fell 36.28% to Rs 2.68 billion from Rs 4.20 billion, while personnel expenses rose 5.35% to Rs 5.32 billion. Distributable profit reached Rs 5.16 billion, up 34.07%. The capital adequacy ratio improved to 12.37% from 11.81%, the non-performing loan ratio ended the year at 4.20% (down from 4.48%), and the cost of funds fell sharply to 3.09% from 4.41% — cheaper funding is the quiet tailwind behind the whole sector’s recovery.

The oddity of the full year is that earnings per share fell while profit rose: EPS was Rs 27.74 against Rs 34.13 the year before, down 18.73%, even as net profit grew by a third. The reason is the denominator. Paid-up capital including preference shares rose 18.48% to Rs 32.06 billion in the year, after the bank issued Rs 5 billion of 8% irredeemable non-cumulative perpetual preference shares to strengthen its capital base. More capital spread over more shares means less profit per share, even when the profit itself grows — and it is the same preference-share layer that explains why ShareSansar’s sector table shows a lower EPS for the current quarter (see §02). Book value per share ended the year at Rs 247.28, above the Rs 233.90 of the first quarter — the 5% bonus shares approved in October increased the share count and diluted book value per share, which is why the quarterly figure is lower than the year-end one, not a sign of the book shrinking.

One discrepancy in the full-year record is stated here rather than resolved by picking the friendlier number. The unaudited fourth-quarter release put year-end deposits at Rs 592.56 billion and loans at Rs 460.32 billion; the audited figures presented at the AGM put them at Rs 602.93 billion and Rs 476.87 billion, with total assets of Rs 734.76 billion. The profit figure agrees exactly at Rs 7.91 billion in both versions; the balance-sheet figures do not, by about Rs 10 billion of deposits and Rs 16 billion of loans. This article uses the audited figures for the year-end balance sheet and the unaudited release for the quarterly detail, and says which is which wherever it matters. The prior-year book value per share printed in the unaudited table (Rs 114.2) does not reconcile with the bank’s capital base and is not used.

05. Dividends: 15.80%, and what it yields at Rs 549

Nabil Bank is one of the most consistent dividend payers on NEPSE, and the latest declaration continues that record. For FY2082/83 the board proposed, and the 42nd AGM approved on October 8, a total dividend of 15.80% — 5% in bonus shares and 10.80% in cash — announced on September 7, 2026 with book closure on September 30. The cash distribution began on November 4, and the bonus issue lifts paid-up capital past Rs 33.40 billion. Preference shareholders receive their fixed 8%. The previous year’s dividend was 12.50% in cash, approved at the 41st AGM in January 2026; in FY2078/79 the bank paid 30% (18.50% bonus plus 11.50% cash). The two intervening years’ declarations were not re-verified against a primary notice at publish time and are omitted rather than stated from a secondary source.

06. The company: Nepal’s first joint-venture bank

Nabil Bank Limited began as Nepal Arab Bank Limited, established on July 12, 1984 as Nepal’s first foreign joint-venture commercial bank, and has traded on NEPSE since November 24, 1985 — one of the longest-listed banks in the country. Its head office is Nabil Center on Durbar Marg, Kathmandu. In its most recent merger it acquired Nepal Bangladesh Bank on July 21, 2022, and the bank now operates 268 branches and 316 ATMs across the country. It is the largest commercial bank by market capitalisation (Rs 148.54 billion, about 1.5 times Global IME’s Rs 101.39 billion, the second-largest), and its promoter–public split is 60% promoter and 40% public, with 10.82 crore shares in public hands — a free float of Rs 59.42 billion, the largest in the sector.

The capital structure has one feature no other peer in the table shares: Rs 5 billion of 8% irredeemable non-cumulative perpetual preference shares, issued in FY2082/83 and listed separately as NABILPNP on March 29, 2026. The preference shares pay a fixed 8% before ordinary shareholders receive anything, which is why the ordinary EPS depends on whether the preference dividend is deducted — the Rs 33.89 versus Rs 30.34 question in section 02. They also explain the gap between the two paid-up capital figures in this article: Rs 27.06 billion of ordinary shares, Rs 32.06 billion including the preference shares, and past Rs 33.40 billion once the 5% bonus shares are listed. For a buyer of the ordinary shares, the preference layer is a prior claim on every year’s profit, fixed at Rs 400 million a year, before the dividend arithmetic even begins.

07. The price path: two-thirds up a narrow 52-week range

NABIL’s 52-week range is narrow for a bank of its size: Rs 485 to Rs 581, a spread of Rs 96, or about 20% from low to high. The November 6 close of Rs 549 sits 67% of the way up that range — closer to the top than the bottom, but not pressing against it. The all-time high, Rs 2,800 in December 2013, belongs to a different era of the market and a much smaller share count, and it is not a meaningful reference for today’s price; the adjusted all-time high of Rs 1,046.73 is the comparable figure, and the current price is about half of it.

08. Risks and what to watch

  • Provisions cannot fall twice. The first-quarter profit jump rested on impairment charges falling from Rs 647.2 million to Rs 164 million. If the second quarter’s charges return to even half their year-ago level, the annualised EPS of Rs 33.89 — the figure both lenses are built on — comes under pressure, and with it the earnings lens itself.
  • Lending is flat on the audited base. Loans were Rs 476.01 billion at the end of the first quarter against an audited Rs 476.87 billion at year-end. A bank that is not growing its loan book is not growing the base that produces next year’s interest income; the 21.3% growth in net interest income came from cheaper funding and better margins, and the cost of funds at 3.09% has limited room to fall further.
  • Asset quality is mid-pack, not best-in-class. An NPL ratio of 4.06% is better than the sector’s 5.64% but far above Everest’s 0.50% and Standard Chartered’s 1.83%. Any deterioration would hit provisions first, which is exactly the line that delivered this quarter’s growth.
  • The preference-share drag is permanent. Rs 400 million a year goes to preference shareholders before ordinary shareholders see a rupee, and the preference shares are perpetual. The Rs 33.89 EPS that the market is paying 16.20× for is the figure before that deduction is applied the way ShareSansar applies it.
  • The bonus shares are still to list. The 5% bonus approved in October lifts the share count to about 28.41 crore ordinary shares once listed; market capitalisation and per-share figures will move with that listing, and the 52-week adjusted range (461.90–557) is the one to use after it.
  • Watch the second quarter. The next verified data point is the Q2 FY2083/84 statement, due in late January 2027: whether impairment stays low, whether the loan book starts growing on the audited base, and whether the NPL ratio keeps falling. The Q1 capital adequacy ratio was not published in the sources used here; the latest verified figure is 12.37% at the end of FY2082/83.

What to take from it

Nabil Bank at Rs 549 is the clearest example in this series of a good bank at a full price. It earned more than any other commercial bank last quarter, it is the biggest bank on the exchange by value, it pays a dividend every year, and its bad loans are falling. The market knows all of that, and has charged for it: 16.20 times annualised first-quarter earnings and 2.35 times book, against peer medians of 13.87 and 1.84. The lenses say Rs 470.05 on earnings and Rs 429.21 on book — computed comparisons, not values — and the price is above both. The premium is earned by the profit, but the profit was earned in large part by provisions falling, and provisions cannot fall twice. If the second quarter shows the loan book growing and the impairment line staying low, the premium has a foundation. If it shows the reverse, the lenses will have been the cheaper read. Nothing in this article is a recommendation to buy or sell any share.

Frequently asked questions

What does Nabil Bank do, and how big is it?

Nabil Bank is a Kathmandu-based ‘A’ class commercial bank, established in 1984 as Nepal’s first foreign joint-venture bank, with its head office at Nabil Center on Durbar Marg. It is the largest commercial bank on NEPSE by market capitalisation (Rs 148.54 billion), with 268 branches and 316 ATMs, deposits of Rs 619.23 billion and total assets of Rs 775.66 billion at the end of the first quarter of FY2083/84.

Why is the price above both lenses if the bank is doing so well?

Because the market is charging for the strength. At Rs 549 the bank trades at 16.20 times annualised first-quarter earnings and 2.35 times book, against peer medians of 13.87 and 1.84. The lenses — Rs 470.05 on earnings and Rs 429.21 on book — are what the price would be if Nabil traded at the peer medians. A premium is not an error; it is the market’s judgement that Nabil’s earnings deserve a higher multiple. The article’s question is whether the quarter behind that judgement can repeat.

Is the 45% profit jump sustainable?

Partly. Net interest income grew 21.3% and fee income 15.1%, which is durable if lending and margins hold. But about three-fifths of the profit increase came from impairment charges falling 74.7%, from Rs 647.2 million to Rs 164 million — and charges that low have almost nothing left to fall from. The second quarter will show whether the growth has a second engine.

What dividend would I get if I buy at Rs 549?

Not the 15.80% just declared: the books closed on September 30, 2026, so that dividend — Rs 15.80 per share, a 2.88% yield at Rs 549, with Rs 10.80 of it in cash — goes to whoever held the shares then. A buyer today is paying for the next dividend, which will be judged against the distributable profit of Rs 2.26 billion the bank carried into the second quarter.

What are Nabil’s preference shares, and why do they change the EPS?

The bank issued Rs 5 billion of 8% irredeemable non-cumulative perpetual preference shares in FY2082/83, listed separately as NABILPNP. They are paid a fixed 8% — Rs 400 million a year — before ordinary shareholders receive anything. The bank’s reported annualised EPS of Rs 33.89 does not deduct that preference dividend the way ShareSansar’s sector table does, which shows Rs 30.34. The article uses Rs 33.89 because it reconciles exactly with the published P/E of 16.20×, and discloses the alternative.

How does NABIL compare with NIC Asia, the other bank analysed recently?

They are in different universes at the moment. NIC Asia’s full-year EPS was Rs 1.22 and its P/E 271.31× at Rs 331 on October 30, with an NPL ratio of 9.26% and a distributable profit that was negative. Nabil’s annualised EPS is Rs 33.89, its P/E 16.20×, its NPL 4.06% and its distributable profit Rs 2.26 billion. The NIC Asia analysis (November 2, 2026) explains why that bank is excluded from Nabil’s peer set.

What should I watch next?

The second-quarter FY2083/84 statement, due in late January 2027: whether impairment charges stay low, whether the loan book starts growing against the audited year-end base of Rs 476.87 billion, and whether the NPL ratio keeps falling from 4.06%. Also watch the listing of the 5% bonus shares, which will lift the ordinary share count to about 28.41 crore and move the per-share figures with it.
