NEPSE on trial: what Parliament’s Finance Committee heard about the capital market

Investor leaders accused the Finance Ministry of siding with vested interests. A market expert warned that policy is turning investors into speculators. Meanwhile the government’s 21-point revival plan is already moving. Here is what was said on the record today — and what it means beneath the surface.

By Shirjan Khadka · Aspiring Financial Analyst, Kathmandu · September 30, 2026 · 6 min read
Facebook X LinkedIn

At a glance

  • What happened: the House of Representatives’ Finance Committee held a hearing on capital-market reform on Wednesday morning (14 Ashoj 2083), with the Finance Ministry secretary, SEBON’s chairman, NEPSE’s CEO, the CDS chief, brokers and three investor associations invited.
  • The allegation: Nepal Share Market Investors Association general secretary Khagendra Raj Kandel alleged the Finance Ministry collaborates mainly with vested-interest groups, and demanded a new Securities Act — the current one dates to 2063 BS, roughly 20 years old.
  • The expert warning: market expert Ajaya Kumar Shrestha said capital-market policy is trading-oriented rather than investment-oriented, turning retail investors into speculators; he called for one unified SEBON disclosure platform and open TMS competition.
  • The industry view: Nepal Shareholders Association chairman Ghanshyam Pande blamed promoter “IPO farming” and lock-in-expiry share dumps, while supporting SEBON’s draft IPO directive and thanking the ministry for its action plan.
  • Already moving: the Finance Ministry’s 21-point Capital Market Revival Action Plan (Bhadra 29), the Cabinet’s capital-gains tax cut to 3.5%/5% (September 16), and SEBON’s draft Public Issuance Qualification Directive, 2083 — now open for public comment.

The capital market got its day in Parliament this morning. The House Finance Committee summoned the entire plumbing of Nepal’s share market to Singha Durbar — the Finance Ministry secretary, SEBON’s chairman, NEPSE’s chief executive, the CDS chief, the broker association and three investor associations — to answer a single question: how do you make this market orderly, transparent, trustworthy and investor-friendly? What came back was three blunt diagnoses, delivered on the record. And beneath the noise, something important: the government’s own 21-point revival plan is already in motion.

Who was in the room, and why does it matter?

The committee’s published agenda for the 8:30 AM session was explicit: discussion on securities issuance and listing, market operation and regulation, and investor-interest protection, with the bodies and associations connected to the capital market. When a parliamentary finance committee puts the regulator, the exchange, the depository and the investor lobby in one room, it is a signal the issue has political weight — this was not a routine briefing. The statements below were made on the record at that hearing, as reported by multiple Nepali outlets present; each position is attributed to its speaker, because an allegation made in Parliament is still an allegation, not an established fact.

What did the investor associations actually allege?

The sharpest words came from Khagendra Raj Kandel, general secretary of the Nepal Share Market Investors Association. He alleged that the Finance Ministry pays little attention to the bodies that should care about the share market and instead collaborates mostly with limited, vested-interest groups — and that the market’s current condition is the result of the ministry’s priorities. That is a serious charge, and it was made as an allegation at the hearing, not as a proven finding.

His concrete demand was legislative: a new, modern Securities Act. The governing law dates to 2063 BS (2006 AD) — roughly twenty years old, written before demat trading, online TMS and most of today’s market structure existed. Kandel argued the market is being run on two-decade-old law and directives while global markets have moved on. He added two striking claims about enforcement: that manipulators moving billions face one to one-and-a-half years of jail on paper but walk free on Rs 40,000–50,000 fines, and that most SEBON chairmen end up resigning and fleeing — asking whether resignation can really absolve proven wrongdoing. These are his assertions from the hearing; the data behind them was not tabled in the reporting.

Ghanshyam Pande, chairman of the Nepal Shareholders Association, aimed lower at market structure than at the ministry. He blamed two things for the market’s condition: promoters’ “IPO farming” and a weak regulator. His most vivid line: the practice of dumping promoter shares the moment the three-year lock-in expires poses more risk to the secondary market than the Bhotekoshi flood. He also flagged the flood of right shares in hydropower and manufacturing straining supply absorption, complained that margin lending and broker automation still don’t work in practice — the law allows a Rs 4 lakh limit on a Rs 1 lakh deposit, he said, but investors struggle to actually get it — and rejected the argument that the 10-share IPO allotment policy ruined the market, defending it as small-investor access. Notably, he thanked the Finance Ministry for its capital-market action plan and supported SEBON’s proposed IPO issuance directive — a reminder that the industry is not uniformly hostile to the official reform track.

What was the expert’s warning about “trading-oriented” policy?

Market expert Ajaya Kumar Shrestha gave the hearing its analytical spine. His core argument: Nepal’s capital-market policies are oriented toward trading and short-term profit rather than investor interest, and the design risks turning retail investors into speculators with a gambling mentality — which, he said, does not work well in a market like Nepal’s. He pointed to weak investigation of manipulation, insider trading, circular trading and pump-and-dump schemes.

His most counter-intuitive point deserves attention: governance reform may push the index down, and that should be accepted. Even if the NEPSE fell from 2,600 to 2,000 while market infrastructure improves, he said, the correction should be taken as natural — building infrastructure and demanding the index always rise cannot go together. That is a statement about trade-offs, not a price prediction, and it cuts against the reflex that every reform must be judged by next week’s index level.

His practical proposals were specific: bring all disclosures onto a single SEBON digital platform (information is scattered today, feeding misinformation); standardize stalled IPOs on governance criteria and move them forward, because frozen IPOs also choke private capital and venture-capital exits; evaluate market readiness before introducing complex tools like short selling and margin trading; open up competition in broking and TMS with open API access — arguing that data monopoly stifles market technology; and stop discouraging in practice what book building and premium-IPO price discovery promise on paper.

What has the government already put on the table?

Here is the part the shouting can obscure: the official reform track is already moving, and the hearing happened inside it, not outside it.

On Bhadra 29, the Finance Ministry published its Capital Market Strengthening and Revival Action Plan, 2083 — a 21-point package unveiled by Finance Minister Dr Swarnim Wagle. Acting on it, a mid-September Cabinet meeting cut the capital gains tax on listed-share sales to 3.5% for holdings over 365 days and 5% for shorter holdings, roughly halving the budget’s rates after revenue from the higher rates collapsed 54%. The full arithmetic of that episode is broken down in this earlier piece.

Then SEBON moved on issuance quality: the board published the draft “Public Issuance General Qualification Directive, 2083” for public comment — a risk-based qualification system for IPOs covering eligibility, governance, prospectus disclosure, due diligence and post-issue obligations, framed as the first implementation step of the ministry’s action plan. The draft is already drawing fire: IPPAN, the independent power producers’ association, has formally asked SEBON to carve out hydropower, arguing one-size-fits-all net-worth and operating-history tests would block under-construction projects from raising public capital at the stage they need it most. Whether the directive survives that pushback in its current form is now the reform to watch.

What should investors actually watch next?

Four things. First, the committee’s own conclusions — Wednesday’s hearing produced testimony, not decisions; what the Finance Committee recommends next will determine whether this becomes law or theatre. Second, SEBON’s draft IPO directive after the comment window closes: the hydro carve-out fight will set the tone for every issuance rule that follows. Third, the Gazette: the new 3.5%/5% tax rates apply after formal publication and system updates — confirm the live rate in settlement notes before transacting. Fourth, the Securities Act itself: Kandel’s demand for a new law is the longest fuse in the room, and the one that would matter most.

The honest summary of Wednesday morning: the stakeholders agree the market is badly governed and disagree on whose fault it is. The government, for its part, has stopped defending the status quo — the action plan, the tax reversal and the draft directive are all admissions that the old settings failed. Reform that everyone applauds on day one rarely survives contact with vested interests; the test is what the directive, the Act and the committee’s recommendations look like six months from now.
