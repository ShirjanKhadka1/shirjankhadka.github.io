Company Analysis · NEPSE: HLBSL

Profits came roaring back at Himalayan Laghubitta. The bad-loan meter keeps climbing.

HLBSL's FY 2081/82 profit jumped 49% to Rs 106.6 million, it paid a 7% dividend after skipping one, and the stock trades at 5.6 times book. Meanwhile non-performing loans crossed 10%. Here is what the numbers actually say.

By Shirjan Khadka · Founder, Nepse Decode, Kathmandu · September 29, 2026 · 10 min read

At a glance

  • What it is: Himalayan Laghubitta Bittiya Sanstha Limited (NEPSE: HLBSL) is a national-level microfinance institution, a 51% subsidiary of Himalayan Bank. It began operations in 2012 as Civil Laghubitta, serves 81,000+ members through 75 branches, and is headquartered at Chuchepati, Kathmandu.
  • FY 2081/82: net profit Rs 106.60 million, up 49.18% from Rs 71.40 million. Net interest income Rs 442.10 million (from Rs 370.80 million), operating profit Rs 152.10 million (from Rs 105.40 million), EPS Rs 31.26. NPL rose to 9.04%.
  • The dividend: 7% for FY 2081/82 (6.65% bonus shares worth Rs 2.12 crore plus 0.35% cash worth Rs 11.19 lakh), endorsed at the 14th AGM on January 12, 2026. For FY 2079/80, no dividend was paid at all.
  • Market snapshot (September 28, 2026): last traded price Rs 850.00, market capitalization about Rs 2.90 arba, price-to-earnings 26.83x on EPS Rs 31.68, price-to-book 5.64x on book value Rs 150.76 per share. 52-week range: Rs 830 to Rs 1,075.
  • The worry: NPL climbed from 5.62% (Q2 FY 2081/82) to 9.04% (FY-end) to 10.04% (Q3 FY 2082/83), and the AGM endorsed merger and acquisition agendas.

Microfinance on NEPSE is a study in contradictions, and Himalayan Laghubitta is one of the sharpest examples. This is a company whose profit nearly doubled in a single year, that paid its first meaningful dividend in years, and whose stock still trades at almost six times book value. It is also a company where one in ten rupees of loans has stopped performing, and whose shareholders just endorsed merger talks. Both facts are true at the same time, and any honest read of HLBSL has to hold both.

That is not a verdict on the stock. It is the factual shape of the business, and it is worth understanding piece by piece: what the company does, how its finances actually moved, who owns and runs it, what the dividend signals, what the market is charging for it, and where it stands against its peers.

What does Himalayan Laghubitta actually do?

Himalayan Laghubitta is a D-class bank and financial institution: a national-level microfinance company licensed by Nepal Rastra Bank to take deposits from and lend to the unbanked and underbanked. In practice that means small, mostly collateral-free loans to micro-entrepreneurs: vegetable vendors, small shopkeepers, livestock farmers, and rural households building income-generating activities. Its published lending rate is 15%, and it pays 7.5% to 8.91% on deposits, per its own quarterly disclosures.

The institution began operations on January 5, 2012, originally as Civil Laghubitta Bittiya Sanstha, and was renamed after coming under the Himalayan Bank umbrella. It is headquartered at Chuchepati, Kathmandu, operates 75 branch offices, and reported serving over 75,000 members in 2024, a figure that had grown past 81,000 by the time of its 14th AGM in January 2026, when the company said it manages loans worth about Rs 5 arba. Its loan book stood at Rs 4.96 billion at the end of FY 2081/82, funded by Rs 5.28 billion of deposits and borrowings, with total assets of Rs 6.14 billion.

The business model is high-touch and high-cost: hundreds of field staff collecting small weekly repayments across the country. That structure is exactly why two numbers decide this company's fate every year: the spread it earns between 15% lending and ~8% funding, and the share of loans that stop repaying.

Three things stand out. First, the profit recovery is real and broad: net interest income grew 19.23%, operating profit grew 44.31%, and net profit grew 49.18%. Second, the loan book barely grew (+1.43%), so the profit jump came from margins and cost control, not aggressive expansion. The cost of funds fell through the year: 7.65% in Q2 FY 2081/82, down from 9.31% a year earlier, as interest rates declined across the system. Third, and unavoidably, the NPL ratio ended the year at 9.04%, meaning nearly one in eleven rupees lent is not performing.

The quarterly path shows the recovery building through the year. In Q2 FY 2081/82 the company posted a net profit of Rs 26.06 million, up 395.69% from Rs 5.26 million a year earlier, on net interest income of Rs 153.30 million (+30.71%), with an annualized EPS of Rs 16.30, per ShareSansar. But the same report showed NPL at 5.62%, up from 3.59% a year before. The most recent data point, Q3 FY 2082/83, puts NPL at 10.04% with EPS of Rs 31.68 and book value of Rs 150.76 per share, per financialnotices.com. Profits are rising on a shrinking cost of funds; asset quality is deteriorating on a rising NPL. That is the central tension of this company, in two lines.

Who runs the company, and who owns it?

The defining fact of HLBSL's ownership is the parent. Himalayan Bank Limited holds 51% of the company and lists it as a subsidiary in its own financial disclosures, alongside Himalayan Capital and Himalayan Securities. This matters for two practical reasons: the microfinance company borrows heavily from its parent (outstanding borrowings from Himalayan Bank stood near Rs 2.00 billion in a recent related-party disclosure), and the parent's brand and balance sheet stand behind it in stress.

The chairman is Mr. Bijaya Man Nakarmi, who chaired the 14th AGM on January 12, 2026, and the chief executive officer is Mr. Binod Acharya. At that AGM, Ms. Sunita Acharya was unanimously elected as a director for a four-year term. The company's 14th AGM was held at Bougainvilla Events, Tripureshwor, Kathmandu, and among its agendas were the auditor's report, election of directors, a 7% dividend, and, notably, merger and acquisition proposals with other microfinance institutions, including endorsement of due-diligence audits and appointment of valuators, per ShareSansar's AGM filing.

One structural point deserves attention: a 51% parent-owned microfinance company is never fully its own master. Funding lines, strategic direction, and ultimately merger decisions run through Himalayan Bank. The AGM's merger agenda is a reminder that in Nepal's consolidating microfinance sector, HLBSL's future shape may be decided at the parent's table as much as at its own.

What does the 7% dividend tell us?

For FY 2081/82 the board proposed, and the AGM endorsed, a 7% dividend: 6.65% bonus shares (212,679.0566 units worth slightly over Rs 2.12 crore) and a 0.35% cash dividend (Rs 11.19 lakh, including tax), on the paid-up capital of Rs 31.98 crore, per ShareSansar. The bonus shares were listed on NEPSE on March 3, 2026, lifting the listed share count to 3,410,862 and paid-up capital to Rs 34.11 crore. The book closure was January 2, 2026, and the price adjusted to Rs 872.01 from a Rs 930 close.

Read it against the recent past and the dividend looks like a statement. For FY 2079/80 the board proposed no dividend at all, choosing to retain profits as reserves. The distributable profit per share for FY 2081/82 reached Rs 27.13, which made the 7% payout easily affordable on paper. At the September 28, 2026 price of Rs 850, the Rs 7 per share dividend implies a yield of about 0.82%: this is a token of financial health, not an income story. Nobody buys HLBSL at 5.6 times book for the dividend yield.

What is the market paying for HLBSL?

On September 28, 2026, HLBSL last traded at Rs 850.00 (unchanged on the day), for a market capitalization of about Rs 2.90 arba on 3,410,862 listed shares. The 52-week range is Rs 830 to Rs 1,075, so the stock sits roughly 21% below its one-year high and just above its one-year low. The broader NEPSE index closed at 2,613.33 the same day.

On multiples, using the Q3 FY 2082/83 figures: the price-to-earnings ratio is 26.83x on EPS of Rs 31.68, and the price-to-book is 5.64x on a book value of Rs 150.76 per share. The microfinance sub-sector on NEPSE has historically traded at premium book multiples because of high return on equity in good years, but 5.64x book with NPL at 10.04% is pricing in a clean continuation of the recovery. The dividend yield of about 0.82% offers no cushion if sentiment turns.

A useful cross-check: the company's own base rate was 11.99% and its spread rate 8.76% in the latest quarter, with a credit-to-deposit-and-borrowing ratio of 101.94%, meaning it is lending out slightly more than it raises. That is a fully deployed balance sheet. Growth from here has to come from better asset quality and pricing, not from simply lending more.

How does HLBSL compare with microfinance peers?

Nepal's microfinance sector is going through a sector-wide asset-quality strain, and HLBSL's 10.04% NPL does not look like an outlier. Mahila Laghubitta (MLBSL), for example, reported NPL of 9.80% in its Q3 FY 2079/80 filing, with EPS of Rs 32.17 and a market price then around Rs 1,970, per ShareSansar. Rising impairment charges and NPLs have been the sector's shared story for several years, driven by post-Covid borrower stress, political agitation against microfinance collections, and NRB's tighter conduct rules.

Where HLBSL stands out is the parentage. Few listed microfinance companies have a commercial bank holding 51% and extending Rs 2 billion in funding lines. That backing is both a strength (liquidity backstop, brand, governance) and a constraint (strategic autonomy, merger risk). On profitability, FY 2081/82's EPS of Rs 31.26 and the Rs 27.13 distributable profit per share put it in the healthier half of listed MFIs on earnings; on asset quality, the 10% NPL puts it squarely in the sector's problem zone. The peer contrast sharpens the question rather than answering it.

What could go wrong, and what could go right?

Risks. Asset quality is the first, second, and third risk: NPL at 10.04% and climbing, with impairment charges rising alongside it. If provisioning accelerates, it eats directly into the profit recovery the market is paying 26.83x earnings for. The credit-to-deposit ratio above 100% leaves little room for error on funding. The 14th AGM's merger and acquisition agenda is a live variable: consolidation could dilute existing shareholders or, in a distress merger, reprice the equity entirely. And microfinance in Nepal carries a political risk that rarely appears in financial statements: organized borrower resistance to collections has periodically forced NRB interventions across the sector.

Catalysts. The profit recovery is genuine: 49.18% net profit growth, a 19.23% rise in net interest income, and a falling cost of funds as system rates declined. The 7% dividend after a zero-dividend year signals management confidence in distributable profits. The 51% Himalayan Bank ownership provides a funding and credibility backstop few peers enjoy. If NPL stabilizes and the merger agenda resolves into a value-accretive combination, the earnings base of Rs 31-plus EPS gives the company something real to build on.

The honest summary: Himalayan Laghubitta repaired its income statement before it repaired its loan book. Whether the current valuation, about 5.64 times book and 26.83 times earnings, is justified depends entirely on whether the asset-quality problem that the profit recovery is currently outrunning gets fixed before provisions catch up with it.
