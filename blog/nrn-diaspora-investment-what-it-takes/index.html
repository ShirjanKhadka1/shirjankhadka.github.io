Rs 2,363 billion came home last year. Almost none of it became investment — here is what has to change

Nepal received Rs 2,363.13 billion in remittances in fiscal 2025/26 and just Rs 28.49 billion in foreign direct equity investment. The diaspora's money is already here; the channels that would turn it into investment are not. This article maps the one channel that works, the two now being built, and the five conditions that decide whether any of them scale.

By Nepse Decode Research Desk · Kathmandu · November 3, 2026 · 22 min read
Facebook X LinkedIn WhatsApp Viber

Start with the scale of the miss. In fiscal 2025/26, remittance inflows reached Rs 2,363.13 billion, up 37.1% from Rs 1,723.27 billion the year before, according to annual data released by Nepal Rastra Bank and reported by New Business Age. In the same year, equity foreign direct investment — the closest published comparator for money that arrives as investment rather than family support — was Rs 28.49 billion. That is 1.2% of the remittance flow (computed: 28.49 ÷ 2,363.13). Nepal does not have a diaspora-money shortage. It has a diaspora-money conversion problem: hundreds of billions arrive every year as transfers to households, and almost none of it arrives as capital for companies, projects and markets.

This article is not a repeat of the secondary-market-access argument. Nepse Decode covered that promise — and its history of missed deadlines — on 8 October 2026 (The government says NRNs will trade on NEPSE. It has made that promise before.), and covered the Securities Registration and Issuance (Eighth Amendment) Regulations on 7 October 2026 (SEBON's 8th amendment and the 85% IPO). Those two pieces are linked here as prior coverage, not re-argued. The question here is broader and more practical: across every channel by which diaspora money could become investment, what already works, what is still only a framework, and what has to be true before the flow changes direction?

Signal summary

  • The flow: remittances of Rs 2,363.13 billion in fiscal 2025/26 (US$16.19 billion, up 28.1%), against Rs 1,723.27 billion the year before. Gross foreign-exchange reserves reached Rs 3,897.67 billion, covering 23 months of merchandise imports — the money is arriving, and it is piling up as reserves, not as investment.
  • The comparator: equity foreign direct investment of Rs 28.49 billion in the same year, up 137% from Rs 12.02 billion — a fast-growing figure that is still only 1.2% of remittances (computed). Even a small diversion of the remittance flow would dwarf formal investment inflows.
  • The channel that works: the foreign-employment IPO quota — 10% of the general-public portion of hydropower IPOs reserved for Nepali citizens working abroad with valid labour permits. Demand is proven: Beni Hydropower drew 79,232 eligible applicants for 10,400 allotments.
  • The channel being built: the NRN Nepal Development Fund's planned IPO — a Rs 10 billion capital plan with Rs 8.50 billion to be raised through an IPO aimed at 2027, under the Eighth Amendment framework that allows 10% to 85% of issued capital to be offered to non-resident Nepali investors, in foreign currency, at a 1,000-share minimum trading unit.
  • The channel still closed: direct secondary-market trading by NRNs. The 21-point capital-market action plan set a mid-October 2026 target for submitting the two amending Acts to Cabinet; no public record of that submission was found in the sources checked at publish time, while a separate Foreign Investment Act amendment bill is now out for stakeholder consultation.
  • The thesis: diaspora money becomes investment only when five conditions hold at once — the law is passed, accounts can be opened from abroad, repatriation is written down, ticket sizes fit ordinary savers, and the products go beyond shares. Today, only the IPO quota meets all five, and only for one sector.

01. The money is already here

The remittance numbers deserve a second look, because they reframe the whole debate. The final month of fiscal 2025/26 alone brought Rs 242.33 billion, up from Rs 189.11 billion in the same month a year earlier. The balance of payments surplus widened to Rs 1,027.04 billion from Rs 594.54 billion, and the current account surplus more than doubled to Rs 923.56 billion from Rs 409.84 billion. These are Nepal Rastra Bank annual figures as reported by New Business Age. They describe a country whose external position is being carried by its workers abroad — and whose domestic investment channels are capturing almost none of that flow at the point of entry.

Put the comparison in plain terms. If just 5% of fiscal 2025/26 remittances had arrived as investment instead of transfers, that would be Rs 118.16 billion (computed: 5% of 2,363.13) — more than four times the year's entire equity FDI inflow. Nobody needs to persuade the diaspora to send more money. The prize is converting a fraction of what already flows, and the reason it does not convert is not reluctance. It is that, for most of the diaspora, there is still no complete road from a pay cheque abroad to a productive asset at home.

Who counts as the diaspora matters here, because Nepal's law does not treat it as one group. Under the Non-Resident Nepali Act 2008, as summarised by the Nepal Economic Forum, an NRN is either a Nepali citizen who has lived abroad continuously for at least two years, or a foreign citizen of Nepali origin; registered NRNs receive identity cards carrying rights to invest, own certain property and access incentives. In practice the two groups face different doors: citizens working abroad can use the IPO quota below, while foreign citizens of Nepali origin are the audience for the NRN Fund's foreign-currency shares. Any honest account of "diaspora investment" has to say which group it means, because the channels do not treat them the same.

02. The channel that already works: the foreign-employment IPO quota

The one proven converter of diaspora earnings into productive assets is narrow, sector-specific — and oversubscribed many times over. Hydropower IPOs must reserve 10% of the general-public portion for Nepali citizens employed abroad holding valid labour permits, who apply through MeroShare with a demat account linked to a bank's C-ASBA service. The quota is a regulatory requirement, not a favour: it exists to channel remittance earnings into infrastructure assets, and the evidence says the demand is real.

Beni Hydropower is the cleanest test, reported by ICTFrame. Its foreign-employment category offered 104,000 shares in March 2026, drew 79,255 applicants, of whom 79,232 were verified eligible by issue manager NMB Capital, and allotted 10,400 applicants the minimum block of 10 shares each. The success rate was 13.1% (computed: 10,400 ÷ 79,232). The remaining 68,832 eligible applicants received nothing — not because they were ineligible, but because the quota was too small for the demand chasing it. That is a demand signal, not a failure signal: the channel works, and it is rationed by size.

The same quota is live right now in the market's current hydropower issue: Him Parbat Hydropower has set aside 422,500 shares for Nepali migrant workers — again 10% of the general public portion — within a Rs 591.5 million public issue equal to 35% of its issued capital, alongside 1.69 million shares for project-affected locals. The quota's limits are visible in its design. It serves citizens working abroad, not foreign citizens of Nepali origin. It serves hydropower, not the wider market. And it rations by lottery at 10 shares, so a successful applicant owns a token stake, not a portfolio. Useful as proof of demand; far too small as a system.

03. The channel being built: the NRN Fund's foreign-currency shares

The second channel exists in law but not yet in the market. The Securities Registration and Issuance (Eighth Amendment) Regulations, 2083, registered at the Ministry of Law, Justice and Parliamentary Affairs and reported by ShareSansar on 6 October 2026, allow eligible companies — including a company established through joint investment between NRNs and the Government of Nepal — to issue between 10% and 85% of their issued capital specifically to NRNs and Nepali-origin foreign citizens, in foreign currency, with no premium over face value, under a trading system to be determined by SEBON, at a minimum trading unit of 1,000 shares. A SEBON official told ShareSansar that companies seeking approval for such issues can now move ahead, since the legal provision has come into effect.

The intended first user is the NRN Nepal Development Fund. According to Insurance Khabar, the Fund aims to bring its IPO by 2027, on a Rs 10 billion capital plan of which Rs 8.50 billion would be raised through the IPO, with the Government of Nepal reported to hold 5%. The Fund's chief executive, Anil Raj Bhattarai, told ShareSansar that the 1,000-share minimum is deliberate — to make overseas investment practical by reducing the relative cost of sending smaller amounts — and that work is underway on the account and investment systems needed to support such transactions, involving Nepal Rastra Bank, SEBON, NEPSE and CDS and Clearing, so that returns can be taken abroad in foreign currency, subject to the required systems and rules.

Read that paragraph carefully, because it contains the whole gap between framework and market. The regulation is registered. The company is named. The IPO is aimed at 2027. But the account systems, the trading system for these shares, and the repatriation mechanics are described as work underway, not work completed. Until those systems exist, the Eighth Amendment is a door frame without a door: legally open, practically unusable. And the 1,000-share minimum cuts both ways. At the Rs 100 face value these shares must carry (no premium is permitted), the minimum ticket is Rs 100,000 (computed: 1,000 × Rs 100) — a sensible floor for keeping transfer costs proportionate, but a high wall for the migrant worker whose proven appetite is a 10-share IPO application. The Fund is designed for the settled diaspora with capital to deploy, not for the worker sending monthly wages home.

04. The channel still closed: the secondary market

The secondary market — buying and selling shares already listed on NEPSE — remains closed to NRNs, and the timeline for opening it has now slipped past its own first deadline. The Ministry of Finance's 21-point Capital Market Strengthening and Revival Action Plan, 2083, announced on 15 September 2026 (ShareSansar), directed that proposed amendments to the Foreign Investment and Technology Transfer Act, 2075 and the Foreign Exchange (Regulation) Act, 2019 be submitted to Cabinet by the end of Asoj 2083 — mid-October 2026. That date has passed. No public record of the submission was found in the sources checked at publish time, and this article does not claim the amendments were or were not submitted behind closed doors; it records only that no public record was found, which is itself the point. A reform whose progress cannot be verified from public sources cannot yet be relied on by an investor abroad.

What is publicly moving is a parallel track. The Ministry of Industry, Commerce and Supplies has published a draft Foreign Investment and Technology Transfer (First Amendment) Bill, 2083, for stakeholder consultation under Section 6 of the Legislation Act, 2081, with a 7-day feedback window; the draft's special provisions for NRNs would let the government permit NRN investment in previously restricted sectors through a simple notification in the Nepal Gazette, and the bill would then go to the Council of Ministers before being tabled in Parliament. That is real movement — a published draft with a consultation mechanism beats a plan's deadline — but it is still three steps from law: consultation, Cabinet, Parliament. The budget for 2026/27, presented by Finance Minister Dr. Swarnim Wagle (Farsight Nepal), points the same way, promising amendments on foreign-investment approval, investment accounting, profit repatriation and capital-gains taxation to enable NRN secondary-market participation, and a shift from prior central-bank approval to a notification system for repatriating investment. Promises, drafts and frameworks now outnumber completed laws by a wide margin.

The fourth row deserves a sentence, because it is often forgotten in capital-market debates. The NRN Act already permits a non-resident Nepali to invest, in convertible foreign currency earned abroad, in industries open to foreign investment, routed through a licensed bank with the source of funds disclosed. That is direct business investment, not market investment — buying into a company, not buying its listed shares — and its friction is procedural rather than legal. It has no published, standardised, from-abroad route comparable to the IPO quota, which is why it does not scale either.

05. Five conditions that decide whether diaspora money becomes investment

The pattern across all four channels is consistent. Every channel that works is small, sector-bound or procedural; every channel that could scale is still a framework, a draft or a deadline. The conversion problem therefore has a checklist, and it is worth stating plainly, because each item is a testable claim about the next announcement, not a sentiment.

First, the law must be passed, not promised. The 21-point plan's mid-October Cabinet target is the latest in a line of dated commitments documented in the 8 October piece, from budget speeches to a SEBON study committee. The FITTA draft bill's consultation is the most concrete step yet, but consultation is not Cabinet approval, and Cabinet approval is not Parliamentary passage. Until an amendment is gazetted, every "NRNs can now invest" headline should be read as "NRNs may soon be able to invest, if the next two steps happen."

Second, accounts must be openable from abroad, end to end. The Fund's own chief executive describes the account and investment systems as work underway across four institutions. For an investor in Doha, Kuala Lumpur or London, that is the whole ballgame: identity verification, a foreign-currency account, a demat account and a trading account must link without a flight to Kathmandu. The IPO quota already proves the demand side of this plumbing works through MeroShare; the NRN channels need the same from-abroad completeness, published as a procedure, not described as an intention.

Third, repatriation must be written down in advance. An investor who cannot see the exit will not enter. The budget's shift from prior approval to notification for repatriating investment is the right direction, and the Eighth Amendment framework envisages returns taken abroad in foreign currency — but both remain contingent on the systems and rules noted above. The test for any future announcement is a single sentence: "sale proceeds may be repatriated in foreign currency after tax, by notification, within X days." Until that sentence exists in a gazetted rule, repatriation is a hope, not a right.

Fourth, ticket sizes must fit the savers, not just the wealthy. The market already knows two ticket sizes: 10 shares for the IPO quota (Rs 1,000 at face value) and 1,000 shares for NRN Fund shares (Rs 100,000 at face value, computed). The gap between them is the gap between the migrant worker and the settled professional — and between a proven demand of 79,232 applicants and an unproven one. A diaspora strategy that only serves the second group leaves the first group's money exactly where it is: in household transfers.

Fifth, the products must go beyond shares. The 21-point plan itself looks beyond equities, toward corporate bonds, money markets and project-specific bonds; the Eighth Amendment framework lets its companies invest in shares, bonds and debentures in infrastructure projects with Nepal Rastra Bank approval. That breadth matters because much of the diaspora's appetite is for income and safety, not equity volatility — and because a market that offers only shares to newcomers is asking them to take the most volatile product first. Bonds and infrastructure instruments are the on-ramp; the secondary equity market is the destination, not the entrance.

What to take from it

Nepal's diaspora does not need to be persuaded, cajoled or celebrated into sending money home. It already sends Rs 2,363.13 billion a year. What it needs is a complete road: a passed law, an account that opens from abroad, a written exit, a ticket size it can afford, and a product it actually wants. Today exactly one road is complete — the foreign-employment IPO quota — and it carries 10 shares at a time into a single sector. The NRN Fund's framework is the most promising road under construction, aimed at a 2027 IPO; the secondary market is still a draft bill and a missed deadline. Watch the next three verifiable events, in order: the FITTA amendment bill reaching Parliament, the NRN Fund's account and trading systems being published as procedures rather than described as underway, and the Fund's IPO prospectus itself. When those three land, the conversion problem starts to close. Until then, the honest headline is the one this article opened with: the money came home; the investment did not — yet.
