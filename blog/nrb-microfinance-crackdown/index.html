Nepal's microfinance lenders made record profits. Their bad loans grew even faster.

NRB just put four lenders under prompt corrective action for holding less capital than the law demands, and fined two more for cash-reserve breaches. The boom and the crackdown are the same story.

By Shirjan Khadka · Founder, Nepse Decode, Kathmandu · September 29, 2026 · 5 min read
Facebook X LinkedIn

Six of Nepal's microfinance lenders are in trouble with the regulator. The Nepal Rastra Bank's microfinance supervision department has penalized six Class D microfinance institutions based on their fourth-quarter reports for fiscal year 2082/83, and the details are unusually specific. Four of them, Forward, Dhaulagiri, Matri Bhumi, and NIC Microfinance, were placed under prompt corrective action for holding less capital than the law requires. Two more, Win Nepal and Sanjeevani, were fined for failing to maintain the mandatory cash reserve.

Start with the capital numbers, because they are the heart of the story. Capital adequacy is the cushion between a lender's own money and the loans it has made. Nepal's rules set the floor at 8 percent: for every 100 rupees of risk-weighted assets, a microfinance institution must hold at least 8 rupees of its own capital. All four lenders now under corrective action fell short. Forward Microfinance of Sunsari reported 6.12 percent, Dhaulagiri of Baglung 6.38 percent, NIC of Banepa 6.39 percent, and Matri Bhumi of Budhanilkantha just 4.08 percent, barely half the legal minimum. NRB acted under the Bylaws Relating to Prompt Corrective Action of Banks and Financial Institutions, 2017, with Matri Bhumi reportedly subjected to a different provision of the bylaw given the depth of its shortfall.

Prompt corrective action is worth understanding precisely, because it is neither a fine nor a shutdown. It is a set of restrictions the regulator imposes to stop a weak institution from digging deeper: curbs on fresh lending, on branch expansion, on dividend payouts, until the capital position is repaired. The logic is preventive. A lender with a thin cushion that keeps growing its book is transferring risk to its depositors and to the system, and the regulator's job is to interrupt that transfer before the cushion is gone.

The other two penalties were for a different, simpler rule. The cash reserve ratio requires lenders to park a slice of their funds as cash with the central bank, a basic liquidity discipline. Win Nepal of Banepa was fined Rs 58.38 for missing the requirement in Chaitra 2082, its first recorded violation. Sanjeevani of Tulsipur, Dang, was fined for four consecutive months: Rs 713.88 for Chaitra, Rs 1,067.40 for Baisakh, Rs 1,412.91 for Jestha, and Rs 1,410.78 for Ashadh, the penalties rising across the first three months and holding near the peak in the fourth. The published fine amounts are small. The pattern is the point. Four straight months of missing a basic liquidity rule is a management problem, not an arithmetic one.

Here is the tension that makes this more than a compliance story. The same central bank data that triggered these penalties shows the sector booming. Net profit across 48 retail microfinance institutions jumped 48.14 percent to Rs 10.78 billion in FY 2082/83, up from Rs 7.28 billion a year earlier, with 46 of the 48 lenders reporting a profit. In the same year, their bad loans exploded 63.83 percent to Rs 50.82 billion, while total credit grew only 10.09 percent. The sector's non-performing loan ratio climbed from 7 percent to 10.42 percent. For every 100 rupees lent, more than 10 are now classified as bad debt. Profits up 48 percent, bad loans up 64 percent. Both numbers come from the same reports, as reported from NRB's off-site supervision data.

Both can be true at once, and the mechanism is worth spelling out. Interest income is recognized while loans are still performing, but the cost of bad loans arrives through provisioning, the money lenders set aside against loans that may never be repaid. That cost is being recognized: provisioning rose 84 percent to Rs 29.43 billion. But reported profit still jumped, which means income growth outran even that surge in provisioning, at least this year. The contrast with the sector's three wholesale lenders is instructive. Their profit shrank 23 percent to Rs 980 million, even though their bad-loan ratio stayed controlled at 3.04 percent. Retail lenders chased growth and booked the income; wholesale lenders absorbed the cost. The enforcement actions suggest the regulator is not confident the retail profit number can survive the bad-loan trend for another year.

The enforcement list also points to a discipline problem wider than six names. Unaudited reports show eight institutions below the 8 percent capital floor, and NRB now has ten microfinance lenders under prompt corrective action in total, four of them added in this round. Sanjeevani, one of the two cash-reserve offenders, also breached the rule capping collateral-based lending at 33.33 percent of a retail lender's portfolio, reaching 38.34 percent, and failed entirely to submit data to the central bank's supervisory information system. Nine other institutions submitted incomplete data. A regulator cannot supervise what it cannot see, and the data gaps are themselves a form of non-compliance.

The stakes are not abstract. Microfinance membership grew nearly 5 percent to 6.53 million people, while the branch network shrank 0.75 percent to 4,924 outlets, concentrated in Lumbini Province at 22.03 percent of branches and thinnest in Karnali at 4.79 percent. The sector holds Rs 655 billion in assets and has mobilized over Rs 437 billion in financial resources. Its borrowers are the small traders, vegetable vendors, and shopkeepers of bazaars like the one pictured above, people with no other formal lender to turn to. They are also, in many cases, the savers whose deposits sit inside these same institutions. When a microfinance lender's capital cushion thins, the risk does not stay on a spreadsheet. It sits with them.

Some caution is owed before drawing straight lines. Several of these figures come from unaudited fourth-quarter reports, so revisions are possible. Prompt corrective action restricts and disciplines; it does not seize or close an institution. And a rising bad-loan ratio in a slowing book can partly reflect old loans aging into default while new lending cools, which is a timing effect as well as a quality problem. None of that erases the direction of travel: bad loans growing six times faster than credit, and a regulator that has stopped waiting for the numbers to improve on their own.

Put the two halves together and the picture is uncomfortable. The sector just reported its most profitable year, and the regulator just punished its weakest members in the same breath. One of those numbers is a record. The other is a warning. Which one describes next year is the question neither the profit figures nor the penalties can answer yet.
